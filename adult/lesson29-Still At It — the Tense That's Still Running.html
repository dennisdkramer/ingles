Still At It — the Tense That's Still Running

By the end of this page you can say what you've been doing: the thing that started earlier and hasn't stopped. It's the tense of the apology, the excuse, and the flex.

1 · Eleven calls

Two in the afternoon to eight at night

Marcos has been counting boxes since 2. The phone's face down on a pallet, buzzing every twenty minutes, and he keeps telling himself he'll pick it up after this row.

He hasn't picked up. His sister's called eleven times. The last voicemail is four seconds long: "Bro. I know you're at it. Pick up."

What to listen for. Three things are still running at eight o'clock: the counting, the ignoring, and the promise he keeps making himself.

The part Portuguese never says out loud is the been.

Marcos has one word for all of it — at it: busy with the same thing, still not done. By eight, it's the only thing he can say.

2 · Three parts, one shape

The same sentence, cut in three

He's been calling since 2
PartWordWhat it's doing
1I'vethe helper you learned in 26 — the time is still open
2beenthe activity started and nobody stopped it
3callingthe activity itself — never how many calls
Swap I've for he's, she's, we've, they've. Been and the -ing word never change.

Why not just "I call"?

Because the activity isn't finished. If it started before, and it's still running now, the been is what says so.

Duration is the cost: in Portuguese the present tense carries it. English makes you say the been out loud.

Drop the been and the listener hears a habit instead of this afternoon: I call him every day is a fact about your life, not about today.

Count or stretch

I've called you three times.

Three finished calls. The number is the point.

Perfect simple — you count the result.

Sounds like an accusation with evidence.

I've been calling you all day.

One long stretch of trying. The time is the point.

Perfect continuous — you feel the activity.

Sounds like the effort tired you out.

Fill the whole shape

Bracket tells you the verb. You write all three parts — helper, been, -ing.

I (try) to call you all day.

She (work) nights since March, bro.

We (wait) for ages — where you at?

He (put) that shi- off for weeks.

They (grind) all week, so nah, they ain't coming out tonight.

3 · Six hours, one sentence

Same shift, four checkpoints

  1. 2:00

    He starts counting. I've been counting since 2.

  2. 4:00

    First call. He doesn't pick up.

  3. 6:12

    Eleven missed calls, and he telling himself the row is nearly done for four hours straight.

  4. 8:00

    I've been at it since morning, no shi-.

for, since

since + the clock: since 2, since March, since he was a kid.

for + the length: for six hours, for ages, for weeks.

You had both in 26. What's new is that the length keeps growing while you talk — that's why the clock word isn't optional here.

4 · Estou … há

The line you want to say in Portuguese

Estou tentando te ligar há uma hora.

presente + há + tempo

One activity, one hour, still going.

Portuguese keeps the verb in the present.

I've been trying to call you for an hour.

perfect continuous + for + length

Same meaning, different machine.

English won't take I'm trying … for an hour.

Two traps, one fix

Estou tentando on its own is I'm trying — right now, this second. The moment a length joins it (há uma hora), the English verb has to change to have been.

Tenho tentado is not the same thing: it's I've tried — attempts, counted. If you want the stretch, you need been.

One fix. Find the length first — há dois anos, faz três meses — then tell the verb to wear have been + -ing. The length never picks the tense; it only says the shape is needed.

Portuguese gets to this meaning through three different doors, and all three land on one English sentence:

1 · Estou trabalhando aqui há dois anos. 2 · Trabalho aqui há dois anos. 3 · Faz dois anos que trabalho aqui.

All three: I've been working here for two years. Word for word, doors 1 and 2 hand you I'm working here for two years and I work here for two years — perfect Portuguese, wrong English.

Door 3 has no English counterpart: faz ("it makes") is no clock word here — drop it and say the length with for.

How long have you been…? is the question that asks for this whole shape. Answer it with the shape, never with a bare since: Since 2. / For six hours.

Pick the English

Marcos, 6:12, on the phone.

1. Estou trabalhando aqui há dois anos.

2. Estou esperando ele há meia hora.

3. Ela está estudando inglês há três meses.

5 · Been trying, meaning to, putting off

Three excuses, one shape

I've been trying — the effort is real and it hasn't worked yet. "I've been trying to get through to the office all morning."

I've been meaning to — it's on your list and you keep not doing it. "I've been meaning to sort that out since last week."

I've been putting it off — worse than meaning to: you're avoiding it on purpose. "I've been meaning to sort it out, but I've been putting it off for f---in' ages."

Same three words every time. What changes is how much of a liar you sound like.

The shape has a second half: the denial. Nothing about it changes except that not rides on the helper — I haven't been ignoring you, she hasn't been sleeping. The been and the -ing stay exactly where they were.

And the question that drags an excuse out of somebody is the same shape with the helper in front: Have you been sleeping at all? · What've you been up to all week?

Written out full — I have not been, What have you been — but nobody says it that way out loud.

The words that carry it

  • all day · all weekthe stretch, unbroken
  • for agesa long time, no number
  • these daysthis period of your life
  • at itbusy with it, still going
  • grindwork with no glamour
  • hustleearn something on the side

Say it with the right effort

The bracket tells you what he means, not the words. Write the whole shape from memory.

1. I to reach the office all morning. (effort, no luck)

2. She call you back. (it's on her list)

3. He for weeks. (avoiding it on purpose)

4. I since 2, bro. (busy, still going)

5. They all week. (hard work, no glamour)

6. These days I on the side. (extra money)

6 · The apology and the flex

She's been hearing this all day

  1. DudaYou had eleven calls, Marcos.
  2. MarcosI know. I've been at it since 2, I swear.
  3. DudaYou've been saying that since June.
  4. MarcosNah, for real — I've been trying to close this row out before the truck pulls off.
  5. DudaAnd you've been putting the paperwork off again.
  6. MarcosThat one's fair.

Read it again: half those six lines are the same tense wearing three different faces, and only one of them is actually an apology.

Same shape, three jobs

The apology owns the mistake and hands over the effort.

The excuse shows the effort and hides the result.

The flex makes the effort the point, so nobody asks about the result.

The clock word is the receipt. An apology with since June or for ages sounds like evidence; the same apology without one sounds like a mood.

The excuse works once. Say it twice and it turns into evidence — that's what Duda hands back: "You've been saying that since June."

The flex invites a question. Make the tiredness the point and the next thing anybody asks is "yeah — but what got done?"

Which one is the apology?

The one that names the mistake and the effort together.

Which one is the flex?

Tiredness stated as proof. Nobody asks how much got done.
