I've Had It — the Tense That Reaches Now

A friend you haven't seen in three years texts you out of nowhere. Every word in her message is yours. The verb shape is not — not yet.

A. Three years, one thread

Read it and keep going. You are not expected to build these lines yet — sections 1 to 5 hand you one piece each, and section 6 makes you answer a thread like this one.

Marina: Look who's f---in' alive. How long has it been — three years?

You: Three years. Same job, same apartment. I ain't going anywhere.

Marina: For real? Have you ever thought about coming back?

You: Never. Not f---in' once.

Marina: And your brother? Has he finished school yet?

You: He's already found a job. Graduated and got hired, the lucky bastard.

Marina: So far, so good, huh?

You: So far. Ask me again in five years, bro.

Eight lines, and the whole lesson is already in there: a length of time, a question about your whole life, a thing that happened a minute ago, and a thing that has not happened yet. You could not have built one of those answers this morning. Tonight you build all four.

1. One helper, one participle

A. The verb splits in two

English does not build this with one verb. It puts a helper in front and a third form of the verb behind.

You already own two forms of every verb, and you have been saying 'em since lesson 13: work → worked and eat → ate. This lesson lives on the third one — the participle: worked, eaten, gone, seen. For most verbs it looks exactly like the past: worked, tried, called. The verbs that surprise you are the same ones that already surprise you in the past: eat / ate / eaten, see / saw / seen, take / took / taken, get / got / gotten.

The helper is have or has, and it carries one meaning only: the window is still open — this runs up to now. The participle behind it never changes for the person.

PersonHelperParticipleWhat it says at this moment
Ihaveworked thereI still work there.
ShehaseatenShe is not hungry now.
Wehavenever metUp to now, no meeting.
Theyhavealready leftThey are gone now.
Ihavehad itI'm f---in' done with this shi-.
Only the helper moves for the person: I have, she has, he has, it has. The participle is one form for everybody — I have seen, she has seen, they have seen. She have and I has are the two mistakes to kill tonight.

That is the whole difference from the past simple you have used since lesson 13. I worked there — the verb carries everything and the door is shut. I've worked there — the helper carries now and the door is open. Same verb, same five years, different job status — and only one of 'em is telling the truth about today.

In speech the helper collapses into the person: I've, you've, we've, they've, he's, she's, it's. Listen to the difference between the two forms — the participle is what tells you which one you heard:

He's finished is he is finished and he has finished at the same time — the same little 's doing two jobs. The rest of the sentence tells you which one, and nobody stops to think about it.

One idiom wears this shape with nothing added: I've had it — have + had. It means I'm done, I'm fed up. I've f---in' had it, bro. I've had it up to here with this bullshi-. I've had enough of this shi-. Same machinery, and the most useful sentence in this lesson.

2. How long? for, since, and the sentence Portuguese builds wrong

A. English will not put a present verb next to five years

This is the correction a Brazilian makes every f---in' day. Read the three lines in order.

Trabalho aqui há cinco anos.

Word for word, that is I work here for five years — and that is what comes out of your mouth, and it is wrong, and every native hears it immediately. English hears a present-tense verb and a length of time and asks the obvious question: then why are you still counting?

I've worked here for five years. Helper, participle, length. The job is still yours.

Now the pair that proves the helper is doing the work: I worked there for five years — I don't work there now. Same for five years, one helper apart, opposite meaning. That is this whole lesson in two lines.

And the line this whole page is built around: I've f---in' had it — five years in this job, since day one. Helper, participle, length, starting point, and a man who is done. Three of this lesson's items in one sentence, and not one of them is the present tense.

for takes a length. since takes a starting point — a date, a year, a day, or the moment something happened. Both answer the same question, and the question is How long have you…?, never How long do you…?

Since has a second job in English: it also means because. Since you're here, grab a beer. Same word, different sentence, and context decides — the same way já que works in Portuguese.

3. Ever and never the question you ask a friend

A. Two words, two jobs

One of them asks. The other one answers. Swap them and the line stops being English.

Ever goes in the question. It asks: at any point up to now — no year, no date, just have you touched this experience at all? Have you ever f---ed up that bad? Have you ever been fired? Have you ever eaten a real taco? Same question every time, and the answer is always a piece of your life.

Never answers it. Never, not yet. — that is one line out of a real mouth: not once so far, and I am not promising anything about tomorrow. Never already means not at any point, so the helper stays positive: I've never been fired ✓. I haven't never been fired ✗ — two negatives in one line, and English keeps never and drops the other one.

Ever also turns up after a superlative, and there it means the same thing: the best job I've ever had, the worst hangover I've ever had. Same word, same job — it sweeps the whole life up to now and picks the extreme.

Been is the participle of be, and in this shape it also does went and came back: I've been to New York — I was there and I am home now. I've gone to New York means I am still there. Lesson 27 takes that pair apart; today been is the one you need, because it is the one you answer with.

The drinking game hands you one fixed line: Never have I ever… — inverted, game only, and it stays inside that game. Do not invert anywhere else; Never I have is not a sentence in any room.

B. The three short answers

A question in this shape has three short answers, and they do not mean the same thing. Pick the wrong one and you have said something you did not mean.

The questionA short answerWhat it tells you
Have you ever been fired?Yeah, I have.At some point — yes.
Have you ever eaten a real taco?No, never.At no point up to now.
Have you ever been to LA?Not yet.No — and it can still happen.
Not yet keeps the door open. No, never closes it. A friend hears the difference immediately, and so does a boss — one of those two answers sounds like a plan, and the other sounds like a verdict. And No shi-? on the other side of the table is not doubt: it means that is a good answer, tell me more.

4. Already, just, yet timing measured against this moment

A. Where each one sits

All three say how the event sits against now, and none of them sits wherever it likes — the slot is part of the meaning.

WordWhere it sitsWhat it says
alreadybetween the helper and the participleit happened sooner than you expected
justbetween the helper and the participleit happened a moment ago
yetthe end of the line, in a question or a negativeup to now — and it can still happen
Put already at the end and it still works, but it carries an edge — I've eaten already sounds like you are annoyed at being asked. The neutral slot is between the helper and the participle. Put yet in the middle and the line breaks completely: it lives at the end, or it does not live.

Here is the part nobody writes in a book. In American speech these three words do not need the perfect at all: I already ate. I just got home. Did you eat yet? Every one of those is normal, and it is what a friend actually says. The perfect is the safe shape; the past simple is the fast one. Both are American, and neither is a mistake — so do not correct a native who uses the fast one.

Someone offers you food the second you walk in: I've already eaten — I just got home, no shi-. Perfect for the eating, past simple for getting home, and the swear is doing what a swear does — it tells you this is a friend, not a form.

Just is the clearest case. I just got home is the standard American line. I've just got home sounds like a British film, and I've just f---in' walked in keeps just in the slot where the perfect wants it. Both work; the register decides.

Yet answers a question that is still open. Has he called yet? He hasn't called yet. Not yet. — a complete answer, and it always leaves room for the call. Nah, not yet, and I'm starting to get pissed. — same answer, plus what the waiting is doing to you.

And keep still out of this family. It looks like a cousin, but still says the thing continues, and it takes the present simple: I still work there — not I've still worked there. Duration is for and since; continuation is still.

5. Finished time or open time the one question that picks the tense

A. When, exactly?

Ask the sentence one question. If it already answers when, the door is shut and the verb goes to the past simple. If the time is still running, the helper stays.

The left column is a date. The right column is a window with no date on it. I saw him yesterday ✓ — I've seen him yesterday ✗. The date is already in the sentence, so there is nothing left for the helper to connect to now — that shi- is closed.

Today and this week are the live ones, and they are where the two systems touch. While the period is still running, the helper is the safe choice: I've seen him twice today. American speech also says I saw him twice today — same day, clock still going. Both are heard; the helper is never wrong while the period is open.

So far does the helper's job out loud, and up to now says the same thing with the volume up: So far, so good. So far this week, I've worked sixty hours. So far, up to now, I've had enough of this shi-. If you can hear so far, you already know which shape is coming, for real.

Lately is the last of the ten, and the loosest: a recent stretch with no dates on it. I've been tired lately. I've been drinking too much lately. She's been weird lately, and nobody knows why.

And the other half of the pair, the one that catches everybody: I lost my keys is a report about a closed moment. I've lost my keys means they are still lost, right now, in your hand, missing. Lesson 27 lives on that difference. Today, use it as a test: if the result is still staring at you, the helper stays.

6. Your turn the same thread, with you in it

A. Answer the message

Marina sent these three before section 1. Answer them now, one line each, in the perfect. Write it the way you text her — contractions, slang, a well-placed f---in' if that is how you talk. There is no single right answer: write true ones, because a teacher reads this and a lie reads like a lie.

Marina: How long have you been at that shi- job?

Marina: Have you ever thought about coming back? For real.

Marina: And have you fixed your damn visa yet?

Every answer needs one helper and one participle. The first wants for or since; the second wants ever or never; the third wants already, just or yet.

C. Three questions, in this order

When a line feels wrong and you cannot say why, run these three. They are the whole lesson, compressed.

  1. Does the sentence say when? Yesterday, last night, in 2019, two hours ago — then the verb is past simple, full stop.
  2. Is the thing still true, still going, or still staring at you? Then the helper comes in: have or has, plus the participle, and the window stays open.
  3. Is there a length or a starting point? A length takes for; a date, a year or a moment takes since. Never a present-tense verb beside either one.

That is it. One helper, one participle, one question about when. You walked in with trabalho aqui há cinco anos; you walk out with I've worked here for five years — and with the line for the friend who asks how the job is going: I've f---in' had it, five years in this job, since day one. Or, when it happens to be fine: so far, so good.
