She Said She Was Coming — Gossip Grammar

Everything you repeat about someone else is one tense further back than they said it.

One step back

Why the verb moves

The words belong to that moment; the report belongs to now

Everything in this lesson is one idea: when you repeat what someone said, the verb steps back one tense. Not because of a rule you have to memorize, but because the speaking moment is over. She said it on Tuesday; you are repeating it on Friday; the verb sits where the words actually live.

"I'm coming." → She said she was coming. "I'll call you." → He said he'd call me. "I can't." → She said she couldn't. "I don't care." → He said he didn't care.

O português também recua ("ela disse que vinha"), mas com menos regularidade. O inglês recua sempre, e a frase que mais delata um brasileiro é "she said me" — em inglês tell leva a pessoa, say não.

In the street, the that disappears and the tense still moves: "He said he was coming — he f---in' told me that, I swear." Nobody says "he said that he was coming" in a group chat unless they are being dramatic.

The ladder, rung by rung

Say it, then report it
Direct Reported What moved
"I'm tired." She said she was tired. am → was
"I work here." She said she worked here. present → past
"I'll be late." She said she'd be late. will → would
"I can't come." She said she couldn't come. can → could
"I saw him." She said she'd seen him. past → past perfect
Two rungs do not move: would, could and should are already back, and a past perfect stays where it is.

Say it to whom

Say or tell, never "say me"

Tell carries the person; say carries the words

tell + person: "She told me she was over it." say + (that) + words: "She said she was over it." If you want the person with say, it needs to: "She said it to me."

The error a Brazilian makes ten times a day is she said me, because ela me disse puts the person straight after the verb. In English the person goes with tell and nowhere else: she told me or she said to me, never "she said me".

Fixed shapes worth owning: tell the truth, tell a lie, tell a story, tell the difference — no person required, because the object is already there. And say something, say a word, say it again.

Then, there, that day

The words that move with the verb

A tense that steps back drags the time words with it

Only when the moment has passed — if it is still today, you may keep the word

If it is still the same day, native speakers keep today and tomorrow. The shift is for when the moment is gone, not a math exercise.

Questions come back as statements

No do, no inversion, and if/whether for yes-or-no

Report a question and it stops being a question. The word order goes back to statement order and the helper disappears: "Where were you?" → He asked where I was. Not "he asked where was I", and not "he asked where did I was".

For a yes-or-no question, English needs a bridge word: if or whether. "Are you coming?" → She asked if I was coming. This if is not a conditional; it is the yes-or-no marker, and dropping it turns the sentence into nonsense.

Three examples from a real night out: "He asked where the f--- I was, so I told him." "She asked if we were still going." "They wanted to know how much I'd paid." The last is the polite version of a nosy question, which is why people use it.

Seven verbs that do the reporting

Each one carries an attitude the plain "said" does not

  • claim — say it, with doubt attached: "She claimed she didn't do it, then she admitted the whole shi-."
  • admit — say it against yourself: "He admitted he'd lost the keys."
  • deny — say it is not true: "He denied everything, then his story fell apart."
  • mention — say it in passing: "She mentioned she was moving out."
  • point out — draw attention to a fact: "He pointed out that I was the one who booked it."
  • touch on — mention briefly, usually in a meeting: "We touched on the budget, nothing more."
  • wonder — think a question without asking: "I wonder if she's still mad."

The gossip chain

Pass it on, one step back

Bia is reporting everything Ana said. Every gap is either the backshift, the right reporting verb, or a question turned into a statement.

  1. Bia You know what Ana said? She said she moving out.
  2. Caio She told the same thing last week.
  3. Bia And she claimed she told her mom yet.
  4. Caio I wonder she's serious this time.
  5. Bia She asked me where her keys , and then she just left.
  6. Caio She also mentioned she paid the deposit already.
  7. Bia She said she over it — bullshi-, she's still mad.

Three things to notice as you fill: gap 5 keeps statement order after where; gap 4 needs the yes-or-no bridge; and gap 7 is the line everyone says about an ex.

Which one is English?

One option is what a native says. Pick it.

1You are reporting that she spoke to you before leaving.
2You are reporting his question about your location.
3You are reporting a yes-or-no question about the plan.

Three things somebody said
