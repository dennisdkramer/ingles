I Was Standing There When He Went Off

Last Friday, 9:41 at night, you were minding your own business. Two minutes later everybody's filming and somebody's calling the cops. This page is the tense that tells it back in the right order — and section 5 gives you the seven verbs that night needs.

A. Rewind two minutes

Four lines out of one night, each with the time it happened. Read them once and find the two that were already going before anything went off — the shape behind that arrives in section 1.

  • 9:41 — I was on the phone with my sister, walking to the car.
  • 9:42 — Some dude came out of nowhere and shouldered me into the door.
  • 9:42 — I was still holding the phone when I swung at him.
  • 9:43 — Everybody was filming by the time security showed up.

Four lines, one night, and two of 'em didn't start at all — they were already running when the next thing hit. That's the whole tense tonight: English keeps one shape for what was going on, and a different shape for what came in on top of it. Mix the two up and your story comes out in the wrong order, because he shouldered me while I was walking and I walked because he shouldered me are two different nights to anybody who wasn't there.

Nothing here is new machinery. You already own was and were, and you already own -ing from the right-now tense. Tonight you put the two of them together and get a past you can stand inside. What that's worth: the difference between what happened and what was going on when it happened — which is the difference between a report and a story somebody listens to at a table.

1. What was already running one line going, one coming in on top

A. Same two events, two orders, two nights

All four lines below are correct English about the same two minutes. Move the was + -ing onto the other verb and the night changes — who caused what changes with it.

The form is the cheapest thing on this page: was or were plus the verb with -ing. I was walking. We were eating. He was talking. No helper, no participle, no third column. You already own both pieces — was from last month's past, -ing from the right-now tense — and tonight you just stop separating them.

What costs you is the choosing, and it's cheaper than it looks once you hear the two versions a native hears:

  • I was walking to the car when he shouldered me. — the walking was going, and he came in on top of it. You didn't do anything to him.
  • I walked to the car when he shouldered me. — same words, different night: now it sounds like you walked because he shouldered you. You became the reason.
  • She was crying when I walked in. — the crying was already going. You walked into it.
  • She cried when I walked in. — it started then, and a listener will assume it started because of you.

Nothing in the second line of each pair is broken English. It's a different claim about the order, and the other person builds a different night out of it. That's the rule, in one line: the -ing goes on the action that was already running, and the plain past goes on the thing that came in.

Two consequences, and both are worth remembering. First, the thing that comes in is usually short and finished — a shove, a ring, a shout — so it takes the plain past and never the -ing. Second, if you put -ing on both halves, you've said that nothing interrupted anything: two things were going at the same time, and the listener waits for a third sentence that never comes.

And there's a third use sitting in the same shape, because it's the sentence you'll reach for most: was about to plus a verb. I was about to leave when he came up hot. The leaving was already decided and already starting, and he stopped it — so when somebody asks why you were still standing there at 9:43, the answer is one -ing and a plan you never finished. We were about to pay when the dude started. No new word in it, and it does something the plain past can't do: it tells the listener you were on your way out.

B. Which one was going?

Six lines out of real nights. The verb is in brackets and you decide the form. Four of these say what was going on and two say what came in — and the time in each line tells you which.

At 9:41 I to the car, still on the phone with my sister. (walk)
At 9:42 some dude me into the door, out of nowhere. (shoulder)
She when I walked in — nobody had said a word yet. (cry)
She the second she read the text, and then she threw the phone. (cry)
We when the lights went out, so nobody saw a thing. (eat)
I leave when he came up hot — and that's why I was still standing there. (be about to)

Numbers 3 and 4 are the same verb, the same room, three seconds apart — and if your two answers match, you read the bracket instead of the sentence. Number 4 is the one nobody gets wrong twice: when the crying starts, there's no -ing on it.

2. while, when, as the joiner already tells you what comes after it

A. Three words, four shapes

Each joiner expects a particular shape after it, and reading the joiner is faster than deciding from scratch. One row below is the case where the rule flips, and it's the row people get wrong.

JoinerWhat follows itIn the wildWhat it claims
whilethe long one, with -ingWhile I was on the phone, he walked up.the whole stretch, and something else inside it
whenthe short one, plain pastWhen he shouldered me, I dropped the phone.one moment, and what it set off
aseither, and often both runningAs I turned around, he was already swinging.the two lining up in the same second
when + -ingthe long oneWhen I was walking home, I saw the whole thing.a during, not a moment — when can hold a stretch
The trap is while with the one-second act: while he shouldered me lands wrong, because while asks for a stretch and a shove isn't one. The mirror trap is when on both halves: when I was walking, he was swinging is grammatical and says almost nothing — two things going on, nobody interrupting anybody.

Your Portuguese pairs with this almost one to one, which is why the section is short: enquanto eu estava no telefone is while I was on the phone, and quando ele veio pra cima de mim is when he came at me. The only thing your ear has to relearn is the refusal: English will not put while in front of a single finished act.

And the last row is the useful one, so don't skip it. When plus -ing means during — When I was walking home is not one second, it's the whole walk. That's how you open a story with a stretch in it and still use the word your ear reaches for first.

B. Join them

Six pairs, one joiner per gap, and the bank has exactly the three words you need. Each line tells you whether it's joining a stretch to a moment or two things that were both going.

while when as

I was on the phone with my sister, some dude walked up to the car.
he shouldered me, my phone hit the ground.
I turned around, he was already swinging.
everybody was filming, security was still inside eating.
the cops got there, the dude was long gone.
I was explaining it, he kept cutting me off.

Number 4 has two -ing halves and no interruption at all — that's the shape that says both were going, and it's the reason while takes it. Number 3 works with when too and nothing breaks. What breaks is while on a one-second act, which is the mistake this section is here to fix.

3. Scene or report and the one thing Portuguese keeps getting in the way of

A. What maps free, and what doesn't

Two of these three mappings cost you nothing. The third is the one that keeps a Brazilian sounding Brazilian long after the rest is fixed, so read it twice.

Estava fazendo is was doing, one to one: eu estava dirigindo → I was driving. Free. Estava plus an adjective is free too — eu estava cansado → I was tired — and it is never I was being tired. Being only goes on behaviour you can switch on: you're being an asshole is about what somebody is doing right now, and you're an asshole is about who they are. Your Portuguese separates those two with estar and ser; English separates them with or without being.

The expensive one is the imperfect for what used to happen. Quando eu era moleque, eu jogava bola todo dia. Your ear wants was playing, because the Portuguese is an imperfect and it looks continuous. English won't take it: a habit is played, or used to play, and if you say I was playing ball every day you have described one particular week, not your childhood. This is the correction to fix tonight — -ing is never a habit. It's one scene, and scenes end.

The whole difference in one line: was doing is a scene with a length to it; did is one finished act or a habit. And the test that never fails — put every day at the end of it and see what happens to your -ing. If it survives, the sentence is wrong.

B. Find the one that's wrong

Five lines, and four of them have something a native would catch. One is already right — say so, because a page that teaches you to doubt everything just makes you slower when you talk.

1."I was knowing her from school."
2."She was being tired after the shift."
3."When I was a kid I was playing ball every day."
4."While he shouldered me, I dropped my phone."
5."When I was walking home, I saw the whole thing."

Number 5 is your reward for reading: when plus a stretch is legal, and it means during the walk. Number 3 is the expensive one from above — the every day is the clue, and it kills the -ing.

4. She kept doing it the tense that means somebody ran out of patience

A. Repetition with a complaint attached

Three ways to say it happened again and again, and only one of them tells the listener you were done being reasonable about it.

Kept plus -ing is the shape tonight. She kept doing it — same act, repeated, and kept is you watching it happen without stopping it. It's a complaint before it's a fact: He kept calling, so I blocked him. I kept telling him, and he kept not listening. Two kepts in one line is normal, and it's how somebody describes a week that wouldn't end.

Kept on is the same thing with more heat: She kept on going, right in front of me. Put right in there and it's the version you say when you're already done with the conversation. Wouldn't stop is the loudest of the three, and it wants a person doing it: He wouldn't stop talking, so I left. All three are followed by -ing and none of them by a plain past — he kept call is nobody's English.

And keep this away from the neutral habit, because that's the one that makes your English sound wrong instead of angry. Used to says it happened regularly and says nothing about how you felt: We used to eat there every Friday. Kept says the same regularity plus somebody's patience running out. So the drill below is a meaning test, not a form test — the sentence tells you which one it's feeling.

B. What is the line feeling?

Six lines, one gap each, four options in the bank. Read for the complaint: if nobody in the line is annoyed, one of these words is already out.

kept kept on wouldn't stop used to

We eat at that place every Friday, back when money was easy.
He texting me at three in the morning, so I turned the phone off.
She going, right in front of her own mother.
The neighbor's dog barking, and I swear I almost lost it.
I telling him to slow down, and he never once did.
She asking the same damn question, and I gave her the same answer.

Numbers 1 and 2 describe the same regularity, and the only difference is that nobody in number 1 is angry. Number 4 is the one to hear out loud — wouldn't stop already carries the whole argument, which is why it usually ends the sentence instead of starting it.

5. Seven verbs for one blow-up who exploded, who panicked, and who just watched

A. The night, retold by two people who were there

Same night as the opener. Read it once for the story, then once for the verbs — seven of them, and not two of them mean the same thing.

Ray: So we're outside the bar, right, and this dude comes up hot. He was going off about a parking spot, bro. Screaming at a girl who wasn't even in it.

Ray: Marco tells him to chill, and the guy goes off on Marco instead. Full f---in' meltdown, spit and everything.

Sofia: I was standing by the whole time. I ain't proud of it, but I wasn't getting in the middle of that.

Ray: Marco never raised his voice. He blacked out for like two seconds and came back swinging.

Sofia: He didn't black out. He lost it. There's a difference, and you know there is.

Ray: Fine. He lost it. Then the bouncer freaked out and called the cops, and now it's my problem.

Six verbs in that exchange and every one of 'em lands somewhere different. Here they are, in the order they hit:

  • Go off — explode, usually with no warning and usually loud. The guy f---in' went off. Nobody has to be the target: a phone can go off too, which is the same idea with a different volume.
  • Blow up — the same explosion, aimed at a person. He blew up at Marco. You can also blow up a plan, a deal, a group chat, or your own life.
  • Freak out — panic, not anger. The bouncer freaked out = he lost his head and called the cops. A person can freak out over a spider and nobody's insulted.
  • Lose it — lose control, and it covers both of the above. Marco lost it = he swung. You can also lose it laughing, which is the only funny version.
  • Black out — the memory goes, not the person. He blacked out = he doesn't remember the two minutes. No anger in the word at all, which is why Sofia calls it a lie.
  • Stand by — watch and do nothing. I was standing by = I was there, I saw it, I didn't move. Not brave, not guilty, just honest.
  • Keep doing — section 4's verb, and the one that turns any of the other six into a pattern.

Two of those lines are the ones you'll actually hear on a Monday. At that point he blacked out, the guy f---in' went off. — at that point is the marker that puts you back inside the night, and the whole line is one person telling you what they saw. Then the confession, from the one who didn't move: I was just standing by while all that shi- happened. Present it that way, -ing on the watching and plain past on the shi-, and nobody asks you a follow-up question, because you already answered it.

B. Name the verb from the meaning

Seven lines. The bracket doesn't give you the word — it gives you what the line means, so you produce it. No bank to copy from, and every verb is in the past.

He on the sidewalk, screaming at nobody in particular. (exploded with no warning)
She at him in front of everybody. (exploded, aimed at one person)
The bouncer and called the cops. (panicked — he wasn't even angry)
Marco after the second shove. (lost control and swung)
He for two seconds and came back swinging. (has no memory of those two seconds)
I the whole time, and I ain't proud of it. (watched it and did nothing)
She doing it, and right then I f---in' lost it. (repeated it)

Number 3 and number 4 are the pair that separates people who know these words from people who guess: a bouncer who panics freaks out, and a man who swings loses it. Swap them and the sentence is still English — it's just the wrong night. Number 7 is the sentence you came for.

6. Tell it the version you'd send to the group chat

A. Last Friday, four lines

One message, four lines, in this order: what was already going, what came in on top, what kept happening, and how it ended. No key here — a made-up story reads like a made-up story, and a teacher reads this one.

Write it the way you text. The scene first, so the other person can see where you were standing. Then the thing that came in — plain past, short, no -ing on it. Then one kept, because every real story has one and it's where your opinion goes. Then the ending, and the ending is allowed to be stupid.

Read it back in order. If your first line has no -ing, there's no scene and the story starts twice. If your second line has one, you've got two scenes and nothing happened. If there's no kept, nobody in it wanted anything. And if your last line explains what the story means, cut it — the other person already got it.

B. Three questions, at the speed you talk

The test was never the rule. It's three questions you can run while somebody's still talking, and they're short enough to run in the time it takes to answer.

  1. Was it going on? Then -ing — I was walking, we were eating, she was crying.
  2. Did it come in and finish? Then plain past — he shouldered me, the lights went out, she read the text.
  3. Did it happen again and again until somebody's patience ran out? Then kept — she kept doing it.

And that's the page: one tense built from two pieces you already had, one joiner family that tells you what comes after it, one habit trap from Portuguese, and seven verbs for the night everything went off. Nothing on this page needed a new word for "past" — you changed the shape of the verb, and the same vocabulary started telling a story instead of filing a report.

Next page picks up the one shape tonight can't do: the thing that was going on and is still going on. I've been trying to call you all f---in' day — same scene you just built, except the door never closed and the counting hasn't stopped. That's lesson 29, and it's where the three questions above get a fourth.
