EXAM — A Night Out, Start to Finish

One Friday night, from the six o'clock phone call to the half-past-two argument on the sidewalk. Everything from the first twenty-four lessons has to survive the same evening.

This is the exam, and it runs as one scene: Ray's birthday, a bar on Ferry Street, a diner on the corner, and a bouncer with no sense of humor. Each section is one beat of the night, and each beat crosses a family of things you already know — the routine and the right now in the same breath, the be question and the do question a line apart, the plan you made yesterday and the decision you make mid-sentence. That crossing is the exam. Nobody in a bar asks you one grammar point at a time.

There is no new grammar on this page. Each section says under its heading which family it is measuring, so when something feels wrong you know where to go back to. Do the whole night in one sitting if you can.

1. Six o'clock — the call

A. He calls while you are still at work

The fact and the moment, then the be question and the do question — all in one short call.

Your phone rings at six. Ray is at the shop, still in his work shirt, and he wants to know if tonight is happening. You have one ear on him and one ear on your boss.

1. Ray (work) every Friday till nine — he's never out before that.

2. Right now he (work) late, because two guys called in sick.

3. he working tonight, or is he off?

4. he work every Friday, or only this week?

5. He (not / like) the new bar on Ferry, so he wants the old one.

6. The girls (wait) outside the shop right now, in the cold.

2. Eight o'clock — who's in

A. Six questions in one minute

The be question flips with no helper. Every other verb borrows one. Same night, same phone.

Ray is texting. Everybody wants a different piece of information and nobody has time for a full sentence.

1 ___ Ray coming tonight, or is he staying in?
2 ___ you have the tickets, or did Ray keep them?
3 "Is she coming?" "No, she ___ — she's working till midnight."
4 "Do you have the keys?" "Yeah, I ___."
5 What time ___ the game start?
6 Where ___ they sitting — inside, or out on the sidewalk?

4 She arranged it last week:
5 An offer, made on the spot, to somebody you just met:

4. Eleven o'clock — getting there

A. The get family, then the little words

Four questions about get first, then the six little words that decide where the night happens.

1 Which one means "leave the house"?
2 Which one means "come home again"?
3 Which one do you do to a bus?
4 Which one means "leave your bed in the morning"?

B. Six little words, one sidewalk

to, for, on, at — the ones that carry the whole night. One gap each.

1. Ray's driving, so this round is him.

2. We're going the bar on Ferry, not the one on Main.

3. I'll meet you the corner of Ferry and Main.

4. The girls are waiting us at the door.

5. Ray's birthday party is Friday night.

6. We got to the bar nine, right when the line started.

5. One a.m. — last week, retold

A. Somebody at the bar asks what you did last Friday

Past simple, every verb irregular you know, and the wires from lesson 22 holding it together.

The music is loud enough that you have to lean in, which means you get about four sentences before somebody loses interest. Order matters.

1. Last Friday we (go) to the bar on Main, the one with the jukebox.

2. Ray (buy) the first round with his birthday money.

3. First we (have) a beer at his place, then we walked down.

4. By midnight we (be) broke, so we (leave) early.

5. All of a sudden the bouncer (tell) us to go home.

6. In the end we (take) an Uber, and it (cost) forty bucks.

Now the same night in your own words: five lines, at least three of the connectors from lesson 22, and every verb in the past.

6. Two a.m. — which bar

A. Ranking the night

Comparative, superlative, and the one that does not take more.

You are outside the diner with two bars behind you and one argument in front of you: which one was better, and is the third one even worth the walk.

1. The bar on Ferry is (good) than the one on Main — better music, better beer.

2. The one on Main is much (bad) than this one, honestly.

3. This place is (crowded) than the diner at this hour.

4. Which is the (cheap) bar around here — the one with the dollar beers?

5. The diner is as (good) as the bar, and the coffee is better.

6. The line looks short, but the place is (big) than it looks from the street.

7. Half past two — the wrong room

A. One swear in the right place, one in the wrong one

Same night, four rooms. The word does not change — the room does.

1 Two a.m., car full of your friends, the bar has just closed behind you. Which line?
2 Ray's parents' kitchen, and you drop a glass on the floor.
3 On the phone with the customer who is paying the invoice you just sent wrong.
4 Which one is aimed at a person, and starts the fight?

8. Final check — the whole night in ten lines

A. One verb, or two, in every line

Nothing new here. Everything crossed: routine against right now, past against present, ranking inside a sentence.

1. Ray usually (work) Fridays, but tonight he (not / work) — he's out with us.

2. We (be) at the diner at two, because the bar (close) at one.

3. The diner is (cheap) than the bar, and the food is (good).

4. I need to get of the house before nine.

5. This round is you, Ray — happy birthday.

6. Long story short, we (get) home at four in the morning.

B. Four questions to close the night

One helper in each question, and one line that belongs in one room only.

1 ___ he gonna come tonight, or is he staying home?
2 ___ he come last week, or was that the week before?
3 ___ you waiting for somebody, or are you on your own?
4 You are on the phone with the customer and you have just made a mistake. Which line?
