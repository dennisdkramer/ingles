The Guy Who — Two Ideas, One Sentence

Who, that, which, where: the tool that turns two plain sentences into one adult one.

Two sentences become one

Who, that, which

The second sentence moves inside the first, and its subject becomes a connector

You already have two sentences for every description: I know a guy. He never pays. English gives you one word to fold the second into the first: I know a guy who never pays. That is a relative clause, and it is how adults say a paragraph in a sentence.

who for people, which for things, that for both in speech. If you learn only one, learn that: it is never wrong in a spoken defining clause. "The guy who runs it is a real asshole." "The bar that opened on the corner is already packed."

The comma changes the meaning, and it is not decoration. The guy who f---ed me over is my brother. — no comma, the clause identifies which guy, and there may be several. My brother, who f---ed me over, is coming tonight. — commas, the clause is extra information; there is only one brother and you are adding the accusation.

In the street, the whole system compresses into one line: "That's the guy who f---ed me over — the one who never pays." Two relative clauses, one person, and no sentence ends in the middle.

Person, thing, place

One table, three connectors

That's the bar. It opened last month. That's the bar that opened last month. that — thing
I hate the app. It eats my battery. I hate the app which eats my battery. which — thing, a little formal
The guy runs it. He's an asshole. The guy who runs it is a real asshole. who — as the new subject
Portuguese uses que for all of it, which is why your instinct is right and your who/which choice is the only thing to train.

Where, when, why

The place, the day, the reason

Three nouns get their own connectors, and they replace a preposition

where = in / at / on which, for a place: "the place where we met", "the bar where he works". when = on / in which, for a time: "the day when everything broke", "the night when he showed up". why = for which, and it almost always follows the reason: "the reason why I left".

Two of them are optional in speech: "the place we met" and "the day everything broke" are both normal, because where and when can be dropped like any object. why is stickier — "the reason why" survives because it does a job nothing else does: "The thing that bugs me is the f---in' price, that's the reason why I left."

Cuidado com "where" traduzindo "onde" em frases que não são de lugar: "the case where" não existe em conversa — é "the case when" ou "if".

The pronoun you can drop

Object: drop it. Subject: keep it.

One test decides, and it takes two seconds

When the person or thing is the object of the second sentence, English drops the connector entirely: the guy I met, the movie we watched, the thing I hate most. No who, no that — and if you say "the guy who I met", everybody understands you, but they hear the extra word.

When the person is the subject of the second sentence, the connector cannot go: the guy who called me. Drop it and "the guy called me" becomes a complete sentence about a different fact.

The test: look right after the connector. If a subject is already there — I, we, he, they, it — the connector is the object and can go. If the verb comes next with nobody in front of it, the connector is the subject and stays.

O português também pode omitir ("o cara que eu conheci" → "the guy I met"). O que não pode é omitir o sujeito: "the guy called me" é outra frase.

Whatever, whoever, and the rest

Four shapes that make you sound like you have an opinion

  • whoever — any person, no matter who: "Whoever shows up, we're fine."
  • whatever — any thing, no matter what: "Whatever you want, I'm paying."
  • somebody who / something that — the indefinite pair: "I need somebody who actually answers the phone."
  • the kind of — a type, with a clause after it: "He's the kind of guy who never pays."
  • such as — examples, not a complete list: "Bring citrus, such as lemon or lime."
  • the reason why — the only place why really lives: "That's the reason why I left."

Put two of them in one line and you sound fluent, not textbook: "Whatever you want, whoever shows up — I don't give a shi-." Two free-choice pronouns, zero conditions, and the sentence still has a subject at the end.

Two into one

Fill the connector

Each pair of sentences is already joined; the connector is missing. Use who, that, which, where, when or why.

  1. That's the guy f---ed me over — the one who never pays.
  2. I hate the app eats my battery.
  3. That's the bar he works on weekends.
  4. I remember the night he showed up with no warning.
  5. The reason I left is simple: the money.
  6. The guy I met at the party never called.
  7. shows up first gets the good table.

Gap 6 is the one to study: that or who both work, and so does nothing at all — "the guy I met" — because the connector is the object there. Gap 7 is capitalised, because it starts the sentence.

Three descriptions

One person, one place, one grudge

Write three lines about your own life: a person you know (who), a place you go (where), and something that annoys you (that). Each line must contain two ideas, not two sentences.

Model answer

1. I have a friend who disappears every time somebody mentions a bill.

2. There's a bar where the music is so loud you order with your fingers.

3. The thing that bugs me is the f---in' price, that's the reason why I left.

Line 3 carries three connectors: that for the thing, why for the reason, and the whole line still reads as one breath. That is the level to aim for.
