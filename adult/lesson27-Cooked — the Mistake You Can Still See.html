Cooked — the Mistake You Can Still See

Six hours ago you f---ed up, and every box in that shipment's still wrong. English got one shape that says exactly that — and the word in the title comes in section 4.

A. Six hours later, and still true

Read the six lines first, and do not try to build them — they are one morning, seen from where you are standing now. The shape that explains all six arrives in section 1, and the word for what you are gets its own block in section 4.

  • The shipment's still on the dock, in the wrong boxes.
  • The customer's still waiting, and he ain't answering his phone.
  • Your boss still ain't looked at you since nine.
  • Two f---in' grand's still missing, and the paperwork still got your name on it.
  • The email's still sitting in his inbox, unread.
  • Your hands are still shaking, and it's been six hours.

One event, six consequences. The whole thing took four minutes this morning. Every line up there is what those four minutes left behind, and not one of 'em is a finished story — the mess ain't in the past, dude. It's on the dock, in his inbox, and in your hands.

That's the sentence you ain't got yet: not what happened, but what it left. By the end of this page you build it without stopping to think, and section 5 makes you send it to the man who's still waiting.

1. The result is the point the helper that reports instead of counting

A. Same helper, new job

Last lesson the helper counted time. Tonight it hands you a result, and the result is the only reason the sentence exists.

In lesson 26 the helper carried how long: I've worked here for five years, and those five years were the whole point. Same helper tonight, different job. I've f---ed up does not tell you when. It tells you the mess is still there.

Put the two shapes side by side. I lost my keys is a report of a finished moment — it happened, it is filed, and you have moved on. I've lost my keys is a state: the keys are still lost, you are still outside, and the door is still locked. Same event, both times. One sentence describes a moment; the other describes you, now.

The eventSaid as a finished momentSaid as what is true now
you lose your keysI lost my keys at the bar last night.I've lost my keys — I can't get in.
he breaks the machineHe broke it on Monday.He's broken it — it's dead, bro.
you delete the fileI deleted it by accident.I've deleted it — the folder's empty.
A date closes the report: yesterday, on Monday, at the bar last night. A mess you can still point at opens the state. The test is two questions, and the second one decides: is it finished? Both are. Can you still see what it left?

And the words from last lesson keep working, which is what makes this shape cheap once you have it: I've just f---ed up puts the mistake a minute ago, I've already f---ed up twice today turns it into a pattern, I haven't told him yet leaves the door open. The vocabulary did not change. What the sentence is for changed, and that is kinda the whole lesson.

B. Four still true, four already filed

Four of these lines are still true and say so with for or since — the shape lesson 26 gave you. The other four carry a date, and a date kills the helper. The bracket gives the verb; you give the form.

I here for five years, and I still ain't got a raise. (work)
She about it since Monday, and she ain't over it. (think)
We the same dumbass mistake for two months. (make)
He in sick every Friday since March. (call)
I my keys at the bar last night. (lose)
He the wrong file on Friday — one f---in' click. (send)
We the rent money in 2019 and laughed about it. (spend)
He his phone on Sunday, in front of everybody. (break)

The first four are why the helper exists: the thing is still true, so the sentence still points at now. The last four are the Brazilian mistake this page kills — I've lost my keys last night is wrong in every room, and now you know why.

2. One line in, two doors out the divide that makes this tense expensive

A. Portuguese picks for you. English makes you pick.

One Portuguese sentence becomes two English sentences, both correct, and they do not mean the same thing. That is the divide — it is not a rule you can skip, and it is not a grammar you can translate.

Perdi as chaves.

Read it once more. It can mean I lost my keys — a report: it happened, and you have moved on to the next thing. It can also mean I've lost my keys — a state: you are outside right now, with your hand on a locked door. Portuguese leaves the choice to the moment, and the moment usually makes it for you. English hands the choice to you and buries it in the verb.

The same three words, both doors, and the situation decides:

Perdi as chaves ontem. → I lost my keys yesterday. The date is in the sentence, so one door is already shut.

Perdi as chaves — and you are still outside, still calling the landlord. → I've lost my keys. No date, and the mess is still standing in front of you.

Two more traps, and both come from Portuguese doing something English refuses to do:

Já. Já terminei is not a free pass to the helper. America says I already finished and I've already finished, and both walk down the same street. The helper is the safe one; the fast one is not a mistake. Lesson 26 made that point already, and it still holds tonight.

Tenho. Tenho trabalhado muito is not the sentence you are reaching for. What you mean is I've been working a lot — that shape is lesson 29, and it is a different machine. Tonight's helper plus a participle reports a result, not a habit: I've worked too much this week is about what it did to you, not about what you keep doing.

B. The situation picks the door

Six Portuguese lines, six situations. One option is English; the other two are the two failures this divide produces — the helper on a date, and the wrong form after the helper.

1.Perdi o emprego em março. — and you have a better job now.
2.Perdi o celular. — you are texting from a friend's phone, still locked out of everything.
3.Ainda não achei. — you are still looking, and the room is a disaster.
4.Terminei ontem. — the report is off your desk and nobody cares any more.
5.Você viu meu irmão? — he is standing right behind you.
6.Alguém já pagou a conta. — and the receipt is in your hand.

Nothing on this page is a new tense for you to memorise. It is one decision, made every time you open your mouth: is this a report, or is it a mess I am still standing in?

3. Been there, gone there two participles, one question between them

A. Where is he now?

Both lines are about a trip. Only one of them tells you where the man is standing while you say it.

Tati: Where's Diego? He ain't answering his phone.

You: He's gone to Miami. Took the early flight, the bastard.

Tati: Shut up. Miami? For real?

You: Yeah. Why you asking me where shi- is?

Tati: 'Cause I've been to Miami. Went once, two years ago, broke the whole time. Being there once ain't the same as knowing it, bro.

He's gone to Miami — he is in Miami. Gone is the participle of go, and in this shape it means he left and he has not come back.

I've been to Miami — she went and she came back. Been is the participle of be, and here it means visited and returned. Tati can say that with her feet on your floor, because she is home. That is the whole difference, and it is why the same trip can be told two ways.

One question settles it: where is he now? There → gone. Here → been.

Have you ever been to…? is the question form, and it only ever takes been — the person is standing in front of you, so you are asking about their life, not about their location. And I've been in Miami for three days is a third thing again: in plus a length means you are still there, which is last lesson's for doing its usual job.

B. Here or there

Six lines, one gap each. Every line already tells you where the person is, if you read it — that is the clue, not the bracket.

Where's Ana? — She to work. She'll be back at six. (go)
I to that bar once. It was trash. (be)
He to Brazil. He ain't coming back this year. (go)
Have you ever to a game? (be)
They to the gym — they're still there. (go)
My bad, I to that place before. I just forgot the name. (be)

Number 4 is the one that gets asked at every f---in' table in America: Have you ever been to…? Never gone — the person you are asking is right there in front of you.

4. The act, the state, and the way out ten words for the worst afternoon of your month

A. Three verbs do the damage

They mean the same thing and they are not the same temperature — one is what you say about a kid, one is what you say about yourself at two in the morning, and one is what you say when the chance is already gone.

Mess up is the soft one: I messed up the order. You can say it about a new guy and he will still speak to you tomorrow. Screw up is the one you say about yourself once you have stopped being polite with yourself: I screwed up, bro. Big time. And blow it is the loud one — it takes a chance, a deal, an interview, a shot, and the thing is gone by the time you finish the sentence: She's f---in' blown it, we're done for.

Then there is the line this page is built around, the one you say with your back against a wall: I've f---ed up big time — I'm screwed, I'm cooked. Look at the two halves. The first half is the act, in the perfect, with the helper doing its job. The second half has no helper and no participle at all — just I'm and a word that means finished. Same logic, different machinery: one half says what you did, the other says what you are now.

That is why the pair below is worth keeping straight: I've messed it up is the act, and I'm screwed is the state. Nah — put the helper where am belongs and you say something else entirely: I'm messed up means drunk, or in bad shape, and at 4:40 on a Tuesday it will land like a joke nobody asked for.

B. What you are at 4:40

Three words for the same situation, and the one you pick tells people how bad it is before you explain anything.

Screwed is the general one, and it fits any trouble: I'm screwed — the money's gone. It carries a little self-blame and no drama, which is why it is the one you say to your boss.

Cooked is the newer one, the one in every group chat: I'm cooked, bro. There's no way out of this one. Done, finished, and usually said with a laugh that ain't funny.

Done for is the dramatic one, and it belongs to we: She's blown it, and now we're done for. One person f---s up; everybody pays. That is the whole point of the word, and that is why it is never about one guy alone.

C. The way out

Four verbs, four different kinds of fixing. Choose the wrong one and you promise something you ain't doing.

Fix is for a broken thing, and you can point at it: Can you fix it? I fixed the machine. If there is an object with a crack in it, this is the verb.

Sort out is for a mess, not a thing — and it is what a boss says when he does not care how: Sort it out. Lemme sort it out is what you say when you need ten minutes and no questions. And it works on your own life: I gotta sort my shi- out before Monday.

Figure out happens in your head, not with your hands: I can't figure out why he's so pissed at me. And the line you will hear on Monday morning, both verbs in one breath: Have you figured it out yet? Sort it out. First the answer, then the action.

Make it is about arriving anyway: Can you make it by six? It is also the one good ending this page has, and section 5 closes on it.

D. One word, and the night changes

Six lines, one gap each — no bank, because the words are already on this page and the sentence tells you which kind of fixing it wants. The bracket is there for the one item that asks for a participle.

The printer's dead again. Can you it before the meeting?
Don't explain it to me. Just it out before Friday.
I can't out why he's so pissed at me.
She's f---in' blown it, and now we're for.
The flight's at six. We can still it if we leave now, bro.
He's it again — third time this shi- month. (blow)

Item 4 is the one to hear in your own voice: we're done for is not we've finished, and it never means anything good. Item 5 points the other way, and section 5 lives on that side.

5. Send it the apology, the panic, and the good ending

A. Your thumb is on the screen

It is 4:40, the shipment is still wrong, and your boss's chat is open. Write both messages in the box — the one to him, and the one to a friend. There is no key here: a lie reads like a lie, and a teacher reads this.

The first message needs three things, in this order. What you did — helper plus participle. What is true right now — one of the states: I'm screwed, I'm cooked, we're done for. And what you're gonna do about it — one of the four from section 4C. Keep it under four lines, because nobody reads a paragraph at 4:40, and write it the way you text.

The second message goes to your friend, and it is allowed to be one line of pure panic: I've f---ed up big time, bro. I'm cooked.

Read it back when you are done. If the first message has no participle in it, you have written a report; if it has no state, you have written a confession with no ending; if it has no repair verb, you have written the text that gets you fired.

B. The result can be a win

Everything on this page has been about damage. The helper does not care — it only asks whether the result is still standing there when you speak.

Same machinery, other direction: We've f---in' made it — it's over. We've done it. I've finally got it. Nothing changes in the grammar. The act is finished, the result is you, standing there, still not believing it — and the sentence is doing exactly the same work as the one you send at 4:40, because the helper never learned the difference between a disaster and a win.

And that is the test, one more time, in three questions you can run at the speed of speech. Is there a date in the sentence? Then the verb is past simple, and the door is shut — yesterday, on Monday, in March. Can you still point at what the action left? Then the helper comes in, and the result is the sentence. Is it somebody's trip? Then it is been or gone, decided by one thing only: where that person is standing right now.

You walked in with perdi as chaves — one line, two doors, and nothing in your own language to tell you which. You walk out picking the door on purpose: I lost my keys yesterday, or I've lost my keys and I am still outside. That choice is the lesson, and from tonight it is yours to make.
