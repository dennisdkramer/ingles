Sarcasm, hyperbole, and roasting

Your friend looks at your burnt toast and says nice work, chef. Every word is a compliment and the whole sentence is an insult. Nothing in the grammar changes between those two readings — the tone does, and tone is what this page teaches you to hear, to aim, and to take back.

1. Oh, great. Another meeting.

B. Pick the reply that means the opposite

Each scene has one reply that says the reverse of its own words. The other two mean exactly what they say.

2. sure, obviously, yeah right, as if

B. Which one fits the scene

Every one of these refusals is polite on the surface. What separates them is what was put in front of you: a claim, a request, a reason, or a favour you cannot refuse out loud.

1.Your friend swears he will be famous by Friday. It is a claim about the future and you believe none of it.
2.Your nine-year-old cousin asks to borrow your car. She is asking for something you will never hand over.
3.A colleague walks in forty minutes late holding a coffee and says "traffic". He lives across the road. You are answering the reason, not the man.
4.Your boss asks whether you can work Saturday. You will not refuse out loud, and he will hear the refusal anyway.

3. literally the worst thing that has ever happened

A. Two dials

Exaggeration runs on a booster and an absolute. The booster is the word doing the shouting inside the phrase — f---ing, absolute, total, literally. The absolute is the claim with no room left in it: the worst, ever, my whole life, a thousand times. Turn both up and the sentence stops being true, which is exactly how the joke lands. There is a third move and it runs the other way: I'm not even mad refuses the whole reaction, and a refusal that flat is louder than any swear.

Ninguém acredita no número. O exagero não é uma mentira: é um convite para rir junto.

B. Find the booster you already know

The booster carries no facts at all. It only turns the volume up on the word beside it.

1.In "that was a f---ing disaster", which word is the booster?
2.In "an absolute nightmare of a Monday", which word is the booster?
3.In "you are totally out of your mind", which word is the booster?

C. Inflate it

Keep the fact and inflate the size of it. Change the two words that do the work, not the story.

1. A two-minute queue at the coffee shop: "I stood in that queue for ."
2. Your phone slips off the sofa and survives: "I died."
3. Your friend apologises for ruining the night and you are long past angry: "I'm mad."
4. One typo in a report you did not write: "This is ."

A. Three voice notes and one recording

B. What the reply is doing

Not one of these replies is a report. Each one is a reaction, and the reaction is always bigger than the news — that size is the point.

1.Someone answers your news with a flat "shut up". Nothing about their voice is angry. What are they telling you?
2.A voice note gets one reply: "I'm dead." Nobody has died. What is the reply saying?
3.You heard every word clearly and you still refuse to accept the fact. Not "sorry?" — you are not asking for a repeat. Which reply is it?
4.You say "I literally died" about a two-line text message. What is the word literally doing there?

5. Roasting a friend without getting hit

A. Roast, insult, or compliment?

A roast and an insult can use the same words. What decides which one it was is who it lands on, who is watching, and whether the person is still laughing at the end of it.

1."You run like the bus is always leaving." Said to a close friend, about the way he runs, with him laughing.
2."You are the reason your parents gave up." Said to that same friend, in front of his family, about something he cannot change.
3."Nice shirt. Did your dad dress you?" Said to a friend who put his dad's shirt on this morning on purpose, and who is now showing everyone.
4."That was genuinely the best goal I have seen this year." Said after his match, and you mean every word of it.

B. The two words that end it

A roast you cannot take back is a roast you lost. Make me closes it: there is no argument to win, nothing to deny, and the joke stops with you instead of running on.

1. "You'd never say that to my face." Answer in two words: ""
2. "You still owe me twenty." Refuse the argument without denying the debt: ""
3. "You can't even lift that." Answer without moving: ""

6. Roast yourself first

A. Why it works

Self-deprecation takes the joke before anyone else can have it. You name the thing you are worst at, in your own words, at your own size, and the room laughs with you instead of at you. Once is a joke. Twice in a row reads as fishing for reassurance, and that is the whole boundary.

B. Which line lands

Three of these keep the joke and keep your face. One of them gives the room a job to do for you.

1.You arrive late to dinner. Again. Pick the line that takes the joke before anyone else does.
2.You trip on the last step at a party and the whole room sees it. Pick the line that lands.
3.A friend roasts your cooking and everyone turns to you. Pick the line that takes the joke back.
4.First day in a new job, you kill the coffee machine. Pick the reply that lands — this one is the boundary.

C. Take the roast

Someone roasts you. Own it, do not deny it, and do not fire back — the reply that agrees with the joke is the reply that takes it away from them.
