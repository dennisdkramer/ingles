So Last Year — Dead Slang and Regional Splits

The word is right, the year is wrong, or the country is wrong. Here is how to check before you say it.

Who may say it

Borrowing rules

Three questions before a slang word leaves your mouth

Lesson 38 taught the set; this lesson teaches the judgement. Before you use a slang word, run three questions. Whose word is it? If it comes from a group you are not part of, it is theirs to lend, not yours to take. How old is it? A word that peaked four years ago reads as a costume. Where is it from? A word that is normal in London can be completely dead in Ohio, and the reverse.

Quem chega de fora e usa gíria de um grupo sem convite parece piada. A regra brasileira é a mesma: "mano" entre amigos é normal; "mano" num relatório é estranho.

Old school dope is still sick, legit — f--- what they say. That line is the test in miniature: old school, dope, sick and legit are all borrowed, all dated in some rooms, and all alive in others. The speaker who can say it and mean it is the one who knows exactly who he is talking to.

The seven, with their lifetimes

Each word carries a date and a region, and both are part of the meaning

Word, meaning, and where it is alive
Word Means Status
basic unoriginal, following the crowd alive, and still a small insult
extra doing too much, theatrical alive, mild, often affectionate
sick excellent (or ill, from context) alive since the 80s — a survivor
dope excellent, cool alive, and ageing; "old school dope" is now a joke about itself
legit genuine, real, seriously alive in both US and UK
bogus fake, unfair, a rip-off dated in the US, alive in UK skate and surf talk
hype excitement, or over-promoted alive as a noun; as "all hype" it means empty promises
"Bogus f---in' excuse, all hype, extra drama." Three of these in one line, and the whole thing says: fake reason, empty promises, too much theatre.

Dead words and wrong countries

Dated and regional

The fastest way to sound like a tourist in your own language

Dated is not the same as wrong. "So last year" tells you the thing happened recently and is already out of fashion. "Old school" is a compliment to something old that still works. "Cringe" is the current judgement, and "That's a cringe take — not gonna lie." is how it lands in a group chat. Words that died and stay dead in the US: "groovy", "rad" (apart from a joke), "bling" as a compliment.

Regional splits are harsher than learners expect. In the UK, wicked means excellent, sorted means arranged, and "that's pants" means it is bad. In the US, solid means reliable, bet means deal, and wicked only means evil. Say "wicked" as a compliment in Ohio and people will think you are describing a villain.

The practical rule: pick the country you are actually speaking to and stay inside it. Mixing dialects is what makes a fluent speaker sound like a search engine.

Your three words

One for friends, one for work, one never

Write three lines: a slang word you would use with friends (and why it is alive), one you could use at work without a problem, and one you would never use — with the reason.

Model answer

1. With friends: "That set was sick — I'm going again next week." Sick is forty years old and still working, and the room is right.

2. At work: "That's a solid plan." Solid is slang-adjacent and safe in any office in the US.

3. Never: "bogus" in the US, or any word borrowed from a group I am not part of, in a room that is not mine to borrow in.

Three lines, three judgements, and the second line is the one you will use most: the words that survive at work are the ones that stopped being slang and became normal English.
