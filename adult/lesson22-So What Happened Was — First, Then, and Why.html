So What Happened Was — First, Then, and Why

Somebody asks what happened last night. You were there for all of it. And you still can't tell it.

The words for the events, you already have: we went, he showed up, she left. What English won't give you for free is the wire between them. Say the events flat and the listener has a pile, not a night: We went to the bar. Ray's ex came in. We left. True, and useless.

So you say the wire out loud instead: First we were at Ray's, then we hit the bar on the corner, and all of a sudden his ex walks in, so we left because nobody wanted that mess. Later we ate, and in the end it was a good night. Long story short: I got three hours of sleep.

Four jobs, and that's the whole page: order, the turn, the reason, the short version. It is the first time on this track that you are not answering a question — you are holding the floor, and somebody has to be able to follow you.

1. Four jobs, ten words

A. What a story has to do before it can be a story

Ten words, four jobs. Everything else on this page is these.

The jobThe wordsHow it comes out
Order — what came firstfirst · then · after that · laterFirst we hit Ray's, then we hit the bar.
The turn — the thing nobody plannedsuddenly · all of a suddenAll of a sudden this dude starts yelling.
The reason and the resultbecause · soNobody paid, so we left. We left because nobody paid.
The short versionin the end · long story shortIn the end nobody cared. Long story short, we got kicked out.
Leave the connector out and the listener starts guessing the order. A person who is guessing is not listening. That is the entire reason these ten words exist, and it is also why the wrong one is worse than none.

2. Order: what came first

A. The four order words

All four point back at the same night. What changes is how much of the night they skip.

The connector goes at the front of the sentence, and then you say what happened. The verb does not lift a finger to help you: every verb in the story stays in the past simple — we went, he showed up, she left — and the connector is what tells the listener where on the line you are standing.

WordWhat it doesIn the wild
firstopens the story. Nothing in it happened before this.First we were at Ray's, just playing cards.
thenthe next step, right after — no gap worth mentioning.Then the girls showed up and the plan changed.
after thatthe next step, with a gap in the middle that you are skipping.After that we walked down to the bar on the corner.
latera jump forward. You are not saying how long the jump was.Later, around two, Ray finally showed up.
then also works mid-sentence with no and in front of it: "We ate, then we crashed." · after that is two words for a reason — after on its own needs something behind it: after the bar, after we left. Nothing behind it? Then it needs that. Depois disso is after that; depois de is after.

3. The scene, then the thing that happened

A. was and were hold the picture still

One line sets the place and the mood. The next line drops the thing that actually moved.

was and were do not move a story. They hold a picture: it was late, we were broke, the bar was packed, nobody was answering. A verb in the past simple is the one that moves: he showed up, she left, it kicked off. So the scene goes first, the event goes second, and the listener already knows what room they are standing in when it lands.

The scene — was / were, no movementThe event — past simple, something moves
It was late and we were broke.Ray called his brother, and his brother showed up.
The bar on the corner was packed.Then two dudes started pushing each other.
Nobody was answering the phone.All of a sudden the lights went out.
I was three beers in and the music was loud.I said something stupid to the wrong guy.
suddenly and all of a sudden are the same job: the event you did not see coming. All of a sudden is what people say out loud, suddenly is what shows up in writing, and neither one will get you a funny look. Put either of them in front of the event, never in front of the scene — a scene does not happen to you.

4. Why it happened: so and because

A. Same two facts, two directions

The cause and the result are one pair. English makes you pick which end to start from.

Start with the cause and land on the result: cause, so result. Start with the result and hand back the cause: result because cause. Same verbs, same past simple, same night — only the direction changes, and in writing the only punctuation you have to care about is the comma in front of so.

DirectionThe shapeOut loud
Cause first, result secondA, so BNobody paid, so we left.
Result first, cause secondB because AWe left because nobody paid.
The cause on its own, as the answerbecause AWhy'd you leave? Because nobody paid.
Every so and every because is a link the listener has to hold on to. You can chain them — "We left because nobody paid, so we walked home" — but one link per sentence is what keeps a person with you in a loud bar.

B. The trap: então is two words in English

Time and result are one word in Portuguese and two in English — and the wrong one changes what your story means.

Então covers both jobs. English does not. If the next thing merely came next, it's then. If the next thing happened because of the one before it, it's so. Pick the wrong one and you have just told the listener that one event caused the other.

You: We got to the bar, then Ray's ex walked in.

Ray: Oh, that's wild. What happened?

Nothing caused that. She was already out, and she walked in. Now say it with so — We got to the bar, so Ray's ex walked in — and you have accused the bar of sending for her. Wrong wire, wrong story.

C. So at the front of the sentence

The spoken version drops the cause, because the cause is already on the table.

Speech starts sentences with so all the time: So we left. Nobody hears half a sentence. The cause came in the line before; so just delivers the result — and it is exactly your então from "Então a gente saiu".

Same word, two positions: in the middle with a comma in front of it, or at the front on its own. What so never means is "next" — that word is then, and it is the one you reach for when nothing was caused.

You: Ray was already wasted when we got there.

Ray: So you left?

You: So we left. Dude couldn't even say my name.

5. Where it landed, and the short version

A. Three ways to land a story

Two of them are about the end of the night. The third is about the end of the telling.

in the end is where it all landed once the noise stopped — the result, after everything. at the end is a position: the last part of something, and the something has to be named — at the end of the night, at the end of the street. long story short is not about the night at all: it tells the listener you are skipping the middle and going straight to the point.

WordWhat it doesIn the wild
in the endafter everything, here is where it landedWe argued for an hour. In the end we split a cab.
at the endthe last part of something you name right after itAt the end of the night the bar was empty.
long story shortskip the middle, give the point — comma or colon after itLong story short, we got kicked out.
No final covers the first two in Portuguese, and English splits them: result against position. No final da noite is at the end of the night; no final, todo mundo foi embora is in the end, everybody left. Same two words in Portuguese, two different wires in English.

C. Your turn to hold the floor

Five lines, five connectors, and you are the one who reads it back.

Tell last night in five lines. Use at least five of the ten, and two of them have to be in the end and long story short. When you are done, read it back and try to swap two lines: if the story still works, one of your connectors is doing nothing.
