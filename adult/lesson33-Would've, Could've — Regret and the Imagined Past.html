Would've, Could've — Regret and the Imagined Past

One step back from reality: the life you did not live, and the night you cannot take back.

The present that isn't

One step back from reality

If + past does not mean past — it means "not real right now"

Lesson 32 gave you if + present for a plan that might happen. Now step back one tense and the meaning flips: the thing did not happen and probably will not. That is the second conditional, the language of the life you are not living.

Shape: if + past simple, would + verb. "If I had the money, I'd be out of this shi- tomorrow." I do not have the money. That is the whole message: the if-clause is a door that is closed today.

Boa notícia: o português usa a mesma lógica — "se eu tivesse dinheiro, eu sairia". O would é o "-ria". O erro comum é traduzir "se eu teria" — em inglês o if nunca leva would. Never "if I would have money". The would lives only in the result.

would shrinks to 'd in speech, always: 🔊 "If I had the money, I'd be out of this shi- tomorrow." If you can say "I'd", you own this shape.

Two distances, one regret

The further the verb goes back, the less real the sentence is
ShapeSentenceWhat it tells you
first If I have the money, I'm gonna go. real: maybe I have it
second If I had the money, I'd go. unreal now: I don't have it
third If I had had the money, I'd have gone. unreal then: I did not have it, and the night is over
third, spoken If I'd had the money, I would've gone. same, the way people actually say it
Portuguese marks the same three distances with the subjunctive. What English adds is would've: the result also steps back, and it is the part Brazilians drop.

The past you cannot fix

Had done, would've done

Both halves step back, and the night is already over

The third conditional imagines a past that did not happen. if + had + participle, would've + participle. "If I'd studied, I would've passed." I did not study. I did not pass. Nothing about this sentence can change.

Three results, three flavours, all in the unreal past: would've = what I intended or expected; could've = what was possible; should've = what was right. "I would've called, I swear — in hindsight I should've." Two of them in one line, and that is how regret actually sounds at three in the morning.

The same sentence without if, for drama: Had I known, I wouldn't have signed. It means "if I had known", and it puts the auxiliary first instead of the if. It is a little formal, so it lands hard in an argument — that is exactly why people reach for it.

And the short form of "or else, in the past": otherwise. "I left early; otherwise I'd still be there." One word carries the whole unreal branch, which is why it is so common in speech.

Wish, rather, hindsight

Four ways to stand in front of a closed door

  • wish + past — unreal now: "I wish I had a car." I do not have one.
  • wish + had done — regret about the past: "I wish I'd never f---in' met him." It cannot be undone, and that is the point.
  • would rather — preference, usually against what is happening: "I'd rather stay home, if I had the choice."
  • what if — opens the imagined branch as a question: "What if we just didn't go?"
  • in hindsight — looking back with what I know now: "In hindsight, I should've stayed home."
  • regret — the noun and the verb: "No regrets" is a lie people tell at 4am; "I regret it" is the honest version.

Two people at 3am

Every line is unreal, and none of them can be fixed

  1. Nanda I wish I'd never f---in' met him.
  2. Joana You don't mean that.
  3. Nanda I would've called, I swear — in hindsight I should've.
  4. Joana Then call him tomorrow. Otherwise you'll be here again next week.
  5. Nanda What if he doesn't pick up? Had I known it would end like this, I'd have stayed home.

Count the shapes: wish + had done, would've, should've, otherwise, what if, had I known. Six in five lines — that is what a real 3am talk sounds like, and every one of them is a door that closed.

The missed birthday

Fill the thread

Amanda missed Diego's birthday. Every gap is a door that already closed: keep both halves in the unreal past.

  1. Amanda If I it was your birthday, I would've come.
  2. Diego I invited you, but my phone died.
  3. Amanda I wish I a second phone — mine dies every single night.
  4. Diego In , I should've called your sister.
  5. Amanda What if we out this Friday instead?
  6. Diego I'd stay home, honestly. I'm still broke from the party.
  7. Amanda Come on. we'll be having this exact conversation next month.

Two traps in one thread: gap 5 is a second conditional question, so the verb is went, not would go; and gap 7 starts the sentence, so it is capitalised.

What does the sentence admit?

Each line hides one fact. Pick the fact.

1"If I had a car, I'd drive you to the airport."
2"If I'd had a car, I'd have driven you to the airport."
3"I wish I hadn't said that."
4"Had I known you were coming, I'd have cooked."

Your own closed doors

Three regrets, said out loud

Write three lines you would actually say: one wish + had done (a real regret), one would've / could've (what you intended or what was possible), and one second conditional about your life right now.

Model answer

1. I wish I had saved money when I was still living with my parents.

2. I could've finished the course last year, but I stopped going in March.

3. If I had a better job, I'd move out of this neighborhood tomorrow.

Check your three lines against one question each: is the regret past (line 1), is the result past (line 2), is the condition unreal now (line 3)? If all three answer yes, the doors are in the right tense.
