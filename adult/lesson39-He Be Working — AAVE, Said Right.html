He Be Working — AAVE, Said Right

"He be working late" is not broken English. It is grammar your English does not have.

Be is not the verb "to be" here

Habitual be

A grammar English is missing, not a grammar English got wrong

Standard English has no way to mark habit on the verb. "He works late" can mean every Tuesday or this Tuesday — you need "usually" or "every week" to be sure. African American English solves that with one word: be. "He be working late" means he works late regularly, as a pattern. Not right now — as a habit.

That is why the same speaker can say three different things without confusing anybody: He working late (he is at work right now — the form is there, just without the is), He be working late (that is his pattern), He been working late (he has been at it for a while now). Three aspects, three forms, zero errors.

"She be f---in' trippin'" is the same grammar doing drama: she is regularly, reliably acting crazy. The be is doing the work that always would do in standard English, and it is doing it in one syllable.

Four forms, four meanings

What the speaker means, in standard English
AAVE Standard English What is marked
He working late. He is working late. right now — the is is absent, the meaning is not
He be working late. He works late, regularly. habit, every week
He been working late. He has been working late for a while. started long ago, still true
He done worked late. He has already finished working late. completed, before now
Put the four in order and you have a whole aspect system that standard English needs adverbs to express. That is the opposite of "no grammar".

The markers

Done, been, steady, finna, tryna, ain't

Six words that carry tense and attitude at once

  • done — completed before now: "I done told you." = I already told you.
  • been (stressed) — a long time ago, and still relevant: "I been knew." = I knew that long before you said it.
  • steady — persistently, without stopping: "She steady calling me." = she keeps calling.
  • finna — about to: "I'm finna go." = I am about to leave.
  • tryna — trying to: "I'm tryna see what's up." = I am trying to find out what is going on.
  • ain't — am not, is not, are not, has not, have not: "y'all ain't listening" = you are not listening. One form, many jobs.

Put them in one line and you sound like the music you already listen to: "I done told you, I been knew — y'all ain't f---in' listening." Three markers, one complaint, and the meaning is exact.

Negative concord is agreement

Two negatives in AAVE mean one negative, and everybody understands it

"I don't know nothing about it, big time." In standard English that is an error; in AAVE it is the rule. The negative spreads across the sentence the way it does in Portuguese — não sei nada — so a Brazilian ear actually hears this one faster than a standard-English speaker does.

The same system builds ain't nobody (nobody), ain't nothing (nothing), don't never (never). It is consistent, it is taught by parents to children, and it is not laziness. Your job is to understand it and never to correct it.

Whose language it is

Context and respect

The grammar is one thing; who gets to use it is another

African American English is a dialect with systematic grammar, spoken by millions of Black Americans and studied by linguists for generations. It is not internet slang, not a joke register, and not "bad English with extra steps". Most of the phrases you see on TikTok that are labelled "slang" started as AAVE grammar and got borrowed without credit.

For a learner, five rules keep you on the right side of it. One: understand it first — that is most of the value, and nobody can take it away from you. Two: use it only where the people around you actually speak it, never as a costume. Three: never correct it, and never repeat it in a mocking voice. Four: don't reach for it in a job interview to sound tough. Five: credit it when you quote it — it came from Black American speech, not from a brand.

Code-switching is a skill, not a betrayal. AAVE speakers switch to standard English for a bank and back to their own grammar at home, and they are not being fake in either room. You are allowed to move toward AAVE only in a room where it is shared. When in doubt: understand everything, perform nothing, and answer in your own voice.

Translate both ways

Same meaning, two grammars

Each line gives you an AAVE sentence and a standard English version with one word missing. Fill the missing word — the meaning must stay exactly the same.

  1. "He be working late." → He late, regularly.
  2. "She steady calling me." → She calling me.
  3. "I'm finna go." → I'm to go.
  4. "I done told you." → I have told you.
  5. "I don't know nothing about it." → I don't know about it.
  6. "Y'all ain't listening." → You not listening.
  7. "I'm tryna see what's up." → I'm to see what's going on.
  8. "I been knew." → I that a long time ago.

Two of these are not simplifications: been knew needs a whole clause in standard English, and steady becomes keeps. The dialect is not shorter or longer — it is a different system, and translating it is real work.

Three lines about you

Habit, completion, and one negative

Write three lines in your own voice: something you do regularly (standard English with usually or every), something you have already finished (present perfect), and one sentence with a negative. Then look at the model, which does the same job in AAVE — understand it, do not copy it into a bank.

Model answer

Standard: 1. I work every Saturday. 2. I've already paid the rent. 3. I don't know anything about the meeting.

AAVE: 1. I be working Saturdays. 2. I done paid the rent. 3. I don't know nothing about the meeting.

Same three meanings, two grammars, zero errors. Your job is to own the first column and read the second one without flinching.

Two vocabulary items worth keeping: on point (very well done, usually about an outfit or a look — "That outfit is on point — finna copy it.") and salty (bitter, annoyed about something small — "he's still salty about the game").
