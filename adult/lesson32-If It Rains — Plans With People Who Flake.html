If It Rains — Plans With People Who Flake

Two conditionals, one question — does it always happen, or only this time?

Two shapes, two jobs

Law of the world, or one possible tomorrow?

Both start with if, and they are not the same sentence

There are two if patterns in everyday English, and only one question separates them: does this always happen, or only this time?

Zero — a law of the world. If the if-clause happens, the result happens every time, as a fact. Both verbs sit in the simple present. 🔊 "If it rains, the street floods." Nobody is making plans here; you are describing how the world works, like a sign or a law. This is the shape for rules, habits and truth: "If you drink, you don't drive."

First — one possible tomorrow. The if-clause is one real possibility, and the result is what I will do about it. The if-clause stays in the present; the result takes gonna or will. "If it rains, we're staying in." The present tense under if is the whole trick: never "if it will rain".

Em português o futuro aparece dos dois lados do "se" — "se chover, eu vou ficar". Em inglês o if trava o presente: "if it rains, I'm gonna stay". That single rule keeps the sentence sounding native.

The same weather, both shapes

Read the difference in the result, not in the if

first If it rains, we're staying in — f--- it. this Friday, a decision
zero If she doesn't answer, she's at work. that is what it always means
first If you don't f---in' answer, I'm leaving, or else. this time, and there is a threat in it
The if-clause never takes a future. If you hear "if I will", the speaker is not a native or is making a promise about a promise.

Unless, and the two faces of if

Four cuts of the same sentence

Every item below says the same thing twice, once with unless and once without

  • unless = if not — "Unless you text me first, I'm not f---in' coming." / "If you don't text me first, I'm not coming."
  • already negative — "Unless you don't want to go" is a double negative: the if is doing the same job twice. Say "Unless you want to stay" or "If you don't want to go".
  • when = it is going to happen — "When you get home, call me." The call is expected; the time is not.
  • if = it might not happen — "If you get home before nine, call me." Maybe you will, maybe you will not.

One test, and it never fails: swap if for when in your head. If the sentence now promises something you cannot promise, you wanted if.

In case is not "se"

Two very different habits, one shared translation

Portuguese se covers both, which is exactly why this one costs you. If introduces the condition that makes the plan happen: "If it rains, we're staying in." In case introduces the reason you prepare: "Bring cash, in case." You do not know whether the thing will happen; you are getting ready.

Put the two together in one line and the difference is impossible to miss: No matter what, play it safe and bring cash, in case. No matter what kills every condition; in case answers "why bring cash"; play it safe names the strategy. Three phrases, three jobs.

The rest of the set, in the shape you will hear it: or else — the threat after a warning ("Answer me, or else"); either way — I win both branches ("Rain or sun, either way we're going"); screw it — I stop caring about the risk ("Screw it, let's just go"); no matter — no condition changes it ("No matter what, I'm not driving"); comes down to — this is the only real question ("It comes down to money"); on the safe side — a little extra, to avoid the worst ("Bring a jacket, just to be on the safe side").

Your Friday

Make the plan, name the condition

Write three lines about a real plan: one zero sentence (a fact that is always true in your life), one first sentence (a plan that depends on something this week), and one line with in case (what you prepare because you are not sure).

Model answer

1. If I work past ten, I don't cook — I order something and pass out on the couch.

2. If my sister calls before six, I'm gonna drive her to the airport.

3. I'm bringing my charger, in case the night turns into an all-nighter.

Line 1 is a law of your life, line 2 is one Friday, line 3 is preparation with no condition at all. If your three lines sort that way, the shapes are yours.
