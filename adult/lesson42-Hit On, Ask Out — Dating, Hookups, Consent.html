Hit On, Ask Out — Dating, Hookups, Consent

The vocabulary of wanting someone, and the four words that end the conversation.

Four verbs and one grey area

The words, in the order they happen

Each one marks a different level of intention, and mixing them causes real trouble

Who is doing what, and how clear it is
Phrase What it means How clear it is
hit on someone approach them with romantic or sexual interest, usually a stranger clear intent, no promise of a date
ask someone out invite them on a date, with a time and a place very clear: it is a date
make a move escalate: a kiss, a hand, a step closer clear physically, and the riskiest step
see someone date them regularly, not necessarily exclusive deliberately vague
hook up something sexual, boundaries left undefined on purpose vague by design — ask, do not assume
catch feelings start to care, when you did not plan to honest, a little embarrassing
ghost someone disappear with no message at all clear behaviour, no explanation
The vagueness of hook up is not a language problem — it is the point of the phrase. If you need to know what happened, you have to ask in plain words.

Hit on, ask out, make a move

Three verbs people confuse, and the difference is consent

Hit on is the approach: "He hit on me at the bar." It can be flattering, annoying or embarrassing, and it commits you to nothing. Ask out is the invitation: "He asked me out for Friday." It has a date on it. Make a move is the physical step: "You gonna make a move or just stare at her all night?" — the line friends say when somebody is frozen.

Only the last one requires permission in the moment, and that is why the three must not be blurred. Hitting on someone is a sentence. Making a move is an act. Between them sits the question that decides everything: is this welcome?

"Hit on" não é "bater em". É chegar junto, dar em cima. "Ask out" é convidar para sair. E "make a move" é partir para o beijo ou o toque — sempre precisa de sinal verde.

Two useful phrases for the person being approached: "I'm flattered, but no." (kind and final) and "I'm not interested." (plain and final). Neither one is rude, and neither one requires an excuse.

And two phrases that look innocent and are not: you turn on a light, and somebody can turn you on; you check out a song, a shirt, or a person at the bar — the context decides which one you meant, and the room decides whether you say it out loud.

The other six

Words with a warning attached

Half of these are about wanting, and half are about respect

Say it, then check who is listening
Phrase What it means Watch out
turn someone on arouse them, someone or something attracts you sexually about desire, not about the person's worth
one-night stand one sexual night, no relationship after keep it kind; nobody owes you a morning
friends with benefits friends who also have sex, no romance agreed only works with honest rules and both people free
check someone out look at them because they are attractive looking is fine; staring is not
break it off end it, clearly and finally do it in words, not by disappearing
kiss and tell share the details with everybody afterwards the phrase is a complaint — nobody likes it
English has a separate vocabulary for wanting somebody and for respecting them. A good speaker uses both in the same conversation.

Three lines from the group chat

Read what each one admits

  1. "They're friends with benefits — a one-night stand, whatever." — the arrangement is casual, and the whatever is doing the pretending.
  2. "She turns me on; I'd get with her, but no kiss and tell." — desire admitted, and one line of decency attached to it.
  3. "Check her out if you want, but if she says no, back off and respect it." — the whole lesson in one sentence: looking allowed, no means stop.

The no that ends it

Consent, in the words people actually use

A yes is enthusiastic, current, and specific; everything else is a no

English has a whole set of soft refusals, and every one of them means no: "Not tonight." "I'm good." "I have a boyfriend." "I'm not looking for anything." "Let's not." None of them is an invitation to negotiate. A "maybe" is a no until it becomes a clear yes, and silence is a no.

The rule is not complicated: a yes has to be clear, sober enough to mean it, and given for this specific thing. A yes to a drink is not a yes to a kiss, and a yes last Friday is not a yes tonight. If you are not sure, you ask — and asking does not ruin the mood; it is the mood.

When you are the one saying no, two words work: "No, thanks." You do not owe a reason, an excuse, or a softer version. When you hear no, one sentence ends it: "No problem — have a good night." Then you leave. That is the whole skill, and it is the one this lesson cares about most.

Three lines you might say

Ask, decline, accept

Write three lines: one asking somebody out (with a day and a place), one declining an invitation without an excuse, and one accepting with enthusiasm.

Model answer

1. Do you want to get a coffee on Saturday, around eleven?

2. I'm flattered, but no — I'm not looking for anything right now.

3. Yes, I'd love that. Saturday at eleven works.

All three are short, none of them is rude, and none of them needs a paragraph. The date and the place in line 1 do the same job as the enthusiasm in line 3: they make the answer easy to give.
