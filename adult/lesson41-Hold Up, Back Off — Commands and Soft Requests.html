Hold Up, Back Off — Commands and Soft Requests

One verb, no subject, and a volume knob from "sit down" to "would you mind".

One verb, no subject

The command

The missing "you" is the point: it points the sentence straight at a person

To tell somebody what to do in English you drop the subject and start with the verb: Sit down. Wait. Come here. Give me a sec. That is the whole imperative — no you, no auxiliary, no ending. If you add the subject, the sentence stops being an order: "You sit down" is a comment or a joke, not an instruction.

O português também faz isso ("senta", "espera"), então a forma é barata. O que é caro é o tom: em inglês o imperativo puro é mais seco do que em português, e dito a um estranho soa como ordem de patrão.

That is why the volume dial exists, and it has five positions. Sit down. is an order, fine with a friend, rude with a customer. Please sit down. adds manners but not much softness. Can you sit down? is a request. Could you sit down? is the polite default with strangers. Would you mind sitting down? is the softest form English has — and note the -ing: mind takes sitting, never to sit.

The negative takes don't: Don't touch that. Don't do that. And on signs, the shortest form drops even the verb: No smoking. No entry.

The same request, five volumes

Could you sit down? polite, safe default anybody
Would you mind sitting down? the softest, almost apologetic a customer, a stranger, a boss
Add just or a sec and any of the last three get softer: "Could you just wait a sec?"

Hold up, back off

The seven you need in a crowded room

Each one stops something, and each has a place

  • hold up — stop for a second, I have something to say: "Hold up — knock that shi- off, calm down."
  • knock it off — stop doing that, said to somebody being annoying.
  • give me a sec — one moment, I am coming: "Give me a sec, I'm on the phone."
  • step back — physically move away, said when somebody is too close.
  • back off — stop pushing, physically or in an argument.
  • leave it / drop it — stop talking about that subject, permanently.
  • calm down — the riskiest of the seven: it is the one that starts fights instead of ending them.

"Step the f--- back, back off, leave it, drop it." Four commands in one breath — that is not politeness, that is a wall being built, and in a bar it is the last sentence before something happens. The polite versions of the same four exist: "Could you give us some space?" "Let's drop it for tonight."

Softeners, and when not to use them

Two small words that keep a request from sounding like an order

Four softeners do most of the work: just ("Could you just wait?"), a sec / a minute ("Give me a sec"), if you don't mind, and when you get a chance. They cost nothing and they change everything: "Send me the file" is an order; "Would you mind giving me a sec? Could you?" is a request. And when a bare command is too much, the soft version still keeps the edge: "Don't do that — just wait a f---in' minute."

And when not to soften: in an emergency, in a fire, when somebody is about to touch something dangerous. There, the bare command is correct English — "Stop. Step back. Now." A softener at the wrong moment is not polite, it is slow.

"Would you mind...?" leva sempre -ing: "Would you mind waiting?" Nunca "would you mind to wait".

Three levels, one request

Ask for the same thing three times

Pick one request you make this week — a file from a coworker, the music turned down, a seat — and write it three ways: to a close friend, to a coworker, and to a stranger or a customer.

Model answer

1. Friend: Turn that shi- down, I'm trying to sleep.

2. Coworker: Could you send me the file when you get a chance?

3. Stranger: Would you mind letting me get by, please?

Same request, three rooms. The words that change are the volume knob, not the meaning — and if you used line 1 in room 3, you would not be rude, you would be starting a fight.
