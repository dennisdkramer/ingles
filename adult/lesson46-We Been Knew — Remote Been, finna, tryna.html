We Been Knew — Remote Been, finna, tryna

The markers that carry time and attitude: stressed BEEN, finna, tryna — and the line between loving a dialect and wearing it.

Stressed BEEN

BEEN with the stress on it changes the time

Not "has been" — "started long ago and it is not news"

Lesson 39 gave you plain been. This lesson gives you the loaded one. When BEEN is stressed — louder, longer, at the front — it means the thing happened a long time ago and you are annoyed that it is being presented as new: "We been knew, deadass — y'all be late every f---in' time." We knew that long before you said it, and your lateness is a habit, not an event.

The stressed form does not need have. Standard English would say "we have known for ages"; the AAVE line does the same work with one word and no auxiliary, and the tense is not ambiguous to anybody in the room.

Two more uses you will hear. "I been told you" — I told you long ago, and I am tired of repeating it. "She been on that" — she has been doing that for a while, and it is not new. In all three, the message is the same two pieces: remote time plus you are late to the information.

The same fact, two grammars

Stressed BEEN and what it replaces
AAVE Standard English The attitude attached
We been knew. We have known for a long time. you are telling me old news
I been told you. I told you a long time ago. I am tired of saying it
She been on it. She has been doing it for a while. nothing new here
They been gone. They left a long time ago. you missed them, and that is on you
The stress is the grammar. Without it, been is just a past participle; with it, the time moves a long way back and the speaker's patience moves forward.

finna and tryna

Two reductions, one immediacy

finna is closer than going to; tryna is the effort, not the plan

finna is fixing to, and it means about to, right now: "I'm finna go, tryna see what's up; this shi-'s dead." I am leaving in a minute, I am trying to find out what is happening, and this party is over. Three ideas, three markers, no wasted word.

tryna is trying to, and it adds effort or intention: "I'm tryna see what's up" is not "I will find out" — it is "I am making an attempt". The negative form is worth knowing: "I ain't finna do that" — I am not about to do that, said with a laugh or with a wall.

"Finna" não é "vou" no sentido de plano marcado. É agora, já, no próximo minuto. "I'm gonna go tomorrow" é plano; "I'm finna go" é levantar da cadeira.

Seven words that keep a chat alive

Short answers that carry a whole agreement

  • tryna see — trying to find out: "I'm tryna see what's up."
  • on me — my fault, my responsibility: "Say less, that's on me."
  • deadass — seriously, no joke: "Deadass, I'm not going."
  • period — end of discussion, that is final: "That's the rule, period."
  • say less — understood, no need to explain: "Say less, I'm on my way."
  • word — I agree, or really?: "Word, facts — she be on it."
  • facts — that is true, agreed: "Facts, no cap."

"Word, facts — she be on it." Three items, one agreement, and the habitual be from lesson 39 inside it. This is what fluency looks like in a group chat: no full sentences, no wasted letters.

Appreciation, not a costume

Whose grammar it is

You can love a dialect without wearing it

This lesson has been teaching you to understand African American English, because it is the engine behind most of the music, the memes and the slang you already consume. Understanding is not the same as performing.

Appreciation sounds like: knowing that finna, stressed BEEN and habitual be are grammar from Black American speech and not "internet slang"; crediting it when you explain it; using it only in rooms where the people around you speak it. Appropriation sounds like: using it as a costume to sound tough in a meeting, putting it in an ad campaign, imitating the accent for a laugh, or correcting the speakers themselves.

The test is simple and it never fails: would you say this line in front of the people it comes from? If the answer is no, you have your answer. If the answer is yes, say it in your normal voice and do not perform it.

  4. "I'm tryna see what's up." → I'm to find out what's happening.
