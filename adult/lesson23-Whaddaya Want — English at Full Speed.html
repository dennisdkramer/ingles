Whaddaya Want — English at Full Speed

You know the words. Then you get to a bar in Newark, somebody leans in and says something, and you understand none of it.

Nothing is wrong with your ears. The English you were taught is the careful version: every word separate, every vowel full, every letter pronounced. Nobody talks like that — not your friend, not the bartender, not the song you have played forty times.

At speed, words crash into each other. Four things happen, they happen in every American accent you will meet, and they happen in nearly every sentence. This page teaches the four, then the ten reductions you will say yourself. Whaddaya want is not a different word. It is what do you want with the speed left in.

1. The four ways a phrase changes

A. What happens between the words

Four mechanisms cover almost everything you will fail to hear tonight.

Linking — the words hold handsa word ending in a consonant hands it to a word starting with a vowelturn it off → tur-ni-toff

None of this is sloppy English. It is the shape the language takes at speed, and it is the version in songs, in movies, and in your friend's mouth. The written form never changes — you will still write could have and what do you want every time.

2. Weak forms: the little words go quiet

A. The small words lose their vowel

Say the row as written, then hear what it turns into at speed.

English gives full weight to the words that carry meaning and almost none to the words that hold the sentence together. and, for, to, can, of, you — at speed their vowel collapses into one lazy sound, the same sound every time. That is why a sentence you can read perfectly comes at you as a blur: the blur is the furniture, and the furniture is most of the sentence.

3. Linking: the words hold hands

A. A consonant walks over to the next word

English will not leave a vowel standing there alone if the word before can lend it a consonant.

The rule is one line: a word that ends in a consonant hands that consonant to a word that starts with a vowel. That is how turn it off becomes one long thing with three beats, and why an apple sounds like a napple — nobody says an as a separate word.

Two consonants of the same kind do not repeat at speed: big game holds the g once, this stuff holds the s once, and the ear still hears two words.

4. Elision: a sound falls out

A. The t and the d that never arrive

Put a t or a d between two other consonants and it disappears at speed.

The tongue has no time to stop twice in a row, so the middle stop is dropped: next day loses its t, hand me loses its d, and old man loses the d in the middle of the phrase. Nothing is missing from the meaning — everybody in the room still hears next day.

5. Assimilation: two sounds become a third

A. t + y is ch, d + y is j

This is the mechanism behind half the words you cannot catch.

When a word ends in t or d and the next word starts with y, the two sounds meet somewhere in the middle: t + y comes out as the ch in "cheese", and d + y comes out as the j in "jam". That is the whole mechanism, and it is why what are you arrives as one word instead of three.

6. The ten you will say yourself

A. Ten reductions, and what each one used to be

Every one of them is two or three ordinary words that met at speed. None of them is a new word.

coulda, shoulda and woulda are could have, should have and would have. Never "of". People write "could of" because that is what it sounds like, and it is wrong every single time — the written word is have. And whatcha is not only "what are you": whatcha want is what do you want, the same shortcut with the other verb behind it.
Nothing here is rude. Gimme and lemme are the normal spoken form between friends, in a bar, with family. In an interview you say "give me a moment" because the room is slow on purpose, and the same person who says c'mon at a bar says "come on in" to a client.

7. Friday night, at full speed

A. The conversation as it happens

Everything the two of them say is written the way it sounds. Read it once slow, then once at the speed it was said.

Ray: Yo. Whatcha doing?

You: Nothin'. Just chillin'.

Ray: Whaddaya want — bar or the diner?

You: Gimme a sec, lemme put a shirt on.

Ray: C'mon, man. Betcha they're already there.

You: Dunno, bro. Shoulda called me an hour ago.

Ray: Coulda called you, woulda called you — you never pick up.

Seven lines, all ten reductions, and not one word of it is slang. Every single one is ordinary English wearing the speed it was spoken at. Select any line and hear it read out — then say it back yourself at the same speed, because the speed is the point.
