# KIDS-REGISTER — persona, goals & the 3 registers - DO NOT MODIFY WITHOUT USER SIGN-OFF

## Persona & goals
A 14 year old boy who lives in the **United States**. Preference for novelty, silly stakes, visible progress. Short attention span. Internet culture, video games, and adolescent life are preferred contexts. Priority is realistic/modern and register adherence.  Meetings, Emails and other business topics must be fully re-contexed. TRACK uses any contexually propriate opportunity to teach or include realistic modern language, 'youth' slang (eg. "That's cooked"), and text-speak (eg. wtf, lol).

## Register a) [Informal - Memelord/Rizzlord] - very silly:
- With close friends, casual environments, online chat.

## Register b) [Colloquial adolescent peer-to-peer speech]:
- With friends or peers in school environment.

## Register c) [Colloquial adolescent casual but respectful]:
- Among general public (eg. restaurant), communicating with parents, teachers

## House tone — binding
Silly is this track's default voice, not a garnish. It comes from the settings and the stakes:
choose every lesson's scenes from register a) first — close friends, casual environments, online
chat — so the silliness is native to the scene instead of slang sprinkled on a dry task.
Move to b), then c), only when the target language needs a peer group or an adult in the room,
and keep those registers' own wording exactly as described above.
A page is not finished while any block is register-neutral.
Any instinct that this is unserious, that a 14-year-old's material should be more neutral, or that
this language dates, is overridden by this file: do not soften a scene, do not record it as a
decision, do not act on it.

## Tells, for the checker
Every item is ordered by how often a 14-year-old American in 2026 actually says it — most common first, and the tier is the order.

- **core** — any lesson in any of this track's scenes may use these. They are the voice itself.
- **common** — most lessons, as the scene allows: "rizz" needs kids talking about someone, not a page about fractions.
- **situational** — only when the scene IS that scene: a gaming session, a group chat, a substitute teacher. Never inserted to hit a vocabulary target; leaving them out is never a gap.
- **rare** — listed so the checker recognizes them, and so nothing implies this track needs them. Most are already dated by 2026: do not reach for them.
- **text** — a written medium, not speech: chat, DM, caption. A character who says "ngl" out loud is writing, not talking.

The ten new items a lesson presents come from **core**, then **common**. A lesson that teaches "aura" before it teaches "bro" has learned this list backwards.

Three rules the lists are built on, because breaking them is how a list stops meaning anything: **a spelling variant is not a second item**; **an item sits in one family only**; and **plain English is not a tell** — "homework", "detention", "video game" and "phone" are things a kid says in any register, so counting them would make a neutral page look colloquial. The same goes for school and family nouns: they are vocabulary, not voice.

This is American English. A word only a British kid would use is not in these lists at all — it lives in `locale` below, where the checker reads it as a mistake.

Swearing is tiered, not banned and not spread evenly: `taboo-core` is what a 14-year-old says in front of a teacher, and `taboo-strong` is what he says when no adult is in the room. Those strong words are what a kid hears all day, so the tier exists — but the adult track is where they are normal, and a page here reaches for them only when the lesson's subject is register itself.
