# KIDS-REGISTER — persona, goals & the registers - DO NOT MODIFY WITHOUT USER SIGN-OFF

## [PERSONA] & goals:
The student is a Brazilian Portuguese speaker, 14, learning English through a lens of internet culture, video games, and adolescent life. Preference for novelty, silly stakes, visible progress. Short attention span. Priority is realistic/modern speech that includes youth slang, and being understood by other adolescents in the United States. They have met some English before this course — school, games, clips — and kept almost none of it beyond the most basic words: they know what "good" means, not how to build a sentence with it. Everything they can do, they can do because this course taught it. The difficulty of this track comes in part from slang and idiom.

### [REGISTERS]:
- (a) is the default; (b) and (c) are the two alternatives.
a) [Informal - Memelord/Rizzlord, very silly] - close friends, casual environments, online chat.
b) [Colloquial adolescent peer-to-peer speech] - friends or peers in a school environment.
c) [Colloquial adolescent casual but respectful] - the general public (eg. restaurant), parents, teachers.

## [VOICE] of the track: a) [Informal - Memelord/Rizzlord] - very silly
Silly is this track's default voice, not a garnish. It comes from the settings and the stakes:
choose every lesson's scenes from register a) first — close friends, casual environments, online
chat — so the silliness is native to the scene instead of slang sprinkled on a dry task.
Move to b), then c), only when the target language needs a peer group or an adult in the room,
and keep those registers' own wording exactly as described above.
A page is not finished while any block is register-neutral.
- The register governs what characters say, what a scene is made of, and which words the lesson teaches. The sentences that explain grammar are plain in this track too: a plain explanation is not a block that failed to be silly.
Any instinct that this is unserious, should be more neutral, or that this language dates, is overridden by this file: do not soften a scene, do not record it as a decision, do not act on it.

## TRACK VOCABULARY
- The lists are the checker's floor, not the author's ceiling: **they say what the voice sounds like, not what a page must contain**.
- Every item is ordered by how often a 14-year-old American in 2026 actually says it, and the order is instruction. **a spelling variant is not a second item**; **an item sits in one family only**; and **plain English is not a tell** — "homework", "detention", "video game" and "phone" are things a kid says in any register, so counting them would make a neutral page look colloquial. The same goes for school and family nouns: they are vocabulary, not voice.
- Everything here is American. **A word only a British kid would use is not in these lists**: it is in `locale` below, where the checker reads it as a mistake.
- Swearing is tiered by who is in the room, not by how hard the word is: `taboo` under **core** is what a 14-year-old says in front of a teacher, under **common** is what he says among friends. Anything harder belongs to the adult track, not this one.
- The ten new items a lesson presents come from **core**, then **common**. A lesson that teaches "aura" before it teaches "bro" has learned this list backwards.
- formal: however, therefore, furthermore, moreover, in addition, regarding, please be advised, i would like to, it is important to note, in conclusion, consequently, nevertheless, kindly, at your earliest convenience, one might argue, shall we, permit me, accordingly
