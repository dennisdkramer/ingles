Fast English: how it really sounds

1. Nobody says every letter

Written down, English is a wall of separate little words. Said out loud, at the speed a person actually talks, half of those little words disappear into the one next to them. You have been hearing the short version your whole life on streams and in songs. This lesson gives you the spellings.

A. Two versions of every sentence

Here is a sentence with every word in it, and the same sentence the way a person says it:

Written: What are you trying to do?
Spoken: Whatcha tryna do?

Nothing was lost. The meaning words — try, do — are still there, loud and clear. What vanished is the scaffolding: what are you became whatcha, trying to became tryna. The grammar words are the ones that get squashed, because the person listening does not need them at full volume to understand you.

That is not laziness and it is not bad English. It is what fluency sounds like from the inside. But there is a rule you do not break: speak short, write long. In a school essay you write I am going to, not I'm gonna. In a voice note to your friend, the short one is the normal one.

Two things happen when English speeds up, and this lesson takes them one at a time. Words merge (want to → wanna) and sounds slide (turn it off sounds like one long word). Learn both and a stream stops sounding like a blur.

3. A voice note, written how it is said

A. Group voice note, 00:12, three friends and a lost controller

GUH: Yo, I gotta ask — has anyone seen my controller?

TEO: Dunno, man. Lemme check under the sofa.

MEG: It is kinda under the sofa, but also sorta not.

GUH: Gimme that phone, I will look myself.

TEO: Do you wanna borrow mine instead? It ain't great but it works.

GUH: I am tryna finish the season tonight, so yes, please.

NOTE: Read it again out loud at normal speed. Every bold word is one of the nine, and not one of them would survive in a school essay — yet this is exactly how the three of them talk.

4. kinda, sorta, and the soft edges

A. Two words that make a judgement quieter

kinda and sorta mean the same thing: kind of, sort of — a little, not exactly. Their real job is social. They take an opinion that could start an argument and turn it into something safe: it is kinda boring is a much easier thing to say to your friend than it is boring.

In a group chat you will see them everywhere as a cushion. Use them when you are not sure, or when you are sure but do not want to say it flatly. Do not use them in an essay, where a clear opinion is the whole point.

1. A streamer says "that game is kinda mid". What is he saying?
2. "It was sorta funny" — how funny was it?
3. Why does a person say "kinda boring" instead of "boring"?
4. Which of these belongs in a group chat, not in an essay?

5. The consonant that moves

A. When a word ends in a consonant and the next starts with a vowel

English does not stop between words. If a word ends in a consonant and the next begins with a vowel, the consonant slides across and becomes the start of that vowel.

an apple stops sounding like an · apple and becomes a-napple.
turn it off becomes tur-ni-toff — one long word.
gimme it becomes gim-mi-it.
lemme ask becomes lem-mask.

That is why a sentence you can read perfectly becomes a blur when someone says it at speed: the gaps you were expecting are not there. You have to hear the joins, not the words.

6. The word "to" is not really there

A. The smallest word in the sentence, at the smallest volume

to is a grammar word. It carries no meaning of its own, so at speed it loses its vowel and joins the word in front of it: want to becomes wanna, got to becomes gotta, going to becomes gonna, have to becomes hafta.

This is the same rule as section 5, one step further: first the gap closes, then the vowel in the small word gives up. Nothing about the meaning changes — which is why a student who has only read English cannot hear it, and a student who can hear it suddenly understands the whole stream.

One warning that matters for marks: hafta and gonna and gotta are speaking forms. In any piece of writing for school they go back to have to, going to, have got to.

1. The coach shouts "we hafta go". Which two words are hidden in hafta?
2. In "gotta", what happened to the word "to"?
3. Which one goes in a piece of writing for school?
4. Why does a stream sound like a blur to a student who reads English well?

8. Quiz

A. One question per word

Twelve questions, one for every word in this lesson. Each one gives you something you would want to say and asks for the fast form you would actually say it in.

1. Your friend asks what you are doing tonight. Fast, you say: "I ___ go to the game."
2. Your mum says the dishes come first. Fast, you answer: "I ___ finish my homework."
3. The film was not great and not terrible. Spoken, you say: "It was ___ boring."
4. Same idea, the other spoken word for "a bit": "It was ___ weird."
5. You have no idea where your keys are. Spoken as one word: "___ , I lost them again."
6. You want to look at your friend's screen. Spoken as one word: "___ see that."

B. The other six

7. Your friend is hogging the controller. Spoken as one word: "___ a turn."
8. Someone asks "coming?" and you answer with the most relaxed yes there is: "___ , one sec."
9. A flat one-word "no", the opposite of yeah: "___ , not doing that."
10. A short, certain "yes", sharper than yeah: "___ , that is exactly it."
11. In a song lyric, "there is not" said fast: "___ nothing wrong with it."
12. You are trying to finish a level and someone asks what you are doing. Fast: "I'm ___ finish this."
