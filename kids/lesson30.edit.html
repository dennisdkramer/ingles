The look family: one verb, ten directions

1. One verb, ten directions

Last lesson get did four jobs with four particles. look does ten — and every one of them is a place your eyes, or your attention, can go. Same root, new direction, new verb. You are not learning ten verbs. You are learning one verb and ten directions.

A. The root and its ten particles

Nothing about look ever changes. The particle is the entire new word: learn the particle and you have learnt the verb.

look at look for look up look over look after look out look forward to look around look into look away

Nine of those are directions. The tenth, look after, is the odd one, and it gets its own section later.

B. How to guess a direction you have never met

Ask what the particle does everywhere else you have seen it. up finishes things. out means leaving or outside. around goes in a circle. into goes inside something. over moves across the top of something.

Then say the sentence out loud. If it sounds like something a real person would actually say in a chat, you have probably got it right. If it sounds like a textbook, you probably have not.

That is the whole method, and it works on the next root too: run out, turn up, check over. The particles are the vocabulary; the root is only the topic.

3. The particle that splits for pronouns

A. Two of them split, and only for pronouns

look up and look over are the two that move. When the object is a pronoun — it, them, him, her, me — the pronoun goes between the verb and the particle: look it up. Never look up it. When the object is a full noun you get a choice: look the word up and look up the word are both fine.

The other eight do not split. The pronoun sits at the end and the pair stays welded together: look after him, look at it, look for them, look forward to it. Nothing goes in the middle — not look him after, not look it at.

1. The word was new to me, so I looked up before I said it.
2. My notes were chaos, so she looked over before the test.
3. Look after while I find his shoes.
4. I am looking forward to more than to the test.

B. You have done this before, with get

Same rule, different verb: when the object is a pronoun, the pronoun goes between the verb and the particle.

4. look after is care, not looking

A. The one that is not a direction

look after is not where your eyes go. It is the work of keeping a person or a thing alive, fed and out of trouble. If you look after your brother, you might not be looking at him at all — you might be looking for his other shoe.

And look over is never that. You look over homework, a form, a list: you move your eyes across the whole thing and check it. One is work done to a person, the other is work done to a page. Swap them and it is instantly strange.

5. The one-word version you never use

A. One column is how you talk, the other is how a document talks

Each of these six has a one-word version that a document would pick instead: you look at something, a worksheet asks you to observe it. The one-word version is longer and sounds like homework, which is exactly where it lives — a school sheet, a news article, a job advert, a game patch note. Recognise it everywhere it turns up. Do not start using it in a group chat unless you enjoy being roasted.
