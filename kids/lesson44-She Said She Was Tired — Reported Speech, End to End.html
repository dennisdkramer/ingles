She Said She Was Tired — Reported Speech, End to End

#drama, 9:14 p.m.

  1. Kikoyo mel said she's not coming tonight bc her mom found out about the group chat 💀
  2. Meli said i MIGHT not come IF my mom found out. she has not found out. i'm literally doing my hair rn
  3. Kikowait so you're coming
  4. Melbro. i was never NOT coming
  5. Nandokiko please never relay anything to anyone ever again 😭 that's an L of a message

Kiko moved the tense, kept the person, and still started a war: he turned might into isn't, deleted the if, and dropped the reason. Relaying is not repeating. Four systems run at once, and any one of them can flip what somebody meant.

The four systems, and the one that gets you caught

1. The reporting verb sets the frame — say, tell, ask — and each frame decides what may follow it. 2. The backshift moves the message one step into the past behind the report. 3. Re-centering moves every word that measured itself from the speaker's mouth: I, my, here, tonight. 4. The reporting verb's own flavour — claim, admit, insist — decides what the report says about whether you believed her.

Systems 1 and 2 are mechanical, and a student who has had forty lessons can already do them. System 3 is where the meaning dies, because the words are small and unstressed and nobody notices them moving wrong. System 4 is where the fight starts, because it is the only one that is not about grammar at all: she said is neutral, she claimed calls her a liar, she admitted says she was guilty.

Why it matters IRL

Being the messenger between two friends is a daily job, and every relay is a small court case: what she said, what you say she said, and what the group chat decides to believe.

The person who relays badly is the person who gets blamed for the message. That is the whole stakes of this lesson — plus one exam next lesson, where a story, a plan and a ranking all get relayed.

The frame does the first half of the work

Three frames, three shapes

A report opens with a verb, and that verb decides the shape of everything after it. The shapes are not interchangeable: swap the frame and the sentence breaks, not just the meaning.

framewhat followshow it sounds in #dramait cannot take

Tell also owns a short list of fixed objects that need no person: the truth, a lie, a story, a joke, and the difference between two things. That is a list to memorise, not a rule to reason from — and it is why tell me the truth and say the truth are not the same sentence.

The verb you choose is a verdict

Say and tell report a message and stop there. Swap in another verb and the report starts judging her:

claim — I don’t believe you
admit — you did it
insist — you wouldn’t drop it
deny — you say you didn’t
warn — there’s a consequence coming

Frames: claim / admit / insist / deny take that + clause, and deny and admit also take -ing (she denied taking it). Warn, remind and advise take a person + to.

One line, five frames, five different accusations

Her words: “I already sent it, I swear.” Not one quoted word changes. Everything the group believes does.

the reportwhat you just told everyone
She said she’d already sent it.a message was relayed. no opinion.
She told me she’d already sent it.same, and I am the one she told.
She insisted she’d already sent it.I questioned her, and she would not move.
She claimed she’d already sent it.I don’t believe a word of it.
She admitted she hadn’t sent it.she was caught, and she gave in.

The last row is the one to notice: admit pulls the polarity round. You don’t admit something you’re proud of, so admitted she’d sent it is already a strange sentence, and admitted she hadn’t is the natural one. Choose the verb carelessly and you have accused her of something she never did.

Where Portuguese sets the trap

Ela me disse que não vinha. One verb, person attached, job done. English splits the job in two and forbids each half from doing the other half’s work.

Both of these are broken, and for opposite reasons: “she said me she wasn’t coming” puts a person where say holds none, and “she told that she wasn’t coming” leaves out the person tell demands.

One repair fixes either: she told me she wasn’t coming.

The frame, marked, with two steps inside it

She told me she'd been waiting outside for twenty minutes.

Read it left to right: told is the frame, me is the recipient the arrow lands on, and everything from she onward is the clause being reported. That clause carries two steps at once — she’d been waiting has come back from I’ve been waiting, which is a tense and an aspect at the same time. Say would drop the recipient and keep the clause; ask would trade the clause for a question that has lost its question order.

Same message, a different frame

The frame is written for you; the gap takes the whole clause. More than one item also moves a pronoun or a time word, and item 2 takes -ing, not that.

Item 3 is where the meaning actually lives: cooked is the reason, and the reason is not part of the reported clause the gap asks for — but a relay that drops it is exactly the relay that starts the fight.

The backshift, and the switch that turns it off

One step back, because the words are older than the report

She spoke before you repeat her, so her verb sits one step further back than the moment she described. That is the entire backshift: distance, not doubt. It is not a comment on whether she lied, and it is not a rule about how long ago it was.

she saidyou relay, after a past verb

We’re already on the bus, we’ll be there in ten.they said they were already on the bus and they’d be there in ten

The step is mechanical, which makes it the cheap half of this lesson — and the half a student at lesson 44 is expected to do without thinking. The expensive half is knowing when the step is not taken at all.

The switch is the reporting verb

The backshift is not triggered by the message. It is triggered by the verb you report with.

She said she was tired — past report, one step back. She says she is tired — present report, and nothing moves, because the words and the report occupy the same moment.

Two cases where the step is skipped even after a past verb: a truth that cannot age (he said water boils at 100), and a fact still true now, where the present is a deliberate claim — she said she IS tired means “and she still is”.

Would, could, should, might, ought to are already past-shaped: they do not step back again. She said she would, never woulded.

Four lines, five changes, one sentence. This is what “answer in a clause” means at this level: the relay is a sentence you build, not a word you fill in.

Do not double-shift

If the original already carries a finished past with a time marker, one step is all it gets: “I saw him last night” → she said she saw him the night before is correct, and she said she had seen him is also correct but more formal. What is wrong is “had saw”.

The marker moves with the tense: last night → the night before, yesterday → the day before. A backshifted verb with an unshifted marker announces itself.

Re-centering: the world moves with the mouth

Two centres, and only one of them is you

When Duda spoke, I meant Duda, here meant the bench outside school, and tonight meant the night she was talking about. You are not Duda and you are not on that bench. Every word that measures itself from a centre has to be measured again from yours — and those words are short, unstressed and almost invisible, which is why they are the ones left behind.

They arrive in three families: person (I, my, we, you), place (here, this, these) and time (now, tonight, tomorrow, last night). A relay can carry the tense perfectly and still put words in someone’s mouth, because one of these kept pointing at the wrong mouth.

This is also where a relay gets caught. A listener usually knows roughly what was said, so a pronoun that moved wrong does not sound like a grammar mistake. It sounds like a lie.

The one no table can fix

You points at whoever was spoken to. If Duda was talking to you, it becomes me. If she was talking to Kiko, it becomes him.

Every other pronoun has one destination. This one is decided by the scene, so read who was actually in the conversation before you touch it.

And a pronoun already pointing at a third person does not move at all: if Mel brings her cousin, her is still Mel’s in your report.

Re-center the whole line

Each gap takes the complete reported clause. Most lines need three moves — person, place or time, then tense — and one of them also changes the pronoun’s case.

2. Kiko: “We’re waiting outside your building rn.” → Kiko said .

Move them in this order

1. Who is speaking now? Fix the person. 2. Where and when are we? Fix place and time. 3. Then step the verb back.

Done in that order, the sentence stays readable while you build it. Done in the other order, you shift the tense first and then lose track of whose hoodie it is.

Case follows the job: me and Bia is an object, so its relay is she and Bia as the subject — never “her and Bia are coming”, which is the mistake this section exists to catch.

A reported question stops being a question

Keep the question word, remove the question

Nobody is asking anything any more, so the machinery of asking has to go. A reported question keeps its question word, loses the inversion, and loses the helper. “Where are you rn?” becomes she asked where I was, not where was I. “Did you post the clip?” has no question word to keep, so if or whether takes its place: he asked if I had posted the clip.

askedreportedwhat went, what arrived
“Where are you rn?”she asked where I wasthe inversion; rn → then
“Did you post the clip?”he asked if I had posted the clipthe helper, replaced by if; past simple → past perfect
“Why didn’t you answer my text?”he asked why I hadn’t answered his textthe helper, the negative kept, the possessive moved
“Can you send me the link?”she asked if I could send her the linkthe modal steps back: can → could
“Are you still gonna be there at eight?”she asked whether I was still gonna be there at eightno question word, so whether; gonna → was gonna
“Who’s coming with us?”she asked who was coming with themnothing — who is the subject; us → them

The last row is the exception that keeps the rule honest: when the question word is the subject, there was never an inversion to remove, so the words cross over almost untouched. Who, what and which all do this — and this is exactly why “she asked who was coming” needs no if and no reshuffle.

The error you will make

The inversion survives. It is the most common reported-question mistake, and it survives because the sentence still looks like a question:

✗ she asked where was I
✗ she asked did I post it
✗ she asked who was I
✓ she asked where I was

Test the clause by reading it as a statement: I was outside ✓, was I outside ✗. If it still sounds like a question, it is still wrong.

if, whether, and where they stop

If and whether do the same job for a yes/no question, and whether is the one that survives in careful writing.

Only whether takes or not straight after it: she asked whether or not I was coming. “If or not” is not English.

Neither of them belongs with a question word: after where, why, who there is nothing to replace, so adding if is a second error on top of the inversion.

Report the question, as a clause

The gap takes the whole reported question — starting with the question word, or with if / whether when there was none.

1. “Where are you rn?” → She asked .
2. “Did you post the clip?” → He asked .
3. “Who’s coming with us?” → She asked .
4. “Why didn’t you answer my text, bro?” → He asked .
5. “Can you send me the link?” → She asked .
6. “Are you still gonna be there at eight?” → He asked .

Every line moves at least two things. Item 3 moves none of the word order and only the pronoun: who was already the subject, so the reported clause is the original with us turned into them.

Orders, favours, and who had the power

No clause survives, so the verb lands on to

An order is not a statement, so there is no clause to report. English keeps the person and puts the verb on to: “Send me the clip.” becomes she told me to send her the clip. The negative puts not in front of to: “Don’t post that.” becomes he told me not to post it.

she saidreported with the frame you choosewhat it claims
“Send me the clip.”she told me to send her the clipan order
“Can you grab my hoodie on the way out?”she asked me to grab her hoodiea favour
“Don’t post that, I’m serious.”he told me not to post itan order, negated
“Whatever you do, don’t tell Mel.”he warned me not to tell Mela consequence is attached
“Remember to bring the cable.”she reminded me to bring the cableI already knew, and I would have forgotten
“You should talk to him.”she advised me to talk to himan opinion, not an order

Rows one and two are the same content with a different frame, and they are not the same event: tell claims power, ask claims a favour, warn adds a threat, advise adds an opinion. A relay can insult someone without changing a single quoted word — which is the whole second half of this lesson.

Say cannot do this at all

Say has no person to hold, so it cannot report an order. “She said me to go” is two errors stacked: the person say refuses, and a clause an order does not have.

Repair both at once: she told me to go. And never “tell me don’t” — the negative lives on to, not in a second clause.

“She said to me, ‘go’” is not a report at all: that is a direct quote with a reporting frame around it, and the words inside stay hers.

Relay it as an order, a favour, or a warning

The reporting verb is given in each line; the gap takes the whole to-phrase. Two items also move a time word inside the phrase.

1. “Send me the clip before you go to bed.” → She told me .
2. “Can you grab my hoodie from the locker room?” → She asked me .
3. “Don’t tag me in that, my mom follows me.” → He told me .
4. “Whatever you do, don’t reply to her.” → He warned me .
5. “Remember to bring the cable tomorrow.” → She reminded me .
6. “You should talk to Kiko before it gets weird.” → She advised me .

Two questions that look alike

She asked me to wait — a request. The thing asked for is the to-phrase.

She asked me if I was waiting — a question about the world. The thing asked is a clause with no inversion.

Same three opening words, two different machines underneath. Read what comes after the person: a to-phrase means she wanted an action, a clause means she wanted information.

And choose between tell, ask, warn and advise by what the scene actually was — who had the power, whether a consequence was named, whether it was advice. The report is a claim about the room.

Spill the tea is what the messenger is asked for; throw shade is what the messenger is accused of carrying. Both arrive with somebody else’s words inside them, which is why this vocabulary belongs in this lesson and not in a list at the back.

Three that Portuguese will mislead

argument is not argumento. An argumento is a reason in a debate; an argument is usually a fight with words. The debate sense exists — a strong argument — but the fight is what a messenger meets.

apology is not apologia. Apologia is a defence of an idea or a person; an apology is what you say when you were wrong.

gossip is uncountable talk and the person who talks: she’s such a gossip. One story is a piece of gossip, or a rumor.

Forms to watch

shout → shouted, whisper → whispered, reply → replied, throw → threw. The sentence decides the tense, so the word has to follow it.

spill the tea and throw shade are fixed: the article never changes and the verb does. She threw shade, never “she threw a shade”.

The whole relay

Five relays, one paragraph

Read this the way a listener hears it — as a report of a report of a report.

“Duda told me she wasn’t coming to Léo’s thing bc her mom had found out about the group chat, except she never said that. What she actually said was that she might not come if her mom found out, and her mom hadn’t found out yet. I said I’d meet her outside at eight anyway, and she said she’d be there unless her mom got home early. Then Kiko asked me where I was, and I told him I was on my way, which was a lie bc I was still at home. He said Duda had told him a completely different story. I asked him what she’d actually said, and he said she’d told him I was the one who wasn’t coming.”

Nothing in that paragraph uses a rare word. Every sentence is a decision, and each one could have been relayed wrongly in a dozen ways — which is the whole point: the difficulty is never the vocabulary, it is holding four systems in your head while somebody is waiting for an answer.

What moved, and what refused to

Tense and aspect: might not come → might not come (no further step available), had found out → hadn’t found out (already past perfect), I’ll meet → I’d meet.

Person: I → she, my mom → her mom, you → me in the question that came back.

Time: tomorrow → the next day, at eight → unchanged, because a clock time points at nothing.

Frame: said → asked where the original was a question; told retained where the person was named.

Why backwards works

Reversing a report is the fastest check there is: if you cannot name the sentence that was actually said, you have not understood the report.

It also shows you the three shapes in five lines — clause after say, person then clause after tell, to-phrase for an order, and a question that lost its question order after ask.

Row five is a report of a report: she was throwing shade has already stepped back once, so relaying it again does not move it twice.

Three checks, in order

1. Does the clause read like a statement? I was outside ✓ · was I outside ✗.

2. Is a helper left over? do, does, did and can do not survive as helpers; the verb carries the tense alone — told, not did tell.

3. Is the subject the thing that acts? If the question word was the subject (who told you), there was nothing to invert and nothing to add.

Item 5 is the one that hides a modal: may becomes might, and have posted stays put behind it.

Speech has its own relay

She was like…

When people talk, they mostly stop reporting and start performing. Instead of she said she was tired, they say she was like, “I’m tired.” The quoting verb is be like, nothing backshifts, and the words are played in the present — the face and the voice do the work the past tense would otherwise have to do.

It is correct, it is everywhere in this register, and it has rules of its own: it belongs to speech and to chat, never to a message to a teacher, a form or a report. In careful writing, the backshift is still the default.

Variants: she goes…, she’s all… — older, or regional, and dated in most of the US by now. Use was like.

The difference in one line

Nothing inside a quote moves, because a quote is a replay. The only thing that still agrees is the quoting verb: she was, they were.

In writing, the frame is past, so everything behind it steps back — said → was, told → had, asked → couldn’t.

Switching between the two is a register decision, not a grammar one. The same afternoon is a was like story for the group chat and a backshifted report for the teacher, and both are correct in their own room.

Two things a gap cannot test

1. Production. Send this to the group chat as one careful message, three sentences, no quotes, keeping every reason: “I’m not coming on Saturday, my mom’s still on my case about the last one, and I’ve got that thing with my dad anyway. Mel said she’d cover for me but I’m not doing that to her. Don’t say anything yet, I’ll tell them myself.”

2. The verdict. Kiko says: “You’d better not put that in the group chat.” Three relays are possible: he told me not to, he asked me not to, he warned me not to. Choose the one that is honest about the room, and write the sentence.

How to mark yourself

A relay is right when all four systems survive it. Read your own writing back and check them one at a time:

Frame — could the person stand where you put them?
Verb — did it step back once, and only once?
Person — does every pronoun still point at the same human?
Time — did any word move that did not need to?

A missing answer is cheaper than a moved pronoun: if you are unsure, drop the tense first and fix the person first.
