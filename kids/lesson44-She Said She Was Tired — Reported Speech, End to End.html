She Said She Was Tired — Reported Speech, End to End

Group chat, 9:14 p.m.

  1. KikoDuda said she was coming.
  2. DudaI said I’d come after my essay.
  3. Kiko…so she’s not coming.
  4. DudaI SAID I’D COME.
  5. MelHe told me “probably”.
  6. DudaI never said probably.
  7. KikoNo way. My bad.

Two different things broke in that chat. Kiko moved the tense correctly and still lost the message, because he let the time and the commitment move with it. Mel added a word nobody said — which is the other way a relay fails: not a broken rule, a broken record.

Reported speech is a translation between two mouths, and four systems run at once. The reporting verb fixes the frame. The backshift moves the message one step into the past. The pronouns and the place-and-time words have to be re-pointed at whoever is speaking now. And the reporting verb's own meaning decides whether you delivered an order or a favour. This lesson is those four, separately, and then in one sentence.

The reporting verb fixes the frame

Three frames, three shapes

Every report opens with a verb that decides what may follow it, and the shapes are not interchangeable. Say takes a clause and refuses a person: she said she was late, never she said me. Tell takes a person and then the clause: she told me she was late. Ask takes a clause too, but a question with its question shape removed: she asked where I was.

Tell also owns four fixed objects that need no person at all: tell the truth, tell a lie, tell a story, tell a joke. That is a short list to memorise, not a rule to reason from.

Where Portuguese sets the trap

Portuguese puts the person in front of one verb that does both jobs: ela me disse. English splits the job in two, and the split is the difficulty.

So the same thought fails twice. “She said me she was tired” puts the person where say cannot hold it; “She told that she was tired” leaves out the person tell requires. Both are repaired the same way: she told me she was tired.

The frame, marked

He told me he'd be late.

The tagger puts told on the verb, me in the recipient slot with the arrow landing on it, and he’d be late as the clause that follows. Read the shape left to right — verb, recipient, clause. Say keeps the clause and drops the recipient; ask replaces the clause with a question that has lost its question order.

Why the person is not optional

Tell names who was told, so a relay built on tell always carries its audience. Say does not, which is why a relay built only on say leaves the listener asking said to who?

When the person has to appear with say, English gives you the preposition and nothing else: she said it to me.

The backshift, and the switch that turns it off

One step back, because the words are older than the report

The message was spoken before you repeat it, so its verb sits one step further back than the moment it describes. That is the whole of the backshift: distance, not doubt, and not a comment on whether the thing is true.

spokenreported after a past verb
I am ready.she said she was ready

I can drive.he said he could drive
I don’t know.he said he didn’t know
I have your keys.she said she had my keys
I want to leave.he said he wanted to leave

The step is mechanical, which makes it the easy half of this lesson. The hard half is knowing when the step is not taken at all.

The switch is the reporting verb

The backshift is not triggered by the message. It is triggered by the verb you report with.

She said she was tired — past report, one step back. She says she is tired — present report, and nothing moves, because the words and the report sit in the same time.

Same person, same message, two tenses, two meanings. The reporting verb is the switch, and it is the first thing to look at in any relay.

Reported in the past

She said she was tired.

Two pasts. said frames the report and was has stepped back from am: the whole sentence sits one step behind the words. This is the default in writing.

Reported in the present

She says she is tired.

says is present, so is stays where it was. The same sentence with one tense changed at the front — which is the switch, in five words.

Before you fill them

Items 1–8 begin with she, he or they, never with the I inside the quote. Item 9 begins with I, because the person he told was you.

Item 8 is the switch. The reporting verb is present, so nothing steps back; the tense you were about to change is already correct.

Re-centering: the speaker moves, so the world moves

Two centers, and only one of them is you

When Duda spoke, I meant Duda, here meant where she was standing, and today meant the day she was speaking. You are not Duda and you are not standing there. Every word that measures itself from a center has to be measured again from yours — and those words are small, unstressed, and very easy to leave behind.

They come in three families: person (I, my, we, you), place (here, this, these) and time (now, today, tomorrow, last night). A relay can carry the tense perfectly and still say something nobody said, because one of these kept pointing at the wrong mouth.

This is also where a relay gets caught. The listener often knows what was said, so a pronoun that moved wrong does not sound like a mistake. It sounds like a lie.

The one no table can fix

You points at whoever was spoken to. If Duda told you, it becomes me; if she told Kiko, it becomes him.

Every other pronoun has one destination. This one is decided by the scene, so read who was in the conversation before you touch it.

Each gap is the complete reported clause. Most lines need three moves: the verb, the person, and one word of time or place.

Three moves, in this order

Verb one step back. Person to the new speaker. Time or place to the new moment. Done in that order, the line stays readable.

Line 2 is the one that catches people: you was the reporter, so it becomes me, and here was Kiko’s position, so it becomes there.

A reported question stops being a question

Keep the question word, remove the question

Nobody is asking any more, so the machinery of asking has to go. A reported question keeps the question word, loses the inversion, and loses the helper: Where are you? becomes she asked where I was, not where was I. Do you like him? has no question word to keep, so if takes its place: she asked if I liked him.

askedreportedwhat went
Where are you going?she asked where I was goingthe inversion
When does it start?he asked when it startedthe helper, and the tense
Do you know him?she asked if I knew himthe helper, replaced by if
Why aren’t you answering?he asked why I wasn’t answeringthe inversion, kept in the negative
Who is calling?she asked who was callingnothing — who is the subject

The last row is the exception that keeps the rule honest: when the question word is the subject, there was never an inversion to remove, so the words cross over untouched. Who, what and which can all do this.

The error you will make

The inversion survives. It is the most common reported-question mistake, and it survives because the sentence still looks like a question:

✗ she asked where was I
✗ she asked did I like him
✓ she asked where I was

Test the clause by reading it as a statement: I was going ✓, was I going ✗.

Two checks

1. Does the clause read like a statement? I was going ✓ · was I going ✗.

2. Is a helper left over? do, does, did and can do not survive as helpers; the verb carries the past alone — started, not did start.

Line 3 needs neither check: who was already the subject, so nothing was inverted in the first place.

Orders and requests lose their clause

Tell, ask, and the infinitive

An order is not a statement, so no clause survives to be reported. English keeps the person and puts the verb on to: Spill the tea. becomes she told me to spill the tea. The negative puts not before to: Don’t tell anyone. becomes she told me not to tell anyone.

saidreportedwhat it now sounds like
Spill the tea.she told me to spill the teaan order
Spill the tea.she asked me to spill the teaa favour
Don’t tell anyone.she told me not to tell anyonean order, negated
Can you wait?she asked me to waita request

Rows one and two are the same words with a different reporting verb, and they are not the same event. Tell claims power; ask claims a favour. Choosing between them is a claim about what happened, which is why a relay can insult someone without changing a single quoted word.

Say cannot do this

Say has no person to hold, so it cannot report an order at all. “She said me to go” is two errors stacked: the person say refuses, and the clause an order does not have.

Repair both at once: she told me to go. And never “tell me don’t” — the negative lives on to, not on a second clause.

Report the order

Order or favour?

Read who had the power in the original scene. A teacher who says “Close the window” is not asking a favour, so told me to is the honest report; asked me to would make her polite and you generous.

Lines 4 and 5 move a possessive and an object pronoun as well. The order is not the only thing that travels.

Spill the tea is the request; you throw shade when the insult lands with no name in it. Both are things a messenger is asked to carry, and then blamed for carrying.

Three that Portuguese will mislead

argument is not argumento. An argumento is a reason in a debate; an argument is usually a fight with words. The debate sense exists, but the fight is what a messenger meets.

apology is not apologia. Apologia is a defence of an idea; an apology is what you say when you were wrong.

gossip is uncountable talk and also the person who talks: she’s a gossip. One story is a piece of gossip, or a rumor.

The whole relay

One message, four systems, five sentences

Read this the way a listener hears it: as a report of a report.

“Duda told me she wasn’t coming tonight, and that she’d bring my speaker the next day instead. I said I’d meet her at the mall, but she said she was waiting outside already. Then Kiko asked me where I was, and I told him I was on my way. He said Duda was telling him a completely different story — that I was the one who wasn’t coming. I asked him who was saying that, and he said she was.”

Nothing in that paragraph is advanced vocabulary — everything in it is a decision, and the same five sentences could have been relayed wrongly in a dozen different ways. That’s wild when you see it written out, and the whole system fits in one line: he said she was throwing shade at him — a past reporting verb, a continuous that stepped back, and a pronoun pointing at whoever the message was about.

What moved, and what refused to

Tense: am not coming → wasn’t coming; I’ll bring → she’d bring.

Person: I → she; your speaker → my speaker, because the speaker was always the reporter’s.

Time: tomorrow → the next day. Tonight stayed, because the words and this report happen on the same night.

Question: Where are you? → where I was — no inversion.

  4. “Don’t tell Mel yet.”
  5. “I can’t deal with the drama right now.”

Every line moves the tense and the person. Move the time wherever the calendar has changed — and report line 4 as the order it was, not as a favour.

No key: this is the production the lesson exists for. Read it back and check three things per line — verb, person, time — and then check that line 4 still sounds like Kiko telling you what to do.

Where each second move hides

Line 1 hides a possessive: you meant the reporter, so the photos come to me. Line 2 hides one too — the charger is his.

Line 3 has no tense to move, only an order and a name. Line 4 has no inversion to remove: who is the subject.

Speech has its own relay

She was like…

When people talk, they usually stop reporting and start performing. Instead of she said she was tired, they say she was like, “I’m tired.” The quoting verb is be like, the tense does not step back, and the words are performed in the present — the face and the voice do the work the backshift would otherwise do.

It is correct, it is everywhere, and it has rules of its own: it belongs to speech and to chat, never to a message to a teacher, a form or a report. In writing, the backshift is still the default.

Variants: she goes…, she’s all… — older, or regional, and dated in most of the US by now. Use was like.

Notice what the speech version keeps: the original person, the original tense, the original question order. Nothing is translated, because nothing is being reported — it is being replayed.

Two systems, one message

Each line keeps the same content in one system or the other. Read which system the line is in before you answer.

The difference in one line

Nothing backshifts inside a quote, because a quote is a replay. The only thing that still agrees is the quoting verb: she was, they were.

Lines 5 and 6 are the same content in writing: the report is past, so everything inside it steps back.
