I've Never Written a Song — have and the participle

1. Two words, two jobs

Portuguese puts the whole claim inside the verb's ending: escrevi, tenho escrito. English splits it into two words that never touch, and one of them is a word you already know from do and will. Learn which word does which job and this tense stops being a list.

have carries the person and the time; the participle carries the meaning

Look at what changes when the person changes, and what does not:

I have written a song.
She has written a song.

Only have moved: have for I, you, we, they; has for he, she, it. The word written did not move, because it is not doing the grammar — it is doing the meaning. That is the whole machine: a mode word that takes the person and the tense, and one fixed form that names the action.

So the form to memorise is not have written. It is the participle — the third column of every verb you have ever looked up, the one nobody teaches you to use.

The third column

For a regular verb the participle is the -ed form, same as the past: walk — walked — walked. For the verbs you actually use, the third column is its own word, and it is the one Portuguese speakers get wrong because it looks like the past and is not:

Two of those columns look identical — won, lost — and that is not a trap, it is luck. The trap is wrote for written, because Portuguese has one word for both and English has two.

1. I have ___ two songs this year.
2. She has ___ that game three times.
3. We have ___ the keys again.
4. They have ___ here since 2019.

2. never, and a claim that covers your whole life

Not once, from the beginning until now

I've never written a song is not a sentence about a year. It is a sentence about every year: from the day I was born to the minute I say it, the count is zero. Portuguese says the same thing — eu nunca escrevi uma música — and this is the one place the two languages agree, which is why the mistake is easy: nunca escrevi looks exactly like the English past, so it comes out as I never wrote a song.

In English that sentence does something else. I never wrote a song is a claim about a stretch of time that is closed — my school years, that summer, the band that fell apart. It can be true or false about that stretch, and it says nothing at all about today. Both sentences are correct; they are about different lives.

never goes between the two words, never after them: I've never written, not I've written never. The same slot holds ever, just, already and the negative not — everything that comments on the claim lives in the small gap between have and the participle.

1. ___ never written a song. (It is my whole life up to today.)
2. When I was ten I ___ a song about my dog. (That year is closed.)
3. He ___ never eaten sushi.
4. Have you ___ been to Rio?

3. A song that chose the past

Wrecking Ball

Miley Cyrus

Why the chorus does not say have hit

A perfect claim needs the story to still be open. This one is not: the person is standing in the rubble describing it. So I never hit so hard in love is the right sentence for that moment — and hit gives you no help at all, because it is the same word in all three columns (hit — hit — hit). The form is silent; the story decides.

Now move the sentence to today, with the relationship still going, and the same person says something else: I've never felt like this before. Nothing about the feeling changed. The claim now reaches the present, so the English changes shape — have appears, and the verb goes to its participle.

1. She is still in the relationship and tells you about her whole life up to now: which sentence?
2. The line All you ever did was wreck me opens the count and closes it. Why is it not have wrecked?

4. The same two claims on one axis

Where the claim stops

One speaker, two sentences about the same two songs.

a now

I've written two songs. — bar a runs all the way to now, and it is open at the front: it keeps going.

b c now

I wrote two songs in 2022. — bar b stops there, and the hatched c is time the sentence says nothing about.

Both sentences can be true of the same person on the same day. Portuguese escrevi duas músicas covers both, so translation cannot tell you which one English wants — the shape can. A bar that reaches now is why never fits it: the count runs to this minute, which is also why yesterday does not.

open — still true todayhatched — outside the claim

Read the two bars again and answer without looking: which one would you use to tell someone your whole life has no songs in it?

2. Which sentence matches the open bar a?

5. Say it fast: I've, you've, he's

Nobody says I have twice in a row

Said out loud, have loses its vowel and joins the person: I've /aɪv/, you've /juːv/, we've, they've. After he, she and it the mode word is has, and it loses more: he's, she's, it's.

That last set does a second job, which is the only real trap here. He's is he is before an -ing word and he has before a participle: He's writing — he is writing now. He's written — he has written, and it is done. One letter after the apostrophe looks the same either way, so read the word that follows.

I've written a song.
He's written a song.

1. I have finished it. → finished it.
2. You have seen this film. → seen this film.
3. She has eaten already. → eaten already.
4. He's ___ the game. (He has won it — not he is winning it.)

6. What the group chat does with it

The same machine, four messages

These are the forms you will actually read tonight, in the order a phone gives them to you:

  • He's lost his keys. — he has lost them. The word after the contraction is a participle, so the apostrophe is hiding has.
  • Have you eaten? — the question turns have to the front, exactly as do and will do. No do appears: do you have eaten is not English.
  • He's been glazing that game all week. — glazing is internet talk for praising something far too much. has been + -ing is the same machine with a longer tail: the praise started before this week and has not stopped.
  • He's never beating the allegations. — here he's is he is, not he has, because beating follows it. Beating the allegations is the internet's way of saying the joke about you is never going to die.

One apostrophe, two verbs, and the next word is the only thing that tells you which. That is not a trick of slang — it is this lesson's rule applied to a group chat. Chant the two loudest irregulars until the third column is automatic: go went gone, do did done.

7. What kills the perfect

A finished time carries a finished form

Say yesterday, last night, at eight, in 2019, two days ago and you have named a time that is over. English then refuses the perfect, every time, without exception. I have seen it yesterday is not a small mistake; it is two incompatible claims in one sentence, a bar that reaches today and a bar that ended on Tuesday.

The words that live inside the perfect name no date at all. ever, never, just, already and yet comment on the claim instead of placing it: I've just finished, Have you eaten yet?, She's already left. None of them says when — that is exactly why they are allowed in.

1. I ___ that film yesterday.
2. We ___ already eaten — no time named.
3. She ___ him two days ago.
4. He ___ his homework at eight, before dinner.

8. Your turn: three lines about you

Nothing to copy

Three sentences, three true facts about your own life. Use the participle — the third column, the one this lesson spent its time on.

1. I've never .
2. I've twice.
3. I've just .

Now say each one out loud, with the contraction, and listen to the gap between I've and the participle. That gap is where never and just live, and it is the only place they live: I've never written, never I've written never.
