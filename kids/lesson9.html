Facts, Habits & Never "Now"

This tense never means "right now". It means always — facts, habits, rules. Say it right and you can win any argument about animals, games or school times.

1. The bathroom argument

There's a spider in Nando's bathroom. The group chat has opinions.

A. Grupo "tem uma aranha aqui" — 21:07

tem uma aranha aqui 🕷️

Duda: spiders bite. be careful

Caio: no they don't. cats bite more

Duda: cats hate water. everybody knows that

B. What this tense actually does

JobExampleMeans
a fact — always trueCats hate water.all cats, all water, every day
a habit — your routineI study English every day.usually, again and again
a rule — how the world worksIce melts in the sun.always, no exceptions
a schedule — a fixed timeThe stream starts at eight.a plan on a timetable
Look at the four jobs: none of them is "happening this second". That's the trap. "Eu estudo inglês" = I study English (a habit). "Eu estou estudando agora" = a different form — that's section 6.

3. Facts about the world

A. Say the whole species or one animal — both work

Whole species (plural)One animal (singular, + -s)
Cats hate water.A cat hates water.
Spiders bite.A spider bites.
Snakes don't have legs.A snake doesn't have legs.
The -s of lesson 6 has found its real job: it marks "one single thing". Facts about the whole group take the plural with no -s on the verb. Facts about one single member take the -s. Both are 100% correct English — you just can't mix the two.

4. Habits: what you do again and again

A. Your routine with a time marker

HabitWith the marker
I walk to school.I walk to school every day.
She studies English.She studies English on Mondays.
We play FIFA.We play FIFA on the weekend.
He reads.He reads at night.
A habit doesn't need a time marker to be a habit — "I speak Portuguese" is a fact about you. But the marker (every day, on Mondays, at night) kills every doubt, and it's what English speakers really stick at the end of the sentence.

5. Schedules: the present that talks about later

A. Timetables use this tense — for today and for next week

SentenceWhen
School starts at seven.every day — and tomorrow too
The stream starts at eight.tonight, on a schedule
The movie starts at nine.at the mall, fixed time
My bus leaves at six thirty.a timetable, no negotiation
This is the one place where the present tense points at the future — but only when a timetable decides it. School, buses, movies, game servers and streams all run on a clock, so English says starts. "A live começa às oito" = The stream starts at eight. You'll also hear "will start", but for a fixed schedule starts is the safe form.

3.A live do Nando começa às oito.
4.O filme começa às nove.

6. The trap: this tense never means "now"

A. Two different sentences, two different meanings

Fact / habitHappening right now
I study English.I'm studying English now.
She works at the mall.She's working right now.
He plays FIFA.He's playing FIFA right now.
The -ing form is the "now" form. It gets its own lesson later — today you only need to recognise it: if you see am / is / are + verb-ing, it means this second. That's why "I am study English every day" is the sentence that marks every Brazilian: two forms glued together. Pick one — I study for a habit, I'm studying for now.
