A, The, There's — Where Everything Is

Three tiny words decide if you sound Brazilian or not: a, the and there's. One stolen snack, one pigeon and one mall later, you'll own them.

1. The chat that started it

Nando is at the park. Duda is at the mall. Read the chat — then look at the three words in bold.

A. Grupo "quem vai pro mall" — 16:04

quem vai pro mall 🛍️

Nando: which shop?

Duda: the shop on the corner, next to the cinema

Nando: lol I'm at the park. there are two courts here, come play

Duda: bro you live at the park 😭 do you have a snack?

B. Three words, three jobs

WordIts jobFrom the chat
a / anone thing — and you don't know which one yetThere's a new shop.
thethat one — we both know which oneThe shop on the corner.
there'sit exists — it's thereThere's a new shop at the mall.
Portuguese has one word doing all three jobs: tem, um/uma, o/a. English splits the work — and that split is the whole lesson.

3. A / AN — one thing from the pile

You say a the first time you talk about a thing. It means um/uma — um entre muitos. Nando doesn't know which snack the pigeon wants. Nobody does. So: a snack.

A. First time / second time

First time — aSecond time — the
There's a dog at the bus stop.The dog is eating my snack. 😭
I see a shop on the corner.The shop sells fake jerseys.
Same thing, two sentences. First mention = a. After that the listener knows exactly which one you mean — so it's the for the rest of the story.

B. A or AN? The sound decides, not the letter

a + consonant soundan + vowel sound
a shop · a gym · a park · a courtan apple · an hour · an old shop
an hour, never "a hour" — the h is silent, so the word opens with a vowel sound. Say the word out loud before you choose.

4. THE — the one we both already know

The is a pointing word. It means "you know which one I mean". Three ways that happens:

A. The three jobs of THE

JobExample
1. We both know which oneThe shop on the corner sells snacks.
2. I mentioned it two seconds agoThere's a court near the gym. The court is free today.
3. There's only onethe sun · the sky · the mall in my town
No the before a name: Shopping Recife, McDonald's, Rio. Names don't need pointing — they're already specific.

B. Places in town: the word that never leaves

at the mall at the park at the gym at the movies on the field on the court at the bus stop at the shop on the corner

Drop the the and it sounds broken: "I go to gym" ❌ → "I go to the gym" ✅. Em português também dizemos "vou para a academia" — aqui o inglês concorda com você. O problema é o contrário: às vezes o português tem "o/a" e o inglês não tem nada.

5. There's / There are — "tem" when nothing owns anything

Portuguese tem does two jobs that English refuses to mix:

A. "Tem" splits in two

a frase em portuguêsEnglishWhy
Tem um parque perto daqui.There's a park near here.the park just exists — nobody owns it
Eu tenho um lanche.I have a snack.you own it — it's in your bag
A academia tem uma quadra.The gym has a court.the gym has it — part of the gym
There's = it exists somewhere. Have / has = somebody owns it. Say "Has a park near here" and every English speaker hears a missing subject — "tem" with nothing before it doesn't translate. Pick there's.

B. One or many?

one thing → there'smany things → there are
There's a court near the gym.There are two courts near the gym.
There's a snack in my bag.There are snacks in my bag. 🍫
There's = there is. Talking fast, people say "there's two courts" — but write there are for many, and your teacher will never mark it.

6. No article at all — when English says nothing

Here is the sentence that gives Brazilians away most often. "Eu gosto de lanches." In English it's "I like snacks." Not "I like the snacks" — the the isn't there at all.

A. General things take nothing

português — com artigoEnglish — no article
Eu gosto de lanches.I like snacks.
Parques são ótimos.Parks are great.
Eu vou para a escola.I go to school.
Ele está em casa.He's at home.
Rule of thumb: talking about the whole category (snacks in general, parks in general) → nothing. Talking about specific ones → the. I like snacks (all of them) ≠ I like the snacks here (these ones, in this shop).

B. Sticky phrases with no the, no to

go home at home go to school at school in class go to bed go to work

Essas são expressões fixas: go home (nunca "go to home"), at school (dentro da escola, na aula) × at the school (no prédio da escola). Decore como bloco, não monte peça por peça.

C. THE or nothing?

1.I like ___ snacks. (snacks in general)
2.I like ___ snacks in this shop. (these specific ones)
3.I go to ___ school at 7. (I'm a student)
4.My mom is at ___ school. (she's waiting in the building)
5.___ parks in my city are small. (the known set — all our parks)
6.___ courts are free in my city. (all of them, in general)
