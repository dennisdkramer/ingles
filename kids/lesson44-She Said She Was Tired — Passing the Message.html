She Said She Was Tired — Passing the Message

The group chat, 9:14 p.m.

  1. KikoMel said she was tired.
  2. MelI said I am tired.
  3. Kiko…so you were tired.
  4. MelI'M. TIRED. NOW.
  5. KikoMy bad.

Passing a message is not repeating it. Every sentence you carry has two things that have to move: the verb, which steps one step back, and every word that points at the person who said it. Get either one wrong and the group chat is on fire before you finish typing.

Say or tell — one word takes the person

Tell takes the person, say takes the words

Tell goes straight to the person: tell me, tell Kiko, tell her. Say goes straight to the words: say it, say something, say yes.

When the person comes after say, to stands between them: say it to me. The person can never come straight after say.

tellsay
+ persontell me · tell Kiko—
+ wordstell the truthsay it · say yes
+ thing + to + persontell it to mesay it to me

A few fixed things also go with tell and no person: the truth, a lie, a story, a joke.

The trap

Portuguese puts the person right before the verb: ela me disse. English puts the person after tell: she told me.

The mistake is "she said me" — no way around it: a person cannot follow say.

"She said to me" is fine: the to is doing the work.

Who receives the telling

She told me the truth.

The tagger marks told as the verb and me as the recipient: the arrow lands on the person the telling is for. the truth is the thing told — that is the whole shape of tell: person, then thing.

The tense steps back

One step into the past

When the verb that carries the message is past — said, told — the words inside the message step one step back. The message happened before you opened your mouth to pass it on, so its verbs sit further back on the line, like they heard their name in the group chat.

they saidyou pass on
I am tired.she said she was tired
I’ll be late.he told me he’d be late
I can’t come.she said she couldn’t come
I don’t know.he said he didn’t know

Portuguese steps back too: ela disse que estava cansada.

Still true?

English steps back even when the thing is still true: she said she was tired — and she still is.

Keeping the present (she said she IS tired) is a choice: it says "and it is still true right now".

The pronouns and the clock

The message is no longer in the speaker’s mouth. Every word that pointed at the speaker has to move to whoever said it: I becomes he or she, my becomes his or her, we becomes they. Leave them where they were and you are officially quoting the wrong person.

The time words move too: now becomes then, today becomes that day, last night becomes the night before, tomorrow becomes the next day. In a chat, people often leave last night and tomorrow alone; in careful writing they move.

Questions lose the question shape

Keep the question word, lose the question order

A reported question is not a question any more — nobody is asking. Keep the question word (where, what, why), drop the helper, and put the subject back in front of the verb.

the questionreported
Where are you?she asked where I was
What did you say?he asked what I said
Why are you crying?she asked why I was crying

Yes/no questions have no question word, so if takes its place: Do you like him? → she asked if I liked him.

The helper disappears

do, does and did exist to make a question. With no question left, they go — and the verb keeps the past.

Where did you go? → he asked where I went. Never “where did I go”.

Tell them to do it

Orders and favours

An order is not a statement, so it cannot be reported with said and a clause. It becomes tell + person + to + verb: Spill the tea. → she told me to spill the tea.

A negative order puts not before to: Don’t tell anyone. → she told me not to tell anyone. A favour uses ask: Can you wait? → she asked me to wait. Nobody likes the friend who passes an order on as a suggestion.

the orderreported
Spill the tea.she told me to spill the tea
Don’t tell anyone.she told me not to tell anyone
Can you wait?she asked me to wait

Portuguese does the same thing with para: ela me disse para contar. English uses to, and not to for the negative — never “tell me don’t”.

The whole message

Mel’s version of Saturday

“So Kiko told me that Duda said she wasn’t coming to the party, and that she was throwing shade at me the whole time. Then Duda texted me and said she never said that, and that Kiko told her something different. I asked her who told her that, and she said she didn’t know. Now everyone is mad at everyone and the party is on Saturday.”

One sentence holds the whole lesson: he said she was throwing shade at him. A past reporting verb, a continuous that stepped back, and a pronoun pointing at whoever the message was about. That’s wild when you see all three in eight words.

Four things to find in Mel’s message: two tells, the verbs that stepped back, one reported question, and one pronoun that points at a different person than it did in the original.
