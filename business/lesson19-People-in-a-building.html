People in a building

The thing done becomes the subject

The shift, in one pair

An active sentence puts the doer first: Two people check the report every Friday. A passive sentence takes the object, makes it the subject, and moves the verb into be + the third form: The report is checked by two people every Friday.

Nothing about the facts changed. What changed is which end of the sentence the room is looking at — and that is the reason to choose one over the other.

The third form is the one the past tense already gave you: checked, sent, moved, called. Three are irregular and worth keeping beside the others: written, done, made.

The be carries the tense, exactly as did did in the negative. Is checked is now, was checked is then, will be sent is later.

The report is checked by two people.

The invoice was sent on Monday.

Read the marks: the subject is the thing, the little word is be, the verb is the third form, and the doer — when it appears — sits behind by. In the second sentence there is no doer at all, which is the next section.

by, or nobody at all

The doer is optional — and that is the point

When the doer matters, the passive names them with by: The report is checked by two people. When the doer does not matter, or is not known, or is better left out, the passive drops it: The invoice was sent on Monday, The meeting was moved to Friday.

That is not laziness. The passive exists so that the sentence can be about the thing while the person steps out of the frame — and in a company that is sometimes the whole point of the sentence.

Three moments where the doer disappears on purpose. Unknown: no one knows who did it. Obvious: the invoice is sent by us, and saying so adds nothing. Polite: The mistake was made in the first version says what happened without pointing at a person — and a manager hears the difference immediately.

One caution: dropping the agent to avoid responsibility is a known habit, and people recognise it. Use the passive for the thing, not to hide a person who should be named.

The words a passive sentence is built from

Twelve pieces, and what each one does

The passive takes the time words the past tense already taught: on Monday, last week, at nine. The be carries the tense and the time word stays where it was: The invoice was sent on Monday. The client was called twice.

Twice is worth a note: it counts occasions, not hours. Called twice is two calls; called two times is the same fact said by someone still counting.

Five verbs, their third forms

  • a sent
  • b written
  • c made
  • d done
  • e checked
  • f wrote

One form is left over, and the passive cannot use it: it is the past, and the passive needs the third form.

Three times, one third form

The be carries the tense; the third form does not move

The passive has three tenses on this page, and the only thing that changes is be: is checked (a rule, every week), was checked (finished, and we know when), will be sent (later). The third form is the same in all three, because the time is already in the be.

Agreement moves with the subject as it always does: The report is checked, The reports are checked, The numbers were sent.

Two errors carry most of the weight. Leaving the be out makes the sentence look like a past active: The report checked by two people has no tense of its own. And using the past form instead of the third form breaks the pattern twice: was wrote.

The repair is one question: where is the be, and is the second word the third form? Two words, in that order, every time.

Look at turns two and three together. Jess says Who sent it? — an active question, because the person is what she wants. Mike answers with the passive, because the answer is a machine and the thing is what matters.

That is the whole choice, made in two turns of a real conversation: name the doer when the doer is the point; leave it out when the thing is.

Turn seven is the polite passive: it was not checked by anyone else states a gap without blaming Ana.
