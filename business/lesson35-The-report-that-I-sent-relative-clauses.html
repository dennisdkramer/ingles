The report that I sent: relative clauses

The clause that describes a noun

One noun, one clause

A relative clause describes a noun and stands directly after it: The person who runs the tests is on vacation. The noun and the clause are one unit, so nothing separates them and the clause never moves to the front.

The clause begins with a word that replaces a noun inside it. who replaces a person, which a thing, that either one, where a place, and whose a possession: the supplier whose invoice was late. The word is not a question word here; it links two statements about the same noun.

Portuguese uses que for people and things alike, so a Brazilian speaker can put that everywhere. English keeps who for people in careful writing, and it never uses that after a comma.

whose belongs to people and things alike: the supplier whose invoice was late, a product whose price changes every week. It is the possessive of both who and which, and it is the only one of these words that carries a noun along with it.

A clause after a comma does not identify anything; it adds a fact. The report that covers Q4 is ready picks one report out of several. The report, which covers Q4, is ready says there is one report and mentions what it covers as extra information. The comma changes the meaning, and it changes the words allowed after it: that is impossible there, so the clause takes which for a thing and who for a person.

When the link word can disappear

The noun's job inside its own clause

The link word can be left out when the noun is the object inside the clause: This is the document (that) you asked for. The clause already has its subject — you — so the link word adds nothing and English drops it.

It cannot be left out when the noun is the subject inside the clause: The person who runs the tests is on vacation. Remove who and the clause has no subject, so the sentence has two verbs and no one to perform the first one.

The test is one question. Read the clause alone and ask who performs its verb. If the noun does, keep the link word. If another word does, the link word may go. You asked for … — the document does not ask; you asks, so the noun is the object, and the word may disappear.

This is the document you asked for.

The two marked lines are the whole rule in picture form: in the first, you performs asked and the document is what the asking was aimed at; in the second, the noun itself performs runs.

That is also why who cannot be swapped for which when the noun is a person and the clause is doing the describing in a careful document: the two words carry the same job and a different kind of noun.

A last check that costs one second: read the clause as a sentence of its own. You asked for. is incomplete — and that is expected, because the noun at the front is the missing object. Who runs the tests is complete, which is why its link word has to stay.

Two consequences worth remembering

When the link word is dropped, the noun and the clause still belong together: the file you sent is one thing, not a file and a separate action.

The word it never follows the clause: the clause already contains the object, so the document you asked for it says the same thing twice.

what is the link word that has its noun inside it: What I sent on Friday is the final version means the thing that I sent. Because the noun is already in what, a sentence that names the noun and then adds what says it twice — the report what I sent is not English, and the repair is that or nothing at all.

One more consequence follows from the same logic: a clause that has lost its link word cannot be moved. The file you sent keeps the file first; put the clause at the front and a link word is needed again — what you sent is the file.

The words this lesson needs

one, the one, the only

picks a single item out of a set, and a relative clause usually follows it: The one that covers Q4. A person is a human being; a supplier is a company that sells you goods; a document is a file or a paper with information on it.

the onethe single item, out of the items available, that the sentence then describes.

the only says that no other exists: the only supplier we have for this part. is the one responsible for the work.

the person in chargethe person responsible for the work or the decision.

introduces examples of a larger group: documents such as invoices and contracts.

such asfor example — the items named are examples of the group, not the whole list.

someone, anyone, everything, nothing

someone and something stand in a positive statement: Someone from support called. anything and anyone stand in a question or a negative: Did anyone call? I didn't get anything.

everything means all of it, and nothing means none of it. Nothing is already negative, so the verb stays positive: Nothing changed, never Nothing didn't change.

Every one of these words can take the same relative clause as a noun: someone who signs off, everything that covers Q4.

Which report? — identifying in a meeting

The clause is how a meeting names a thing

A meeting spends much of its time identifying: which report, which supplier, which version. The relative clause does that work in one unit, and it replaces the repetition a speaker would otherwise need: the version whose numbers changed instead of the version, then a second sentence about the numbers.

When the noun is already clear from the situation, the link word disappears and the clause runs straight after it: the file you asked for. When the noun needs describing for the first time, the clause carries the description: the person who signs off.

A comma changes the job of the clause, and it is the one punctuation mark this lesson cannot skip. Our supplier which we have used for six years is raising prices says you have more than one supplier; our supplier, which we have used for six years, is raising prices says you have one, and the clause is an aside. The first identifies, the second adds.

That is also why that cannot follow a comma: it only identifies. A clause with a comma in front of it takes which for a thing and who for a person, and the sentence is read as two facts rather than one label.

In speech the difference is carried by a pause, and in a meeting that pause is doing the same work as the commas: the version that covers Q4 picks one out, and the version, which covers Q4, tells the listener something new about a version that is already known.

One more use is worth having ready: the one plus a clause, which is how a speaker answers a question about a set — the one whose numbers changed, the ones that cover Q3.

And the identifying clause is what a manager uses to avoid naming a person in public: the team that owns the release points at a group without pointing at anyone in it.

Written down after the meeting, those answers become the notes: the version whose numbers changed, the one we sent on Friday. The clause is doing the filing.
