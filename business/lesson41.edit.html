Grammar Under the Clock

What this lesson installs: nothing new. This is the consolidation page — every form here you already own from lessons 4 to 36, and the work is to reach for it while the clock runs. The law is that fluency is grammar with the attention somewhere else: when the topic is a late project, the grammar has to arrive without being looked at. The second law: there are three decisions, not twelve tenses — when (past / now / future), aspect (simple / continuous / perfect), stance (fact / expectation / hypothesis). One mapping to fix on the way in: Portuguese eu estava esperando is not I was waiting in a stand-up — at work the natural line is I'm still waiting on it; the continuous holds the shape of an action, it is not a politeness device. By the end of the page you leave with three named errors of your own and a retest, because at this level the remaining work is personal.

1. Heard — the twenty-second update

This is a real stand-up update at real speed. Press ▶ once and listen only for the facts. Do not study the words: the panel asks three questions, and the questions are about the project, not about the grammar.

Priya: "Quick one. So — we were aiming for the fourteenth, and that's gone. The build's been holding since Tuesday because the payment gateway didn't come back with the certificate, and honestly by the time we got it, QA had already been sitting on the same three tickets for a week. We've moved the launch to the twenty-eighth. The client knows, they're fine, we're not paying for it. What I need today is somebody to own the comms to the two hundred beta users — anyone?"

A. Facts only, no grammar

1.What is the new launch date?
2.Why is the build holding?
3.Who is paying for the delay?
4.What does she need before the meeting ends?
If you got the date wrong, you were listening to the grammar. That is not a failure of English — it is what happens to everyone in a real meeting, in every language, when the forms are still being assembled in the background. The rest of this page moves the forms into the background so the facts get the attention.

2. Second pass — six forms in view

Now read the same update with the forms visible. Six of them carry the whole thing: name what each one is doing, not what it is called — knowing the job is what makes a form retrievable at speed.

#The lineWhat the form is doing
1"we were aiming for the fourteenth" a plan that was in progress and is now dead — the continuous holds the shape of the old plan
2"that's gone" present perfect: the past with a result you can see right now
3"the build's been holding since Tuesday" perfect continuous: a state that started and has not stopped — since gives the start, not the length
4"by the time we got it, QA had already been sitting on the same three tickets" two clocks: by the time + past simple, plus a duration already running in the past perfect continuous
5"We've moved the launch" present perfect for a decision that is now the situation
6"they're fine, we're not paying for it" present simple / continuous for the current state — no perfect, because nothing is being linked backwards
Notice what is not there: no future form for the twenty-eighth. In a stand-up, a fixed date is said in the present perfect or the present — we've moved it to the twenty-eighth, it's the twenty-eighth now. The future arrives only when the date is a prediction again.

3. Three decisions, not twelve tenses

Every tense question on this page is answered by three choices in this order. The trigger is what decides — not the meaning you had in your head, and never the Portuguese sentence you started from. The chart is the reference for the rest of the lesson.

DecisionThe trigger that decides itThe form it producesWhat it sounds like when it is wrong
1. When?now · today · since · for · already · yet · ago · last · by the time · next · ifthe timeline of the sentence: past, present or future"I am working here since 2019" — the since demands the perfect
2. Aspect — simple?the listener needs the fact, the result, or the habitpresent / past / future simple"I was send the file" — a fact dressed as a story
2. Aspect — continuous?the listener needs the shape: in progress, temporary, repeatedbe + -ing"I am knowing the client" — a state in a form that only takes actions
2. Aspect — perfect?the listener needs a link to now, or an earlier pasthave + participle / had + participle"I have sent it yesterday" — yesterday closes the door the perfect opens
2. Aspect — both?a duration that was already running when something else happenedhad been + -ing + for / since"I worked there for six months when they called" — the two clocks collapsed into one
3. Stance — fact?you saw it, or it is in the recordplain tense, no modal"It must be the certificate" when you know — a deduction where a fact belongs
3. Stance — expectation?a rule, a promise, a plan somebody owesshould / should have"You should send it yesterday" — the expectation is already missed; it needs should have sent
3. Stance — deduction?you are reasoning from evidence, not reportingmust have / can't have / might have"He must send it last night" — the evidence is past, the modal is not
3. Stance — hypothesis?if, or a branch that was not takenthe four conditionals / would have"If I would have known" — the would does not belong in the if
Twelve tenses, four aspects, five stances — and one order: when → aspect → stance. Decide in that order and the form is almost forced. Decide the form first and you will spend the meeting rebuilding it.

4. Two clocks, and the if with the if removed

Left: a duration was already running when something else happened — by the time + past simple, and had been + -ing + for. Right: the formal conditional, where if leaves and the auxiliary moves in front. Both are forms you own; the drill is retrieval.

A. Both clocks in one sentence

1. They ____ (work) on it for six months by the time we ____ (ship).

durante + marco
2. By the time the certificate ____ (arrive), QA ____ (sit) on three tickets for a week.

o segundo já estava rodando
3. She ____ (wait) since March by the time they ____ (answer).

since + marco
4. We ____ (run) the old system for four years when they ____ (pull) the plug.

for + when

B. Take the if out

Formal, written, dramatic — the same sentence with the if dropped and the auxiliary first. Never in a stand-up; always in a summary that has to land.

1. If I had known about the certificate, I would have called. →

Had I…
2. If we had moved the date in June, we would have avoided this. →

Had we…
3. If the client had asked, we would have flagged it. →

Had the client…
4. If it had been tested, we would not be here. →

Had it…
Both halves are the same move: one form does the work of two clauses. In the two-clocks sentence, had been sitting carries a duration that the past simple cannot; in the inverted conditional, Had I known carries the whole hypothesis in three words. Speed comes from having them as single units, not from building them.

5. The four modal perfects are stance, not tense

Same past event, four different positions on it. should have = the expectation that failed · must have = the deduction I am sure of · would have = the branch not taken · might have = the one I cannot rule out. Listen ▶ to all four on the same sentence.

should have — "They should have told us on Monday."

must have — "They must have known on Monday."

would have — "If they had told us on Monday, we would have re-planned."

might have — "They might have sent it and it bounced."

A. Which stance is it?

1."The invoice can't have gone out — I never approved it."
2."Somebody should have caught this in review."
3."If we'd had one more day, we would have shipped it."
4."The certificate may have expired on the twelfth."
5."She must have been in the meeting — her calendar was blocked."
6."You should have told me before you called the client."

6. The timed grid — twelve lines, four seconds each

This is the lesson. Set a timer that beeps every four seconds, or use a phone metronome. Twelve items, four seconds each, no consulting, no correcting — write the form and move on at the beep. A correct answer that arrives in eight seconds is a wrong answer in a meeting.

How to score it: one mark per line that is both right and written before the beep. 12-10 = retrieval is automatic; 9-7 = the form exists but arrives late (this is the normal score on the first run); 6 or fewer = two or three specific forms are missing, and they will show up as the same mistake four or five times. Circle the repeats — those are your errors for section 8.

If untimed is 12 and timed is 7, your knowledge is complete and your retrieval is not — which is exactly the gap this page exists to close, and it closes with repetition, not with more explanation.
