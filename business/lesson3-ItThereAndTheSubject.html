It, there, and the subject English refuses to drop

Portuguese leaves the subject empty; English fills the slot

Three sentences with no subject — and the English they need

Three sentences a Brazilian says every working day arrive in English with an empty gap at the front:

Está frio. · É importante para o cliente. · Tem um problema.

In Portuguese nothing is missing: está and é need no subject, and tem hides one. In English the first slot of the sentence belongs to a subject, and it is never empty. When there is no person or thing to name, English puts a placeholder in that slot, and the two placeholders are it and there.

Both are real subjects. They sit before the verb, the verb agrees with them, and a sentence without one of them is not an English sentence.

Read the middle column: each English sentence starts with a word the Portuguese does not have.

The choice between the two is not a feeling. It points at something or carries the weather, the time or an opinion. There announces that something exists, and the thing itself comes after the verb: There's a problem — the problem is the news.

This is the sentence a Brazilian loses in a meeting: Is important has no subject, and an English ear hears a sentence that never started. The word in front is not decoration; it is half of what makes the sentence a sentence.

It: the subject for weather, time and opinions

It names nothing, and that is the job

It usually replaces a thing — the report becomes it. Here it does something else: it fills the subject slot when the sentence has nothing to name. Four families of sentence work this way:

  • Weather — It's cold. · It's hot.
  • Time and date — It's nine. · It's Friday.
  • Distance — It's far.
  • Opinion — It's important. · It's a good idea.

The Portuguese sentence has no word for it in any of the four. Está frio has one verb; é importante has one verb. English gives each one a subject first and the verb second.

It's important for the client.

It's cold.

The mark on It says subject — the same mark I and she carry. That is the whole lesson in one colour: the word has no meaning of its own here, and it still does a subject's work.

Spoken English contracts it every time: it's. The full it is is for emphasis or for a form.

Which English sentence says it?

1. É importante para o cliente.

2. Está frio hoje.

3. É uma boa ideia.

4. São nove horas.

One trap in option d of the first item: have never replaces it. Tem in a sentence about the weather or an opinion is not have — it is either it is or there is, and the next section draws the line.

There is and there are: English says that something exists

One thing: there is. Many things: there are

There announces that something exists. The verb agrees with the thing announced, not with there: there is one thing, there are many things. The thing itself arrives after the verb, so the first word is there and the news comes late: There's a problem. · There are two options.

This is where tem and há land in English. Have is not the word for it, because have means that a person or a thing possesses something: It has a problem says the it owns a problem, which is not what tem um problema means.

The contraction there's is the normal spoken form of there is. With a plural, write there are.

Four of the words this lesson owns, in the sentences they live in:

  • problem(pro·bləm) — There's a problem with the invoice. problema
  • time(time) — There's no time today. tempo
  • money(mə·ney) — There's money for the project. dinheiro
  • idea(i·dea) — There are three ideas on the table. ideia

Two more of the lesson's own sentences, both plural in meaning and both common in a calendar: There are two options. and There's a match on Sunday.

Write is or are

The thing after the verb decides the form.

1. There a problem with the invoice.

2. There two options.

3. There a match on Sunday.

4. There three ideas on the table.

5. There no time for a meeting today.

Read each finished line aloud and keep the pair together: there's a problem, there are two options. They are two words in speech — there'sa problem — and that is why the singular hides so well and the plural gives people away.

No time is a fixed phrase worth having: sem tempo, said as there's no time. It uses the singular because time is one thing you cannot count.

I'm cold, not I have cold: a state uses be

Portuguese says estou com; English says I'm

A state is how a person is at the moment. Portuguese builds it with estar + com: estou com frio, estou com fome, estou com calor. English builds it with be + adjective and nothing else: I'm cold. I'm hungry. I'm hot. I'm tired.

There is no with and no have in the English line. I have cold is not a sentence about the body; it sounds like you own a cold. And I'm with cold is a word-for-word copy that English does not use.

One more state works exactly the same way, and it surprises every Brazilian: age. Tenho trinta anos is I'm thirty. — a form of be, never have.

I have hungry.

I'm hungry.

The four adjectives, with the sound that catches people out:

  • cold(cold) — com frio. The d is written and not said.
  • hot(hot) — com calor. The t is written and not said.
  • hungry(hun·gry) — com fome. Two syllables, one g sound.
  • tired(ti·red) — cansado. Three sounds, not two.

Write the English adjective

Each line is a state. Write the one word English puts after be.

Now read the same adjective in two sentences: It's cold. and I'm cold. The first is the weather — it for a thing nobody names. The second is your body — I for the person who feels it.

That is the pair to keep: it when the sentence has nothing to name, I when it has you. Never have in either one.

his, her, our, their: the possessive follows the owner

English looks at the person, not at the thing

A possessive says who owns something. English chooses the word from the owner: a man owns it — his; a woman owns it — her; you and I own it — our; they own it — their. The thing owned never changes the word: his job, her job, our job.

Portuguese chooses from the thing: o seu emprego and a sua vaga change with the noun. That is the direction to reverse. Once you know who owns it, the English word is fixed.

The word Portuguese hides inside seu

Seu and sua can mean your, his, her, its or their. Portuguese lets the sentence decide. English makes you decide before you speak, and the common error is your where the owner is a third person: Bruno está no seu escritório is Bruno is in his office, not your office.

The lesson's own example uses the plural owner: Our deadline is Friday. — Nosso prazo é sexta. Deadline (prazo, data limite) is the word for the date a thing must be finished, and it is one of the most used nouns in this office.

Write the possessive

Decide who owns it, then write the word.

1. Ana is at desk.

2. Bruno is in office.

3. We are in office on Friday.

4. deadline is Friday — I am on the team.

Two small things that save letters and mean the same: our and their are never written with an apostrophe. It's means it is; its means belonging to it. The apostrophe is a small thing that reads as a large one in an email to a client.

Read the four finished lines as one office: Ana is at her desk. Bruno is in his office. We are in our office on Friday. Our deadline is Friday. Every possessive points back at the person, never at the thing.

this, that, here, there: near and far

Four words, one question: is it close?

This and that point at things. This is the thing near you — in your hand, on your screen; that is the thing further away. Here and there point at places: here is where I am, there is somewhere else.

  • This is my desk. — the desk I am touching.
  • That is the client's office. — across the floor.
  • The file is here. · The office is there.

In a meeting, this does one more job worth copying: it introduces a person. This is Bruno. — Este é o Bruno. No he, no it, no article before the name.

There is a place word and there is a subject

The same five letters do two jobs, and the verb tells you which one you are looking at:

  • There as a place — The office is there. Nothing follows it; it is the end of the sentence.
  • There as the subject of existence — There's an office on the second floor. A verb and a thing follow it.

When you hear there's, the sentence is announcing something. When you hear there at the end of a line, someone is pointing. Same word, two machines.
