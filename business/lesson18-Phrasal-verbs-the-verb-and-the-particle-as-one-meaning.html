Phrasal verbs: the verb and the particle as one meaning

One verb, four particles, four different verbs

The particle is part of the word

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word, and the small word is not a direction and not a decoration. Look after is care, look for is search, look up is find in a list, look at is put your eyes on it. Four words that share a verb and share nothing else.

This is the same lesson as the preposition page, one step further: there the pair was a verb and a preposition, here it is a verb and a particle that can move. And because it can move, there is one rule about where the object stands — the next section.

The particles do a small number of jobs again and again: up often means complete or find (set up, look up, pick up), off means away or cancelled (turn off, put off, take off), on means continue (go on).

The pattern is a guide, not a rule. Give up means stop, and the up there is not completion at all. When the pattern and the meaning disagree, the meaning wins — that is why the list is learned with its sentences.

Where the object can stand

Separable and inseparable

Some phrasal verbs let the object travel between the verb and the particle. Turn off the light and turn the light off are the same sentence. These are separable, and the office favourites are turn off, pick up, put off, set up, find out, look up.

Others refuse. Get back to keeps its object after the particle: I'll get back to the client, never get the client back to. These are inseparable, and they are usually the ones that already end in a preposition: get back to, run out of, look after, go on.

The long object likes the end. Turn off the printer in room two is easier on the ear than turn the printer in room two off — both are correct, and the second is what a native speaker avoids when the object is long.

Short object, particle last: turn the light off. Long object, particle first: turn off the printer in room two. Nothing is wrong with the other order; one is simply easier to say.

And then the object is a pronoun

The rule that has no exception

With a noun, a separable verb has two orders. With a pronoun — it, them, him, her — there is only one: the pronoun goes inside. Turn it off, pick them up, put it off, turn them down. Turn off it is not a variant; it is not English.

The reason is stress. A pronoun is the weakest word in a sentence and the particle is one of the strongest, so the strong word goes last. Turn it OFF. Two words, and the stress lands where meaning lives.

An inseparable verb keeps the pronoun outside, because it has no place to put it in: get back to her, look after them, run out of it. Look them after is the error the rule produces when it is applied to the wrong family.

One test, before you speak: is the verb in the separable list? If yes, the pronoun goes inside. If it ends in a preposition — to, of, after, on — the pronoun stays where it is.
