Innovation, without the hype

What this lesson installs: the language of claims — who is allowed to say what, with what evidence, and at which rung of certainty. The future is a ladder and the evidence chooses the rung: will is a commitment, is set to / is expected to is a sourced prediction somebody can be held to, could is possibility, is due to is a diary entry. One rung too high is a promise; one too low is a shrug. Then the drill that turns cynicism into a tool: a buzzword is a word that has stopped predicting anything, so audit it with three questions — what would we see if it were true, what would we see if it were false, and who disagrees. And the vocabulary of a real plan: pilot → gate → rollout → sunset, with the gate's evidence named.

2. The certainty ladder

Six rungs, from a promise down to a shrug. The evidence behind the claim chooses the rung — not your mood, and not how much you want the deal.

StructureThe evidence it needsWhat you commit toWhere it belongs
willyour own authority to decidea promise — you own the outcomea contract, a decision, a public commitment
is going toa plan plus the resources to run ita plan you can be asked abouta stand-up, a project kick-off
is set to / is expected tosomebody else's plan or forecasta sourced prediction, not your promisepress releases, roadmaps, board papers
is likely toa base rate, or a model with a track recorda probability you would defendanalysis, a risk register
could / may / mightpossibility onlynothing you can be held tooptions, early exploration, scenarios
is due toa date in somebody's diarya scheduled event, not a desired onea timeline, a delivery note
Two failures, symmetric: one rung too high is a promise you will be held to (the rollout will be complete in Q3 when it is a decision, not a delivery), and one rung too low is a shrug that makes you unusable (it might improve things about a measured pilot). The ladder is the same instinct as lesson 26's deduction scale, pointed forward.

A. Place the rung

Name the rung and what the speaker has actually committed to. Five words, no essay.

1. "The board will approve it on Thursday."
2. "The pilot suggests a 78% reduction in planning time."
3. "The rollout is expected in Q3."
4. "It could cut costs by a fifth."
5. "The union consultation is due to close on the twenty-first."
6. "We're going to run a second pilot in April."

3. Two drills: the press register and the honest hedge

A. Into "is set to"

Convert each plain future into the business-press pattern. Keep the meaning — you are sourcing the claim, not strengthening it.

1. "We think the market grows 4%."
2. "The trial finishes in June."
3. "Prices go up in January."
4. "The software launches in March."
5. "Depots cut overtime by a third."
6. "The board decides in Q3."

B. Hedge without hype

Six claims you cannot verify. Write the honest version — neither a promise nor a refusal.

1. It will save money.
2. Drivers will like it.
3. It works at scale.
4. The union is on board.
5. Q3 is realistic.
6. This is AI.
"Early data indicates…" · "the pilot suggests…" · "we have not tested this at scale".
Hype and refusal are both failures of nerve. "This will transform logistics" and "we really can't say anything yet" are the same answer to a person who needs to decide: neither one helps. The usable middle is a claim with its evidence attached and its limits named — "the pilot suggests a 78% reduction across six depots; we have not tested it at regional scale, and the rollout decision is in Q3 subject to the union consultation."

4. The buzzword audit

A buzzword is a word that has stopped predicting anything. The audit is three questions, asked in this order: what would we see if this were true? what would we see if it were false? who disagrees? A word that survives none of them is decoration. Audited below, not preached.

The termThe testable meaning (yours)Verdict
disruption

The three terms that survive the audit, with their precision restored: disruption is not "new" — it is a cheaper or simpler offer entering at the low end and moving up until the incumbent's margin disappears; scalability is not "big" — it is cost per unit staying flat as volume rises; digital literacy is not "liking computers" — name the three skills (finding information, judging its reliability, producing something with it) and the word becomes usable. Write keep or strike in the third column; a term you cannot test is a term you cannot use in an argument.

A. Three questions, one verdict

Take three terms from the table and run the questions on each — in writing, short.

The termIf true, we would see…If false, we would see…Who disagrees
The third question is the one that costs something, and it is the one that turns a slogan into a decision: who disagrees? If nobody in the room can be named as a sceptic, the claim has not been tested — it has been agreed.

5. Pilot, gate, rollout, sunset

A rollout plan is a plan because of the gate: the named decision point, with its evidence. Without a gate you have an announcement with dates attached. Put the six steps in order.

#The stepWhat it needs
1six depots, ninety days, one measure agreed in advance
2results compared with the baseline, not with the ambition
3a named person, a date, and a written criterion for go / no-go
4one region first, with the union consulted before, not after
5a named owner per site, and a number that ends the trial
6what stops, and when — the plan nobody writes and everybody needs
The vocabulary of a decision point is the most useful part of innovation English and the least taught: gate, go / no-go, criterion, baseline, sunset. A gate with no criterion is a meeting; a pilot with no baseline is an anecdote.

6. The claim about yourself

Same audit, pointed at your own CV and pitch. The rule is one line long: say what changed, not what adjective. I moved 40% of onboarding to self-service in nine months beats I am a passionate innovator — and it survives the interview question that follows it.

The adjectiveWhat changed (yours)
"I am a passionate innovator."
"Results-driven, with a hands-on approach."
"Experienced in digital transformation at scale."
The test: could a sceptical interviewer ask "how do you know?" and get an answer? If not, delete the line — an adjective on a CV is a question you have invited and cannot answer.

A. Record ⏺ — "so is this AI, or what?"

The question everybody actually asks, and the one most likely to produce either hype or a dodge. Select this line and record ⏺ three sentences: what it does, what the evidence is, what you have not tested. No adjectives you cannot defend.
