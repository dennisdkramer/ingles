Gonna, wanna, woulda and get

Three sounds that hide a word

Fast speech moves the same words into one sound

English speakers do not slow down for going to. Three common groups of words are said as one short sound, and the written words disappear inside it:

  • gonna(gə·nə) is going to — I'm gonna call the client.
  • wanna(wa·nnə) is want to — I wanna check one thing.
  • woulda is would have — I woulda called, but my phone died.

Two rules hold for all three. The to is already inside the sound, so it is never said or written twice. And the verb after the sound stays in its base form — except after woulda, which takes the third form (called, sent).

Write the full form in an email. The short sound belongs to speech and to messages between people who know each other.

gonna is going to, with a verb after it

The plan is a sound before it is a sentence

gonna = going to + a base verb. It is the plan in your mouth: I'm gonna call the client. We're gonna join the call later. She's gonna send the file.

Going to has two jobs, and only one of them becomes gonna. With a verb it is a plan: I'm going to call → I'm gonna call. With a place it is movement: I'm going to the office — no gonna, because the verb go is the whole point.

When both appear, the sentence carries both: I'm gonna go to the office. The plan is gonna; the movement is go to.

Never gonna to: I'm gonna to call says to twice.

I'm gonna to call the client.

I'm gonna call the client.

The action sits after now: at this moment the call is a plan, and gonna is the arrow that points to then.

wanna is want to, but only after I, you, we and they

The s on "wants" does not disappear

wanna = want to + a base verb: I wanna check one thing. Do you wanna join? I don't wanna wait.

The subject decides whether the sound is possible. After I, you, we, they, want has no ending and the sound is wanna. After he, she, it, the third-person -s from lesson 4 is there, and it is never swallowed: She wants to join — not she wanna join.

wanna takes a verb, not a thing. I wanna coffee is wrong; the verb want with a noun keeps its shape: I want a coffee.

In an email write want to. wanna belongs to the call, the chat and the corridor.

woulda is would have, and the verb takes its third form

Something that did not happen, said in one sound

woulda = would have + the third form of the verb. It describes something that did not happen: I woulda sent the file. The file was not sent, and the sentence says so without saying why.

The verb after it is the third form — called, sent, told, come — never the base form and never the past form. woulda call and woulda came are both wrong.

In writing the same words are would have or would've. woulda is the sound of speech, and it sits beside the other two sounds on this page.

I woulda call you.

I woulda called you.

Three verbs to keep by ear until the full list arrives:

get carries a dozen daily meanings

What comes after get decides what it means

get is the verb English uses where Portuguese reaches for several different verbs. The word after it decides the meaning, and the pattern is short enough to keep in one picture.

With a thing, get means receive or obtain. With an adjective, it means become. With the idea, it means understand. With home or there, it means arrive.

Four meanings, one verb — and the listener hears which one from the word that follows.

get + an adjective is the change, not the state

The adjective is where you arrive; get is the movement

get + an adjective means become. The adjective names the state; get says you are moving into it. English uses this pattern where Portuguese uses ficar: I get tired after lunch. Get ready — the call starts in five minutes. It gets late fast in winter.

The adjective never turns into a noun after get: get tired, get ready, get better, get late.

I am tired describes the state you are in now. I get tired after lunch describes the change that arrives every day. The first looks at the state; the second looks at the movement into it.

The subject can be a person or a situation: I get ready, it gets late. The adjective does not change.
