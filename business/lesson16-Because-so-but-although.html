Because, so, but, although

One word decides which clause carries the reason

The same two facts, in two orders

Every explanation is two clauses: one carries the cause, the other carries the result. The connector decides which one comes second. Because puts the reason after the result: We moved the meeting because the client is travelling. So puts the result after the reason: The client is travelling, so we moved the meeting.

The facts do not change. What changes is where the listener's attention lands — on the decision or on the reason. That is the whole skill of a status update.

That's why does the same job from the other direction: it points back at a reason already given and names the result after it. The client is travelling. That's why we moved the meeting. Three sentences in a meeting, and the argument is complete.

Portuguese maps this well — porque and então sit in the same places. The cost is not the concept: it is the comma, and the habit of saying the reason second.

Contrast: but, although, however, though

Four words, four positions

But joins two equal halves and takes a comma before it: I like the plan, but the timing is bad. Although makes one half subordinate and can open the sentence — and when it opens, a comma follows its clause: Although the deadline is close, the plan is clear.

However is not a joiner. It opens a new sentence and takes a comma after it: The timing is bad. However, the plan is clear. It belongs to writing — a report, an email — and in speech it sounds like a document read aloud.

Though at the end of a sentence is how people actually say it: It's a good plan. Tight timing, though. Same meaning as but, and it lands after the fact instead of before it.

The error to kill now: Although the deadline is close, but the plan is clear. One connector does the job — although already carries the contrast, so but is a second one saying the same thing. Portuguese does this with embora… mas; English never does.

While contrasts two things more softly than but, and it usually opens the sentence: While the plan is good, the timing is bad. It is the word to reach for when both halves are true at once.

While has a second life as during the time that: While the client is travelling, we can test the app. The same word, and the clause after it tells you which job it is doing — a state that lasts, or two facts set against each other.

The order a status update runs in

first, then, finally — and the two that are not steps

A status update is a sequence, and the sequence words are the bones of it. First opens: First we tested the app. Then continues: then we loaded the numbers. Finally closes: finally we sent the deck to the client. The same three words carry a release, which is why they are the ones a standup runs on: first we test, then we deploy. Three words, one short report, and the listener always knows where they are.

Also adds one more thing without moving the sequence: We also tested the app, or after be — The app is also ready. It never opens a sentence in careful writing, and in speech it sounds like a note added late.

One connector per pair, and where the comma goes

Two rules that decide most of the mistakes

The first rule: one connector per pair of clauses. Although the deadline is close, but the plan is clear has two, and English keeps one. Choose the word that carries the position you want and let the other half stand alone.

The second rule: the comma follows the clause, not the word. When the opening clause is the subordinate one, the comma comes after it: Because the client is travelling, we moved the meeting. When the same information comes second, there is no comma at all: We moved the meeting because the client is travelling.

But takes a comma before it, always. So and then take one when they open a clause. However takes one after it, because it is an adverb standing at the front of its own sentence.

In speech, because and so both open sentences with no trouble at all: So we moved it. Because the client is travelling. Careful writing avoids the opening so, and nothing else on this page changes.

A route told in connectors

Where the route ends, and why it goes that way

18. You are going to the building where you return books and borrow new ones. From the station, turn right into High Street, then take Mill Lane because the café is shut today. Turn left into Low Street — the park is closed, so the walk is longer — and the building you want is on your left, next to the bank. Tick it on the map.

Three connectors carry that route: then moves you a step, because explains a detour, and so names what follows from it. Without them the same directions are a list; with them they are a way to go.
