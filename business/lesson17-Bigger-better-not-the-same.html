Bigger, better, not the same

One syllable adds -er, and spelling is the only surprise

The rule, then the three exceptions in writing

A short adjective compares by adding -er: fast → faster, cheap → cheaper, small → smaller. The comparison is finished by than, and than is what tells the listener the second thing is coming: This plan is cheaper than the last one.

The word that finishes the pair is the one people get wrong. It is than — not that, not of. Cheaper that is not English, and the ear of a client hears it immediately.

The new version is faster and easier. Two comparatives, one sentence, and no than in sight — because nothing is being compared to a second thing. Than appears only when the second thing is named or understood.

The spelling rules are the same three from the past tense — doubled consonant, y to i, dropped e. One set of habits, two endings.

than, as…as, and the other direction

Three ways to put two things side by side

than finishes an unequal comparison: This plan is cheaper than the last one. As … as says the two are equal: The new version is as fast as the old one — and the negative says they are not: It is not as fast as the old one.

Less points the other way: This option is less expensive. It is correct and it is formal; in a meeting most people say cheaper. What less cannot do is attach to a short adjective that already has -er: less cheap is not English, because cheaper already carries the direction.

Long adjectives take "more", and three words refuse the rule

From two syllables up, the comparison moves outside

A long adjective does not take -er. English puts more in front of it and leaves the adjective alone: expensive → more expensive, useful → more useful, important → more important. It is more expensive, but more useful — two clauses, four words of comparison, and not one ending changed.

The line between short and long is not exact: a few two-syllable adjectives accept both. When it is a judgement call, more is the safe choice for anything of two syllables or longer.

Three words refuse both rules and are learned as three pairs: good → better, bad → worse, far → further. This is the best option we have. The second proposal is worse than the first.

The superlative: one of a group, and only one winner

-est, most, and the "the" that comes with them

A superlative puts one thing above the whole group, so it takes the: the cheapest option, the most useful deck, the best answer. Short adjectives take -est and follow the same spelling rules as -er: cheap → cheapest, big → biggest, easy → easiest. Long ones take the most.

The irregulars keep their shape: good → the best, bad → the worst, far → the furthest.

Three errors live here, and all three come from doubling something that is already complete. More better doubles the comparison. The most cheapest does it twice in the other direction. And one of the best manager forgets that one of the always opens a plural: one of the best managers.

A fourth, smaller one: the superlative without the — It is best option. If the group is named, the the is not optional.

Four gaps, four different shapes: more with a long adjective, -er with a short one, not as … as for a soft negative, and the best for the one winner in the group.

The two halves in turns two and three are the pattern of the whole page: one comparison takes something away, the next gives something back. More expensive … but easier. That is how a recommendation is built in a meeting.

Notice Mike never says "the cheapest". He is not ranking a catalogue; he is comparing two proposals, and a comparative is enough.
