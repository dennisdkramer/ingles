Films and series: understanding fast dialogue

The words do not change, the sounds disappear

Two things fast speech does

Fast dialogue removes sounds; it does not use new words. Two changes account for most of it. First, two words join into one sound: going to becomes gonna, want to becomes wanna, kind of becomes kinda, and what are you arrives as whatcha in a question like Whatcha doin'? Second, a sound inside a word or phrase disappears: doing loses its final g, and get out of becomes get outta.

Two quieter habits complete the picture, and they are what a line with no fast word still hides. Unstressed words lose their vowel: for, and, of and to arrive as a short sound with no clear vowel of their own, so a lot of is heard as a lotta, got to as gotta, sort of as sorta, and because as 'cause. And a word that ends in a consonant joins the vowel after it: an hour, pick it up and turn it off each arrive as one unbroken sound.

One rule keeps gonna and wanna in their place: they stand only where going to or want to is followed by another verb. I'm going to call you becomes I'm gonna call you; I'm going to the store never becomes I'm gonna the store, because the to in that sentence belongs to the place, not to a verb.

These are sounds, not spellings. In an email you write going to and want to, and gonna in writing reads as a note from a friend. A subtitle file and a film script both use the full form, and that is why the line you hear never matches the line you read.

A learner who reads the subtitle first hears less, not more: the eye fills in the sound before the ear has had to work it out, and the next scene arrives with the same habit.

The four words behind most of it

The going(go·ing) in gonna: the final letter is written, never said.

The want(want) in wanna: the t disappears between two vowels.

kind(kind) of becomes kinda, because of reduces to a single sound.

The doing(do·ing) in doin' loses the same final letter as going.

The got in gotta: the t joins the vowel after it, and the to is swallowed whole — I've gotta go is I have got to go.

The sort in sorta: of shrinks to one vowel, so the two words arrive as one, and sorta keeps the t the phrase would lose in careful speech.

The out in outta: get out of becomes get outta, and the f of of never sounds.

The be in 'cause: because loses its first syllable when the sentence is already moving, and a script prints because anyway.

The what in whatcha: what are you arrives as two sounds, and the r of are disappears with the vowel it needed.

The same loss happens to a whole family of -ing words in a scene: callin', talkin', workin', waitin'. The letter is on the page and absent from the sound, in every one of them.

A warning that costs marks in an exam: the key above shows what a speaker does, never what a writer does. Written work uses going to, want to, because and doing in full, whatever the film sounded like.

The list is short on purpose: learn these, and the rest of a fast line becomes ordinary English at speed.

Why the dictionary teaches them

gonna and wanna have their own dictionary entries, because they are their own sounds: a learner who knows going and to still may not recognise gonna at speed.

The same is true of the small phrases around them: you know, I mean, look, come on. They carry no new information; they mark where the speaker is in the sentence. A subtitle skips them and the ear must keep them.

That is also why a dictionary entry for gonna is worth its space: the word has no spelling an English speaker would guess from going to, and no grammar of its own. It is a sound with a job, and the job is the same as the full phrase.

A scene, line by line

The small phrases carry the tone

look gets attention before a disagreement. you know refers to something both speakers know. I mean clarifies what was just said. come on pushes back on something unreasonable. no way states disbelief. whatever dismisses the topic. my bad is an informal apology. long story means the explanation is too long for now. I'm out means the speaker is leaving.

None of these phrases carries the information; each one carries the speaker's position. A scene is understood when the ear follows both: what happened, and where the speaker stands.

Two of them belong to speech between friends, not to a client email: my bad and I'm out. At work, say that's on me or my mistake, and I'm heading off.

The same phrases carry a whole register: a scene between friends runs on them, and the same exchange in an office drops most of them and keeps the tone. Come on, you know I hate waiting becomes I'd rather not wait — could we start on time? The information is identical; the distance between the speakers is not.

A third small set marks the end of a topic rather than a position: anyway, so yeah, moving on. They are the signposts of a conversation, and a listener who hears them knows a new idea is coming before the new idea arrives.

Watch a scene twice with that in mind: the first pass for what happened, the second for which of these markers each speaker uses and when. The markers repeat far more often than the vocabulary does.

In a series the same speaker uses the same three or four markers for a whole season, so a listener who learns one character's set hears that character clearly in every episode.

None of them is a mistake to be removed from a transcript: each one tells you where the speaker stands, and that is often the only way to hear whether a sentence is a joke, a warning or an apology.

The ear needs the scene, not the script

A line is understood from three things at once: the words, the situation and the speaker's face. Reading the subtitle replaces all three with one, which is why a series watched with subtitles in Portuguese trains almost nothing.

Watch one scene with English subtitles, then the same scene with none. The second pass is the lesson; the first one is the map.

A sentence is caught in pieces, not in one go: the subject, then the verb, then the ending that says what kind of sentence it is. Pausing after each piece is a slower way of hearing, and it is how the ear learns to run the pieces together.
