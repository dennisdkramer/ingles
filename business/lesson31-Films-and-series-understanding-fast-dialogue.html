Films and series: understanding fast dialogue

The words do not change, the sounds disappear

Two things fast speech does

Fast dialogue removes sounds; it does not use new words. Two changes account for most of it. First, two words join into one sound: going to becomes gonna, want to becomes wanna, kind of becomes kinda, and what are you arrives as whatcha in a question like Whatcha doin'? Second, a sound inside a word or phrase disappears: doing loses its final g, and get out of becomes get outta.

Two quieter habits complete the picture, and they are what a line with no fast word still hides. Unstressed words lose their vowel: for, and, of and to arrive as a short sound with no clear vowel of their own, so a lot of is heard as a lotta, got to as gotta, sort of as sorta, and because as 'cause. And a word that ends in a consonant joins the vowel after it: an hour, pick it up and turn it off each arrive as one unbroken sound.

One rule keeps gonna and wanna in their place: they stand only where going to or want to is followed by another verb. I'm going to call you becomes I'm gonna call you; I'm going to the store never becomes I'm gonna the store, because the to in that sentence belongs to the place, not to a verb.

These are sounds, not spellings. In an email you write going to and want to, and gonna in writing reads as a note from a friend. A subtitle file and a film script both use the full form, and that is why the line you hear never matches the line you read.

Why the dictionary teaches them

gonna and wanna have their own dictionary entries, because they are their own sounds: a learner who knows going and to still may not recognise gonna at speed.

The same is true of the small phrases around them: you know, I mean, look, come on. They carry no new information; they mark where the speaker is in the sentence. A subtitle skips them and the ear must keep them.

A scene, line by line

The small phrases carry the tone

look gets attention before a disagreement. you know refers to something both speakers know. I mean clarifies what was just said. come on pushes back on something unreasonable. no way states disbelief. whatever dismisses the topic. my bad is an informal apology. long story means the explanation is too long for now. I'm out means the speaker is leaving.

None of these phrases carries the information; each one carries the speaker's position. A scene is understood when the ear follows both: what happened, and where the speaker stands.

Two of them belong to speech between friends, not to a client email: my bad and I'm out. At work, say that's on me or my mistake, and I'm heading off.

The ear needs the scene, not the script

A line is understood from three things at once: the words, the situation and the speaker's face. Reading the subtitle replaces all three with one, which is why a series watched with subtitles in Portuguese trains almost nothing.

Watch one scene with English subtitles, then the same scene with none. The second pass is the lesson; the first one is the map.
