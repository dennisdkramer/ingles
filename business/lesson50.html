Your English, in the room

What this page is: the last page of the track, and a performance, not a review. Nothing new is taught; nothing here is scored — the graded assessment was lesson 40's and this is not it. What you produce today is the evidence that the other forty-nine pages exist: a ninety-second uninterrupted answer with a structure inside it, a register you can switch on purpose and switch back when the client walks in, the candid register understood including its cost, clarity ahead of idiom when the room is international, a one-page recommendation that survives being forwarded, and a plan for the errors that are yours alone — three patterns, three drills, one habit, thirty days. Fluency is not speed. It is not stopping.

2. The three registers, and the switches that show them

Register is not a feeling; it is a set of visible switches. This chart is the spine of the page — everything else points back to it.

RegisterThe switchesThe same messageThe room it belongs in
business-casualno contractions · full forms · we · neutral verbs · a name in the greeting"We need to discuss the timeline on Thursday. Is 10:00 suitable?"a client, a director, a written follow-up
peer-to-peercontractions on · phrasal verbs · first names · short sentences · hey instead of dear"Got a sec to talk about Thursday?"a colleague, the corridor, the team chat
candid informaleverything on, plus slang and — where the relationship earns it — a swear"Thursday's a mess. We need to sort it."a peer you trust, at lunch, off the record
The professional skill is not one register; it is the flip — on and then back, mid-conversation, without losing the thread. The flip back is the move most learners cannot make: the client walks in, the sentence changes shape in the same breath. That is what marks a professional, and it is what this page trains.

3. The candid register — strengths, and what each one costs

This register is core content, not decoration. It is the language of the corridor, the lunch, the private channel — and every item below is a decision with a price. Recognise, understand, choose: three verbs, and the third one is the skill.

The itemStrengthWho you may say it toWhat it costs
"That's a mess."
"We're screwed if the cert doesn't land."
"Hell of a week."
"That's bullshit."
"That's cooked."
"wtf" (written only)
The two strongest — bullshit and wtf — are recognised here and not deployed: bullshit says the claim is false and you are annoyed, which is a message about a person's work; wtf exists only in writing and only in a channel where nothing is ever forwarded, which is a channel that does not exist. Below them, in order: we're screwed (serious trouble, peer only), that's cooked (finished, and mildly amused — younger registers), hell of a week (solidarity, almost always safe), that's a mess (a description anyone may hear).

Same two people, ten minutes apart, one on each side of a door. Listen ▶ to both, then mark where the switches happen — and find the flip back.

A. Six clarity rewrites

Idiomatic → international. Slower, shorter, fewer idioms — and write the number down.

1. "Thursday's cooked."
2. "The cert's a no-go."
3. "We'll park it and touch base."
4. "That's a ballpark figure."
5. "It's in the pipeline."
6. "We're on the back foot."

B. The switch-points

Name the switch and which direction it went. Six of the eight — the last one is the flip back.

1. "Thursday's cooked" → "the Thursday timeline"
2. "no chance" → "unlikely"
3. "the cert" → "the certification"
4. "we'll" → "we would like to"
5. "I'll have it with you by five" →
6. "see? told you"
Which one was the mistake? Only one of those eight switches is a problem, and it is not the last one — the quiet "see? told you" is a legitimate flip back between peers who share the room. The mistake is the opposite case: the flip into candid while a client is still on the line. Knowing which one you did is the whole skill.

5. Said — the ninety-second turn

The prompt: "Tell me about a project that went wrong, and what you did."
The clock: ninety seconds. No notes.
The structure inside it: where we were → where we are → what I would do next.

An answer with a structure and two hesitations in it is worth more than a fluent list. The repairs from lesson 44 run inside the turn rather than ending it — let me start that again, what I mean is — and the clock keeps running.

7. The plan — three errors, three drills, one habit

At level 50 the remaining errors are personal and they are permanent unless named. This replaces a grade the page is not allowed to give: three patterns, three drills, one habit, thirty days.

A pattern with a name can be caught while you are speaking; a pattern described as "my grammar" cannot be caught at all. And the drill must be a sentence you can say in four seconds, or it will not survive a real Tuesday.
