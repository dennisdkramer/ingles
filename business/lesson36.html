The Story and Its Five Beats

What this lesson installs: you can tell the same piece of work as a fifteen-word CV line, a thirty-second answer or a two-minute interview story — and you can hear which beat an answer is missing. The law is that a story is a shape, not a chronology: hook → set-up → complication → climax → resolution, five beats, each answering one question. The second law is that the tense is the signpost: the continuous paints the room, the simple past walks through it, the past perfect reaches back over the shoulder, the present perfect says why it matters now. One mapping to fix on the way in: Portuguese por fim / finalmente is three different English words — at last after a wait, in the end after everything, eventually sooner or later — and de repente is suddenly, never repentantly. By the end of the page you tell your own project story at three lengths and drop nothing that was doing work.

2. A story is a shape

Five beats, one question each. A list of what happened in order is chronological order (ordem cronológica) — it is not a story, because it has no tension and nothing to release. The shape is what a listener follows. Listen ▶ to one line per beat.

BeatThe question it answersWhat it sounds likeWhat it must not be
1. hook (o gancho)Why should I keep listening?Nine days before go-live, our main supplier pulled out. a summary of the whole story — that spends the reason to listen
2. set-up (o cenário)Who are we with, and where are we?I was running the launch for a retail client. We were three weeks out and I had two people. a CV — job titles, dates, no situation
3. complication (o problema)What went wrong?They pulled out, and the material was already at the printer. a complaint with no stake in it
4. climax (o momento da decisão)What did you do?I split the launch in two and sold it to the client in one call. a list of everything the team did
5. resolution (o desfecho)What changed?We lost four per cent of the orders and kept the account. "…and that was it."
Vocabulary of the shape: hook (gancho) · set-up (cenário) · complication (o problema que aparece) · climax (o momento da decisão) · resolution (desfecho) · protagonist (quem conta — aqui, você) · chronological order (ordem cronológica) · audience engagement (o quanto o outro quer continuar ouvindo) · reach-back (voltar no tempo, com o past perfect) · at first (no começo — e promete que mudou) · by the time (quando chegou o momento em que) · in the end (no fim de tudo). Each one is used below, not just listed.

4. Four tenses, four jobs

One story, four tenses, no competition between them. Each form has a job the others cannot do. Listen ▶ across the row, then use the chart to check your eight answers above.

TenseIts job in the storyOne line of this storyWhat it sounds like if you use it here instead of the right one
past continuousthe wallpaper — what was already going onI was running the launch for a retail client. I ran the launch — the scene becomes a fact on a CV
past simplethe events — each one finished, in orderThe supplier pulled out. I split the launch in two. I was splitting the launch — nothing ever gets done
past perfectthe reach-back — before the story startedThe material had already gone to print. The material went to print — it lands inside the story and the listener waits for its consequence
present perfectthe point it makes nowI have kept a second supplier ever since. I kept a second supplier — the lesson becomes history, not policy
The tense is the signpost, and the listener reads it. Getting the tense wrong is what makes an account sound like a report instead of a story — the theme of lesson 34, now in speech. Rule of thumb: one reach-back per story, and only when the listener needs it. A flashback is one clause, not a new section.

7. One story at three lengths

Same project, three sizes. Length is a choice, and the choice is what gets cut. Read the three, then decide what each length threw away.

The CV line (fifteen words). Split a nine-day supplier failure into a two-phase launch; lost four per cent of orders, kept the account.
o que ficou: o problema, a ação e o resultado. Nada mais.
The thirty-second version (networking). I was running a launch for a retail client when our main supplier pulled out nine days before go-live. I had already printed the material, so I split the launch in two: the part that didn't need them went out on the date, the rest three weeks later. We lost four per cent of the orders and kept the client.
o que ficou: os cinco beats. O que saiu: pessoas, reuniões, sentimentos.
The interview answer (a hundred and forty words). I was running a launch for a retail client — three weeks out, two people, and a print run already paid for. Nine days before go-live the main supplier withdrew. At first I assumed it was a date problem; that's when I saw the e-mail. I had already gone to print, so the choice was to move the date or split the launch. I split it: the part that didn't depend on them shipped on the date, and the rest three weeks later. I sold that in one call, because the client needed a launch, not a supplier. In the end we lost four per cent of the orders and kept the account, and I have kept a second supplier on every launch since.
o que ficou a mais: a cena, a hesitação, o que mudou depois.
