I've worked here for five years

A period that started in the past and is still open

have or has + the past participle

The present perfect is have or has plus the past participle: I have worked, she has been, we have used. It names a situation that began at a point in the past and is still true at the moment you speak.

The past simple and the present perfect differ in where the period ends. I worked there for five years says the five years are over. I have worked there for five years says they are not: you are still there. The past simple closes the period; the present perfect leaves it open.

Portuguese uses the present tense for this meaning — Trabalho aqui há cinco anos — so a word-for-word translation gives I work here for five years, which an English speaker hears as a habit, not as a length of time. The perfect is the form Portuguese does not use in that sentence.

have goes with I, you, we, they; has goes with he, she, it. The verb after it is the past participle, and it takes no second ending: she has worked, never she has works.

The two words divide the work, and that is why the tense holds together in a long sentence: have carries the person and the time, and the participle carries the action. Because the tense is already on have, nothing after it changes for the person — he has worked, they have worked, one participle in both.

Most participles end in -ed and are the same shape as the past form — worked, used, joined. The ones that are not are the ones to keep in memory: be → been, go → gone, do → done, write → written, take → taken, grow → grown, know → known. Using the past form in their place — I have went, I have wrote — is the error a listener notices first.

What the line shows

The span starts five years ago and runs through now: the period has not closed, so the sentence uses the perfect.

The same words in the past simple draw a span with two ends: it began five years ago and it stopped when you left. Nothing on that line reaches now, which is why I worked there for five years and I am still there cannot both be true.

since draws the same open line and marks only where it starts: since 2021 puts a date at the left end instead of a length on the span.

Read the two lines together and the English is visible: the same sentence, the same length, two different endings — and the tense is decided by the ending alone.

The then arrow is not decoration: it is the part of the sentence that says the reader should expect more.

A note on how it is actually said: American English often drops for before a length — I've worked here five years, she's been in that role two months — and never drops since. Writing for is correct at every level and is what a careful email uses; hearing the sentence without it is normal.

Where a span stops is the only difference between the two lines, and it is the whole difference between the tenses: for five years counts the same length on both, so the words that carry the meaning — for and the number — are identical. Only the end of the line moved.

for a length of time, since a starting point

for takes a length — for five years, for two months, for a decade. since takes the point where the time started — since 2019, since March, since Monday. for answers how long; since answers from when.

We have used this system for a decade. She has been in the role since March. The length and the starting point describe the same period, so a sentence takes one or the other, never both: since five years and for 2019 are both impossible.

Two questions to ask about a sentence

Is the situation still true now? If it is, the sentence takes the perfect. Does its time word give a length or a starting point? That answer picks for or since.

Run the two questions on a real update: We've had the same supplier for a decade. Still true? Yes, so the perfect. Length or starting point? A decade is a length, so for. Change one fact — We changed supplier in 2014 — and both answers change: the period is closed, so the past simple, and a date needs no for at all.

Nothing else in the sentence changes. There is no did, the participle keeps the form it has, and nobody adds -ed to it twice.

A question moves only have: Has the team grown since January? — the participle stays exactly where it was.

ago belongs to the other side of the line. It counts back from now and it takes the past simple — I left two years ago — so I have left two years ago is the one mistake the word always exposes.

Written out, the pattern is short: have/has + participle + for + length, or have/has + participle + since + the point it started. Nothing else moves.

One fact from the other end

The same length can be reported from either end. One sentence counts the years — I've been here for five years — and the other names the moment the period began and says how long ago it was: It's been five years since I joined. Both sit in the perfect, because in both the period runs up to now.

since takes a clause as easily as it takes a date: since I joined, since we moved to this floor, since the new manager arrived. The clause reports an event that is finished, so it takes the past simple, while the main clause stays in the perfect because the period is not finished: It's been two months since we last reviewed the contract.

A status update often chooses this frame for what it implies. It's been three weeks since the client last replied reports a length and adds, without complaining in words, that the silence has run long enough to notice.

The frame has a negative, and it is the one a polite message uses to soften a complaint: It hasn't been long since we last spoke says the gap is short, so nothing is wrong. It has been and It's been are the same sentence; the contraction is the one a person says, and a formal note writes it out.

Four of these verbs have a participle that is not the past form: been, grown, known, taken. Stay, join and live add -ed and keep it.
leavestop working somewhere, or go away from a place — past left, participle left — sair, deixar

Time words that include now

up to now starts at some earlier point and stops at this moment. this year and this month name a period that has not finished. All three include the moment of speaking, so all three go with the perfect: Up to now, we've had no complaints. I've hired two people this year.

The opposite time words close the period — last year, in 2021, yesterday — and those take the past simple. The time word chooses the tense, and the tense is not a matter of taste.

have works twice. As a main verb it means own: we have two suppliers. As the perfect's first word it carries the tense: we have used two suppliers this year.

so far behaves like up to now and sounds more like a status update: So far, we've had two complaints. this morning belongs to the same family while the morning runs — at 11 a.m. it takes the perfect, and after lunch the same words take the past simple, because the period has closed.

recently and lately are the loosest pair in the list: they name a period nobody has closed, so We've hired two people recently is the normal form, while We hired two people last month names a month that ended.

How long have you been ...? — the interview sentence

The question and the shape of the answer

The question puts how long in front with the perfect: How long have you been at the company? The answer gives the length with for or the starting point with since: Five years — I've been here since 2021.

How long are you at the company? asks about a habit, not a length, and an interview is where that sentence is heard most. The perfect is what makes the question ask about duration.

Most answers use be or a working verb: I've been in the role since March, I've worked here for five years, I've lived here for ten years. She's been sick since Monday works the same way: the sick period started Monday and has not ended.

The question has a past form too, and it asks about a period that has already closed: How long did you work at your last company? — Four years, until last spring. The answer to how long have you been is a length still running; the answer to how long did you work is a length that stopped when you left. An interview asks both, and the tense tells the listener which company is being discussed.

Two smaller questions sit beside it. Since when? asks for the starting point alone and takes a date or a clause: Since March, Since the merger. How long ago? asks about the gap after something ended, and it takes the past simple: How long ago did you leave? — Two years ago. Reaching for the perfect in that last question — How long ago have you left? — is the mistake this pair exists to prevent.

An interview rarely stops at the first answer, and the follow-up is where the tense is tested again. And what did you do before that? takes the past simple, because the job is over. How long have you been in this field? takes the perfect, because the career runs to today. A candidate who answers both in the same tense has answered one of them wrongly, and an interviewer hears it.

What changes and what stays

The person stays the subject: I have worked keeps I, and the participle never moves to the front. Only have or has stands before it.

A length needs no perfect when the period is closed: I worked there for five years — that was 2015 to 2020. With the period open, the perfect is the form.

At work the sentence appears in three places: an interview, a status update about something still running (We've used this supplier for a decade), and a colleague's question about a person (How long has she been on the team?).

The same length can be asked about a colleague without naming them: How long has the position been open?, How long have you been waiting? — the pattern is how long + have/has + the person or thing + the participle, whatever the verb is.
