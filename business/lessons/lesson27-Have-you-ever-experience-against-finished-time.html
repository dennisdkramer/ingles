Have you ever? — experience against finished time

The time expression chooses the tense

Two periods, two tenses

The present perfect looks at a period that is still open: I've sent the report says the report is with the reader now. The past simple looks at a period that is closed: I sent the report yesterday puts the sending inside yesterday and leaves it there.

The time expression decides which one you need. Unfinished time includes the moment you speak — ever, never, yet, just, recently, so far, this week — and takes the perfect. Finished time names a period that is over — yesterday, last year, five minutes ago, in 2023 — and takes the past simple.

Portuguese já covers both: Você já terminou? can be Have you finished? or Did you finish? The English choice is made by the time frame, not by the word já, and that is the trap this lesson exists to close.

The trap has a second half. Já also means already, and for that meaning English uses the perfect too — I've already sent it. But the moment a sentence names a finished period, the perfect becomes impossible: I've sent it yesterday is a sentence an English speaker cannot finish. The perfect's period has to reach the moment you speak, and yesterday has ended before it.

A note from the American office: I already sent it, I just saw him and Did you eat yet? are all normal in speech in the United States, where a recent event often takes the past simple. The perfect is the form to write and the form to use in a status update; the past simple is what you will hear across the desk.

Three more places the choice shows

News and experience both take the perfect, because neither names a closed period: The client has confirmed the date, I've worked with this tool twice. Add last week or five minutes ago and the same fact moves to the past simple.

So far counts from the beginning up to now, and it always brings the perfect with it: So far, nobody has complained.

A finished period inside a question behaves exactly the same way: Did the client sign on Friday? names the day, so the past simple; Has the client signed? leaves the day unsaid, so the perfect. Same word order, two different questions, and the time word is the whole difference.

A promise about the future is often built on the perfect: I'll send it as soon as I've checked the totals, Call me when you've spoken to her. The checking finishes first, so by the time the sending happens it is already past — which is exactly what the perfect says.

Notice what none of these pairs do: they never name a finished period and then ask for the perfect. I've sent it last week is not a mistake of style, it is a sentence with no tense it can use, and a reader stops at it.

Every one of them is answered by the same question, so it is worth saying in one line: has the time the sentence talks about finished, or is it still running?

Where already, just and yet stand

The place of each word

already, just, recently, ever and never stand between have and the participle: We have already paid the invoice, I have just sent it. recently may also end the sentence: We've moved recently.

yet stands at the end, and only in a negative sentence or a question: We haven't finished yet, Have you finished yet? A positive sentence does not take yet.

already in a question asks about surprise rather than information: Have you already finished the Q2 numbers? says the work was expected to take longer than it did. yet in the same question is neutral: Have you finished the Q2 numbers yet? asks whether the work is done and carries no view on how long it should have taken.

been and gone are two different participles. She has been to Berlin means she went and came back. She has gone to Berlin means she is in Berlin now. The first is a visit; the second is a position.

Why the position is fixed

The participle and its object form one unit — paid the invoice, sent it — so the short time word cannot sit between them. Put it before the participle and the unit stays whole: have already paid the invoice.

I have sent already it breaks that unit, and an English speaker hears the break before hearing the meaning. Yet is different: it qualifies the whole sentence, so it stands outside it, at the end.

still and yet both say the work is not done, and they are not interchangeable. I still haven't finished puts still before the negative and sounds impatient; I haven't finished yet puts the fact at the end and sounds neutral.

already never goes in a negative: We haven't already paid says the opposite of what the speaker means, and the negative sentence takes yet or drops the word altogether.

The time word may also close the sentence when it is not yet: I've sent it already is heard everywhere, and I've sent it just now mostly in speech. The middle position is the one that is never wrong.

Two questions, two answers

Have you ever worked with this tool? asks about your whole working life, up to now. The answer is Yes, I have or No, I never have, and it cannot use yesterday.

Did you work with this tool at your last job? asks about a closed period. The answer is Yes, I did, and it can take every finished time word.

The short answer keeps the negative too, and the two denials are not the same: No, I haven't leaves the door open, and No, I didn't says the period is over.

One warning about the answer

A short answer repeats the first word of the question: Have you finished? — Yes, I have. Did you finish? — Yes, I did. Using did after have is the mistake that makes the answer sound borrowed from another sentence.

In a meeting the safe short answer is the same word the question used, and nothing more.

The rule covers the negative as well: Haven't you finished? — No, I haven't. Didn't you finish? — No, I didn't. Mixing the two is heard as a different sentence rather than a slip, because have and did place the event in different periods.

A status question, a correction, and a request

Three ways the choice appears in a workday

Experience: Have you ever worked with this tool? Nobody names a date, so the perfect is the form.

News: I've just sent it tells the listener what is true now. Finished time: I sent it five minutes ago gives the moment and closes the subject.

A correction: a colleague says I've sent it yesterday. The time word is finished, so the sentence becomes I sent it yesterday — the perfect cannot carry a closed period.

When the sentence names no time at all, the perfect is the safer form, because an open period is what the listener assumes: I've sent the deck says where the deck is now. Name the moment and the sentence switches: I sent the deck at four o'clock. That is the whole decision, and it takes one question — has the time the sentence talks about finished?

A request leans on the same question from the other side. Could you send it when you've finished? waits for a piece of work that is not done yet, so the perfect; Could you send it when you finished? sounds like a complaint about something overdue. The polite form of the same request is Have you had a chance to look at it? — a question that leaves the period open and the listener free.

A follow-up call shows both tenses in two lines, which is why the pair is worth practising together: I sent the summary on Tuesday. I've had no answer since. The first sentence names a closed day; the second counts from it to now.

Nothing in the choice depends on how long ago the event was: I've just sent it and I sent it three days ago can describe the same week.
