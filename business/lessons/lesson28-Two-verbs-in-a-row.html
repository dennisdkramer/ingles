Two verbs in a row

The first verb decides the form of the second

One tense, two verbs

When two verbs stand together, the first one decides what the second looks like: We decided to move the launch, I suggest checking the numbers first. Only the first verb carries the tense and the person; the second is always in the base form after to, or in the -ing form.

The group is part of the verb, and meaning does not predict it. decide and suggest both name a plan, and one takes to while the other takes -ing. A dictionary entry gives the pattern with the word, so the verb and its pattern are learned together.

Portuguese uses the infinitive after both kinds of verb — decidi mudar, sugiro verificar — so the -ing group has no counterpart to copy. Those verbs are memorized.

manage means to succeed at something difficult, and it takes to: I managed to finish it on time says the finish was not certain.

The mistake heard most often in a meeting is I suggest you to check or I suggest me to check. suggest takes no person between the two verbs: I suggest checking the numbers, or I suggest that we check the numbers.

The reason that mistake is so common is that English has two frames here, not one. Some verbs put a person between themselves and the second verb, and those take to: I asked him to check the figures, we need her to sign it, they expect us to deliver on Friday. Others take the second verb directly, with nobody in between — suggest, avoid, mind, enjoy, finish — and there to never appears. I suggest you to check is the first frame built around a verb that belongs to the second.

When a person has to be named after suggest, the sentence uses a clause instead: I suggest that you check the figures, or I suggest you check the figures, with that left out. The person stands in a clause, never between the two verbs.

A pattern inside the lists, useful as a memory hook rather than a rule: the -ing group is mostly verbs that react to an activity — enjoy, avoid, mind, suggest, finish — and the to group is mostly verbs pointing forward at something not yet done — decide, offer, promise, hope, manage. Where a new verb fits neither picture, the dictionary entry decides.

stop, forget and try: the form changes the meaning

Three verbs that take both endings

stop + -ing ends an activity: I stopped taking the bus. stop + to pauses in order to do something else: I stopped to take a call.

forget + -ing means the action happened and you do not remember it: I'll never forget meeting the client. forget + to means the action did not happen: I forgot to send the invoice.

try + -ing tests a solution: Try restarting the system. try + to attempts something that may be too much: I tried to call you, but the line was busy.

remember works exactly like forget, and the two are worth learning as a pair: Remember to book the room warns about a task not yet done, while I remember booking the room says the booking happened and you still recall it.

start takes either form with almost no difference: It started to rain and It started raining describe the same moment.

One sentence, two facts

I stopped to check the numbers. I paused in order to check them.

I stopped checking the numbers. I no longer check them.

I forgot to send the invoice. The invoice never went out.

I'll never forget sending the invoice. It went out, and I remember it.

Try restarting the system. Test that solution.

I tried to restart the system. I attempted it, and it may not have worked.

The pattern behind all three pairs: the -ing form looks at an activity that happened, and to looks at an action still to be done. That is why forget + to is always a task nobody started, and forget + -ing is always a memory of one that happened.

Building the sentence at work

The order to build in

The pattern is fixed once the first verb is chosen, so build the sentence in that order: the subject, the first verb with its tense, the second verb in its form, then the rest. We decided to move the launch. She offered to take the minutes.

Two of these verbs run a whole exchange. suggest puts a proposal without a person between the verbs: I suggest checking the numbers first. mind asks about a small inconvenience: Do you mind waiting five minutes? The answer is No, not at all or Sorry, I can't right now.

avoid is the polite refusal of an action: I'd rather avoid booking the room until we know the date. It never takes to.
