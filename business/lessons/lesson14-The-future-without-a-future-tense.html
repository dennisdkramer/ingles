The future without a future tense

Three forms, and one line

O inglês não tem tempo futuro. Tem formas que apontam para a metade direita da linha, e a escolha entre elas diz o quanto a coisa está firme: I'm gonna review it tonight é plano; I'll review it é decisão tomada agora.

The right half of the line

English has no future tense. It has forms that point at the right half of the line, and the choice between them says how fixed the thing is. I'm gonna review it tonight is a plan already in your head. I'll send it now is a decision made this second, in the conversation. I'm meeting the client at ten is an appointment that already exists in a calendar.

gonna is going to, spoken — the same form, cut short, and it is what a room sounds like. The verb after it stays in base: gonna review, gonna send, never gonna reviews. The past of a plan is was gonna or were gonna: I was gonna call you, but the day went.

The arrow leaves review and lands past now, on the light half of the line. A plan is a place on the line, not a mood.

Four ways to the future: decided, decided now, booked

A forma é escolhida por quando a decisão aconteceu: um plano que já existia antes da conversa é gonna; uma decisão tomada dentro da conversa, ou uma promessa, é 'll; o que já está na agenda é o simple present.

Four forms, and what each one promises

The form is chosen by when the decision happened. A plan that existed before the conversation is gonna. A decision made inside the conversation, or a promise given in it, is 'll. An appointment that already has a time in the calendar is the present continuous. And a timetable that runs whether you are there or not is the present simple.

This is why I'm gonna send it and I'll send it are not the same promise. The first says the decision is already made; the second says you are making it now, in front of the person who asked.

What "I'll" is actually promising

'll faz três trabalhos, e os três acontecem na conversa: a decisão tomada enquanto você fala (I'll check and write to you this afternoon), a promessa (I'll send it today), e a oferta.

Three jobs, one form

'll does three jobs, and all three happen in the conversation. A decision made as you speak: I'll check and write to you this afternoon. A promise: I'll send it today. An offer: I'll call the client — you are busy. The listener hears one thing in all three: the commitment is new, and you are giving it now.

Using 'll for a plan you made last week is not a grammar error. It is an information error: it tells the listener the decision is new, and they will ask you again tomorrow.

The negative is won't — will not said as one word: I won't be in the office tomorrow. It is a promise in the other direction, and it is heard as one.

is also the word for a refusal: The client won't move the date — not "does not want to and cannot", but "will not, and that is the answer".

won'twill not, written as one word and said as one syllable. It is the negative of a promise, and the word for a refusal that is not going to change.

The time words that do the pointing

A palavra de tempo costuma fechar a frase: I'm gonna review it tonight. Também pode abrir, com vírgula depois: Tonight, I'm gonna review it. O que ela não pode é se esconder no meio.

Where the time word sits

The time word usually ends the sentence: I'm gonna review it tonight. It can also open it, with a comma after: Tonight, I'm gonna review it. What it cannot do is stand between the verb and its object — I'm gonna review tonight it is not a sentence.

The form and the word work as a pair. Now and right now pull 'll, because the decision and the word arrive together. Tonight, tomorrow and next week work with any of the three forms, and the form is what tells the listener which one you mean.

By Friday is the one to read carefully. It means at some point before Friday ends — not on Friday, and not after it: I'll send it by Friday. A promise with by in it is the promise a manager writes down.

Soon and later are the opposite of a promise: they name no moment at all. I'll do it soon is what somebody says when they are not committing to a date, and everyone in the room hears that.

The timetable says it, not you

O simple present é a quarta forma, e é o que as pessoas duvidam: The call starts at nine tomorrow está correto, porque a frase não fala do futuro — fala da agenda.

The form that belongs to a schedule

The present simple is the fourth form, and it is the one people doubt. The call starts at nine tomorrow is correct, and the reason is that the sentence is not about your intention — it is about a timetable that exists whether you are in the room or not. The present simple is the form of facts, and a schedule is a fact about tomorrow before anybody decides anything.

The same is true of transport and openings: The train leaves at seven. The office opens at eight. None of these is a plan of yours, so none of them takes gonna.

Three forms, three owners. A timetable belongs to the calendar: present simple. An appointment belongs to two calendars: present continuous. A plan belongs to you: gonna. A decision belongs to this conversation: 'll.

The trap: the present simple used for a personal plan sounds like a habit. I start at nine is what you do every day; I'm gonna start at nine is what happens tomorrow.
