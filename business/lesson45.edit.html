The document that survives forwarding

What this lesson installs: a professional document is read twice, and the first read is not a read. Somebody skims four things — the subject line, the first line, the last line, the attachment name — and decides from those whether to open it properly. That is why the tone you learned in lesson 34 often never gets heard: it is buried under a wall of text. Two more things sit underneath. Format is a promise about effort: one idea per paragraph, and the first sentence of each paragraph is a contract that names it. And the report is an argument, not a log — purpose, findings, recommendation — because a document that stops at findings gets sent back.

1. Heard — the read-aloud test

A director opens an e-mail at 08:58, reads down four lines, and stops. Listen ▶ to what that sounds like from the outside — then decide what should have been line one.

"Hi all, I hope this finds you well and that the quarter is going as planned. Following our conversation last week and the various points raised in the steering meeting, and having now had the chance to speak with both the supplier and our colleagues in legal, I wanted to write to set out where we are with the delivery date, which as you know has been the subject of some discussion over the last few weeks, and to explain the background in a little more detail so that everyone has the full picture before the board meeting on Thursday, at which, as I understand it, the programme will be discussed in some depth, and I think it is fair to say that the position has changed since we last spoke, because the supplier has now confirmed that the certification will not be available until the nineteenth rather than the twelfth as originally planned, which has a knock-on effect on the annex that legal require, since that document normally takes five working days to review and we would now have four, and legal have indicated that they can work at risk if asked, though they would prefer not to, and so a decision is needed from the programme board as to whether we ask legal to work at risk or whether the bundle proceeds without the annex and we accept the associated risk, and I would be grateful for a view before Thursday. Kind regards, Sam."

The director, at line four: "Sorry — what do they actually want?"

A. Which sentence should have been line one?

1.Pick the opener.
2.Why does the wall of text fail?
3.Which sentence, anywhere in the paragraph, is the actual point?
4.The paragraph is 190-odd words and contains how many ideas?
What you just heard is the whole lesson: the writing is grammatically clean, polite, and completely unusable. Nothing in it is wrong; the problem is order. A reader who cannot find the request in four lines does not read line five — they reply "can you summarise?", and the document has now cost two round trips instead of one.

2. The four lines that are the document

Not decoration, not the wrapper around the content — for the first read, these four are the content. A reader decides from them whether the other 180 words exist.

RoleWhat it must containWhat happens if it is missing
subject linethe decision or the fact, plus the deadline — "Decision needed Thu: annex in bundle?"the mail is filed, unread, behind nine others
first linethe same thing in a sentence, with the ask — "We need a decision by Thursday."the reader hunts; some readers do not hunt
last linethe action and the date again, plus who does what — "Please confirm by Wednesday 12:00."the mail ends in a wish, and no action follows
attachment namewhat it is and its date — UAT-pack_2026-05-19_v3.pdfthe attachment is re-sent, or the wrong version is signed off
A busy reader reads these four and nothing else. That is not laziness — it is how anybody survives ninety e-mails. If the four do not carry the message, the message does not arrive.

4. One idea per paragraph

A. Six topic sentences

Each line says what the paragraph is about. Write the opening sentence that names it — a contract, not an introduction.

1. the cert moved from the 12th to the 19th
2. legal need five days, we have four
3. two options, and only one keeps the date
4. what we recommend, and why
5. what is not affected by any of this

B. The cut list — keep or cut?

Eight sentences from the §1 paragraph. Write K or C: does it carry a fact, or is it throat-clearing?

1. "I hope this finds you well."
2. "The certification will not be available until the nineteenth."
3. "Following our conversation last week…"
4. "Legal normally take five working days to review the annex."
5. "…so that everyone has the full picture before the board meeting."
6. "A decision is needed: legal at risk, or ship without the annex."
7. "I think it is fair to say that the position has changed."
8. "Please confirm by Wednesday 12:00."
What the cut list teaches: throat-clearing is not random. It is (a) greeting, (b) the history of the conversation, (c) the announcement that you are about to say something, and (d) the narrator's opinion of the facts. Together they are usually a fifth of the document — and not one fact leaves with them.

5. The report shape

An e-mail is a message; a report is an argument. The difference is the third section — a log stops at findings, which is exactly why logs get sent back with the words "and?"

SectionWhat it containsWhat happens if it is missing
purposewhy this document exists, in one sentence — "To decide whether legal work at risk."the reader spends the first paragraph working out what they are reading
findingswhat is true, with numbers and dates, no opinionthe recommendation is unsupported, and becomes a preference
recommendationwhat should happen, who does it, by whenthe document is a description, and the meeting happens twice
BLUF — bottom line up front — is a placement decision, not a style: when the reader is senior and the reasoning is short, the recommendation goes first and the findings justify it afterwards. When the reasoning is long or contested, purpose first, findings, then recommendation.

6. The passive, chosen on purpose

The form is lesson 29's. What this page adds is the judgement: the passive earns its place when the doer is unknown, irrelevant, or the reader; it hides when the doer is you or your team. Same grammar, opposite ethics.

The sentenceEarns it or hides?Why
"The certificate has not yet been issued."
"A decision was taken to move the date."
"You were copied on the thread on the fourteenth."
"The annex will be reviewed before the board."
"Mistakes were made in the original estimate."
"The bundle can be shipped without the annex if required."
The test is one question long: does this sentence protect somebody, or protect the writer? The certificate has not been issued protects nobody unfairly — the issuer is a system. Mistakes were made protects exactly one person, and every reader knows who. Write earns or hides, then say why in six words.

A. Name the doer

Two of the sentences above hide. Rewrite them with the person in the subject — it will feel less comfortable, and that is the point.

7. Salutation and closing: a machine, not a mood

These pair. They are not two independent choices, and a mismatch — Hey Sam above Yours faithfully — is the fastest way to look like you do not know the room.

You open withYou close withAnd it means
Dear Mr Smith,Yours sincerely,you know the name; formal, external
Dear Sir or Madam,Yours faithfully,you do not know the name; formal, external
Hello all,Thanks / Best regards,a group you work with; neutral
Hi Sam,Best / Thanks,you know them; internal or warm external
Sam,—a thread that is already running; no ceremony at all
Notice the pattern: the opening carries how well you know them, the closing carries how much distance you are keeping. Once both are chosen, the rest of the document has a register to live in — and the reader can hear it in the first two words.

A. Fix two mismatches

1. "Hey Sam," … "Yours faithfully," →
2. "Dear Sir or Madam," … "Thanks!" →
Cada par diz uma coisa só: quanto te conheço, e quanta distância estou a manter.

9. One notice, three house voices

House voice is repetition done on purpose, and it lives in exactly three decisions: sentence length, whether it says "we", and how it names the reader. The same notice from three companies:

The houseHow it soundsThe three decisions
a bank"From 1 June 2026, payments made after 16:00 will be processed on the following business day. No action is required from you."long clauses · no we · the reader is you, addressed formally
a startup"Heads up — from 1 June, anything you send after 4pm goes out the next day. Nothing for you to do."short clauses · we implied by the tone · the reader is you, informally
a consultancy"We are changing our cut-off time. We will process payments received after 16:00 on the next business day, and we will confirm each one in writing."medium clauses · we throughout · the reader is barely named at all
All three are correct, all three say the same thing, and a customer of each would recognise their own. That is what house voice is for: consistency is the promise, not the personality. Your job across the three documents this afternoon is the same one — keep one voice while the reader changes.

A. Rewrite the notice in your house voice

Same fact — the handover moves from Tuesday to Wednesday — for the partner whose house style is nothing like yours. Six sentences maximum; write it as one voice, not a mixture.

Escolhe as três decisões antes da primeira frase: comprimento, "we", e como nomeias o leitor.
