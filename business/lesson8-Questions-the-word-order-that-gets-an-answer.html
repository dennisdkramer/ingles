Questions: the word order that gets an answer

The mode goes first in a yes-or-no question

Pergunta de sim ou não: o indicador de modo (do, does, did) — ou o modal, ou o be — vai para a frente, e o verbo principal volta à forma base. Do you work here? · Does she work here? — o -s viaja para o does.

Move the mode indicator, and the main verb goes back to base

A yes-or-no question moves the mode in front of the subject. The mode is do, does, or a form of be — the same place from lesson 5 that carries the tense.

The statement The report includes the numbers carries its -s on the verb. In the question the -s moves onto does and the verb returns to base: Does the report include the numbers?

be is already a mode, so it goes first by itself: You're ready → Are you ready? Never do you be ready.

The short answer echoes the mode: Yes, it does. No, it doesn't. Yes, I am.

Do you work from home?

The mode stands first; work has no ending; the subject sits between them. That is the whole form of a yes-or-no question.

The wh-word comes before the mode

A palavra de pergunta vem antes do indicador de modo: Where do you work? · When does it start? Primeiro o fato que falta, depois o indicador de modo.

The question word names the missing fact, then the mode follows

A wh-question has four parts in a fixed order: wh-word + mode + subject + base verb. When does the meeting start? — when names the missing time, does is the mode, the meeting is the subject, start stays base.

The question word asks for one kind of fact, and the answer fills that hole: Where do you work? — At the office. Why does she call? — To check the numbers. What do you need? — The report on your desk.

be questions keep the same order with no do: What's your name? Where is the bus stop?

The most common one at work asks about the clock: What time does the call start?

Who wants coffee? When who is the subject, there is no do

Quando a palavra de pergunta já é o sujeito, o do não entra: Who wants coffee? · Who called? — nunca Who does want coffee? O -s fica no verbo principal, porque nada se moveu.

The mode moves nothing when the question word is already the subject

In most questions the mode goes first because the subject is after it. But when who or what is the subject, the sentence has nothing to move: it keeps statement order and takes no do.

Who wants coffee? — who is the subject, so wants keeps its -s and no does appears. Never Who does want coffee?

When who is the object, the subject is somebody else and the mode comes back: Who does she call? — she is the subject; who is the person she calls.

Who does want coffee?

Who wants coffee?

how long asks for a length of time, and which asks you to choose

how long pede uma duração (How long does it take?), e which pede uma escolha (Which file?). As quatro palavras desta seção parecem iguais e pedem respostas diferentes.

Four question words that look alike and answer different things

how long asks for a duration, not a clock time: How long does it take? — About an hour. when would ask for the clock (When does it start?); how long asks for the length.

how alone asks for the way: How do you check the numbers? — With the new system.

which asks you to choose from a set the other person already knows: Which day is better, Monday or Tuesday? what leaves the answer open: What do you need?

A tag turns a statement into a question

Uma tag no fim transforma a afirmação em pergunta: ..., right? — o "certo?" do inglês, dito com a entonação subindo. Nada muda na frase; só entra a palavra no fim.

right? is the short tag that asks for agreement

A tag is a short question at the end of a statement. It invites the other person to agree or correct. In American business speech the most common one is right?: You're coming, right?

The tag sits after a complete statement and works the same after be, after do and after a plan: The file is on your desk, right? You work from home on Fridays, right? We're meeting on Monday, right?

The voice does the rest. Falling right? means I think so — confirm it. Rising right? means I am not sure — tell me.

A tag is not a second question: right? stays short and neutral after any statement.

  1. AnaYou're coming to the client call, right?
  2. MikeI am. Nine o'clock, right?
  3. AnaNine thirty. The room is on the second floor, right?
  4. MikeIt is — the small one.

Where questions, answered on the map

Where pede um lugar, e o lugar pode ser um ponto na rua: at the bank, on Market Street. A resposta repete a preposição da figura.

Where asks for a place, and a place can be a point on a street

Where asks for a place: a room, an address, or a point on a map. Where do you work? — At the office on Market Street. Where is the bus stop? — On the corner.

A where question can also ask about the use of a place rather than its position: Where do you take a letter? The answer is a place you know, not a compass direction.
