Same Day, Two Rooms

What this lesson installs: nothing new — every verb here was taught in lessons 18, 22 and 23, and the job today is retrieval in a room you did not learn it in. The law is that the particle carries the meaning: up completes, out exits or solves, off separates or postpones, on continues, through completes against resistance, into investigates — so an unseen verb is still half-readable. The second law: the single word is not better English, it is a different room — phrasal verbs are spoken, the Latin word is written, and both directions are decisions of register (o nível de formalidade). One mapping to fix on the way in: Portuguese dar um retorno is not give a return; it is get back to you — and resolver is sort out in the room and resolve in the report. By the end of the page you tell one day twice: once in the 9 a.m. stand-up, once in the 5 p.m. report.

1. Heard — the thirty-second stand-up

Eleven phrasal verbs in half a minute, at speed. Listen ▶ once and do two things: catch what is happening with the supplier, and count the phrasal verbs you hear. Both are asked below.

Marco: "Morning. So — the supplier's gone quiet, we'll look into it today and I'll get back to you by lunch. The design review's been put off to Thursday, which is fine, we can sort out the open questions before then. Priya came up with a workaround for the certificate, so we'll set it up in staging this afternoon and carry out a full check on it. If it holds, we wrap up early and I'll write it up for the client tonight. Anyone need to bring anything up?"

A. Catch and count

1.What is he going to do about the supplier?
2.What happened to the design review?
3.What did Priya do?
4.What happens if the fix holds?
The eleven, in the order he said them: gone quiet · look into · get back to · put off · sort out · came up with · set up · carry out · wrap up · write up · bring up. They arrive at about one every three seconds, and nobody slows down for you — which is why the next section is about the particle rather than the list.

2. The particle is the meaning

3. The pronoun settles it

If the object is a pronoun, it goes inside: sort it out, never sort out it. When you are unsure of a verb, inside is almost always safe — and the few that refuse it are the ones worth naming out loud.

Collocation is the difference between translated and spoken. Wrong nouns are what make otherwise fluent speech sound assembled — the grammar is fine, the verb is right, and the sentence still lands as Portuguese in English clothes. Learn the verb with its noun from the first day, the way you learn a name with a face.

4. The two-way conversion

The same day, twice. In the stand-up everything is short: a verb plus a particle. In the report the same event needs the single word, the passive, and no personality. Convert each line, then read the register note — because the note is what you are actually learning.

DirectionThe lineYour conversionWhat changes
spoken → written"We'll look into the supplier's silence."look into → investigate; the agent can stay
spoken → written"We've put the review off to Thursday."put off → postpone; note the date survives
spoken → written"Priya came up with a workaround."came up with → proposed / devised
spoken → written"We'll carry out a full check this afternoon."already formal — but the report prefers the passive: a full check will be carried out
written → spoken"The matter is being investigated."the passive has no owner; the room needs one — I'm looking into it
written → spoken"The review has been postponed until Thursday."drop the auxiliary, keep the date: we've pushed it to Thursday
written → spoken"The issue was resolved by the engineering team."resolved → sorted out, and name the person: Priya sorted it
written → spoken"No decision has yet been taken."no decision → we haven't decided, and say when you will
The rule under the table: the single word is not better English — it is a different room. A report full of sort out reads as a chat; a stand-up made of investigate and postpone reads as a memo being read aloud. The escape hatch works in both directions, and the skill is knowing which room you are standing in.
The passive problem, in one line: the report's passive removes the person, which is exactly what a written record wants and exactly what a meeting cannot survive. In the room, somebody owns the verb — I'll look into it. In the report, the verb owns itself — the matter is being investigated. Same fact, opposite needs.

5. One day, told twice

Same events, same facts, same person. Read both and mark what changed: the verbs, the owners, the pronouns, and the length of the sentence.

A. 9 a.m., the stand-up

"So the certificate's still not back, we're looking into it, I'll get back to you by lunch. Review's been put off to Thursday — fine, we'll sort out the open questions before then. Priya came up with a workaround and we'll set it up in staging this afternoon, then carry out a full check. If it holds, we wrap up early and I'll write it up tonight."

Verbos com partícula, sujeito em "we" e "I", frases curtas, duas palavras de ligação, nenhum passivo.

B. 5 p.m., the client report

"The outstanding certificate has not yet been received; the matter is being investigated and a response will be provided by midday. The design review has been postponed to Thursday, which allows the open questions to be resolved in advance. A workaround has been proposed and will be configured in staging this afternoon, after which a full verification will be carried out. Subject to a successful result, the engagement will be concluded earlier than planned and a written summary submitted this evening."

Palavra única, passivo, nenhum dono, frases longas, zero contrações.

A. Mark the differences

1.In B, who owns the investigation?
2."A response will be provided" corresponds to which stand-up line?
3.Which version would you send to a director?
4.Which version would you say out loud in the room?
5.B is not "more correct". It is…
6.The one thing B adds that A does not have is…
The trap: learners who have just learned phrasal verbs start putting them in e-mails, and learners who have just learned formal writing start saying the matter is being investigated in a stand-up. Both are the same mistake in opposite directions — the vocabulary is right and the room is wrong.

6. The jargon audit

Not every phrasal verb carries meaning. Reach out, touch base, circle back, take it offline, deep dive, move the needle are fashion with a half-life of about two years: an international audience decodes them slowly and a plain verb does the work better. Mark each one keep or replace, and write the plain version.

The phraseKeep or replace?Your plain version
"Let's touch base next week."
"I'll reach out to the supplier."
"Can we circle back on the budget?"
"We'll look into the complaint."
"Let's take this offline."
"We need to wrap up by four."
The answer key is a judgement, not a rule: touch base → talk / speak; reach out → contact / call; circle back → return to; look into → keep (it carries meaning and everyone decodes it); take this offline → discuss it separately; wrap up → keep. The test is not age or elegance: does the phrase say something a plain verb cannot? If not, it is decoration, and decoration slows a reader down.
