Two frames, one fact

Reporting what someone said needs one of two verbs, and the verb decides what follows it. say takes the words and no person: She said the launch was delayed, she said that the launch was delayed. tell takes the person first and the words after: She told me the launch was delayed, never she told the launch was delayed.

She said the launch was delayed.

He told me the date had changed.

The pair is the same fact twice, and the marks show the difference: after told a person stands between the verb and the news, and after said the news begins immediately. That person is required in one frame and impossible in the other, which is why the mistake is heard so often.

Why the pair is hard from Portuguese

Portuguese separates the same two ideas with disse and contou, but the person is optional after both: ela disse (para mim) que o lançamento atrasou, ela me contou que o lançamento atrasou. English fixes the person to one verb only, so a speaker who translates the person across puts it after said and produces she said me the launch was delayed.

The repair is mechanical: if a person comes first, the verb is tell; if the news comes first, the verb is say. Nothing else changes. She told us the client had agreed and she said the client had agreed report one fact, and only the first says who was in the room.

A note on writing: say is the safer verb for a report or a mail, because it needs no audience — the supplier said the part would arrive Friday. tell names who was told, which is worth saying when the audience matters: the supplier told our buyer the part would arrive Friday.

Three consequences of the frame

The person after tell is a pronoun or a name, never a prepositional phrase: he told me, he told the team, never he told to me.

say can carry the words exactly: she said, "the launch is delayed". tell cannot take a quotation: a quotation needs say, while a report takes either verb.

Both verbs accept that, and both drop it in speech: she said that the launch was delayed and she said the launch was delayed are the same sentence.

One more pair belongs to the same family and is worth separating now: speak takes to and names a conversation, not the words — I spoke to the client says a conversation happened, while I told the client says what she was given. A sentence with spoke and no to is the commonest of the three errors.

One step back, and when the step is skipped

The tense moves back one step

When the reporting verb is past, the words inside the report move one step into the past. This is backshift: is → was, will → would, can → could, has sent → had sent, sent → had sent, do → did.

The reason is the one the perfect already taught: the sentence holds two times, and English keeps them apart. The reporting happened then, and the words were spoken before that, so the tense inside the report steps back to show which time is which.

One pair shows the whole move: "The launch is delayed," she said becomes She said the launch was delayed. The present became the past, and the report now sits behind the moment she spoke.

One step, not two, unless the meaning asks for two: she said the launch had been delayed reports a delay that was already finished when she spoke. Each extra step says something extra, so the step is chosen rather than automatic.

What a report keeps, and what it moves

Two lines, two decisions about the tense

The opening of a song does this lesson's work twice in a few seconds. The singer has been told something and passes it on — that is a report — and the two lines take different decisions about the tense inside it.

The first line keeps the present: I heard that you're settled down. The fact is still true at the moment he sings, so the report does not step back. The second line reports a fact that had already finished before he heard it, so the tense stays in the past: that you found a girl — and the same line ends in the present again, and you're married now, because the marriage is still true.

I heard that you're settled down.

So the rule has two halves, and this verse carries both: a report moves a present into the past when the fact itself has moved, and it leaves a fact alone when the fact has not — because it is still true, or because it was already in the past.

hear works exactly like say: it takes the clause and no person. I heard that you're settled down. When the person is the source of the news, the verb takes from: I heard from Ana that the date had moved.

hear has three shapes and each one changes what follows: I heard the news takes the thing itself, I heard from Ana takes the person who got in touch, and I heard that the date had moved takes a clause. Only the third is a report, and only the third moves the tense back.

Three questions cover the whole rule: is the fact still true (is), was the event already over when the news arrived (had found), and is the promise still ahead (would remember)? A report that answers all three is right in all three places.

Reporting a report is the same move one step further back. The singer's line, passed on to someone else, becomes he said he'd heard that you were settled down: the first report is already in the past, so the second one steps back from it rather than from your name and your address. This is why office messages so often carry two pasts — she told me they had been informed.

The end of the same line is not part of what he heard. And you're married now is the singer's own knowledge, added after the report and left in the present, so the sentence runs a reported fact and a comment side by side and the tenses keep them apart. A report that stops before the comment is still a complete sentence: I heard that you found a girl stands on its own, and the comment is what the singer adds once the report is finished.

The verbs that carry a report

Seven verbs that take the same frame

All seven take a clause and no person of their own, exactly like say: mention, explain, add, admit, announce, claim, point out. He mentioned the delay, never he mentioned me the delay. What changes is the reporter's attitude toward the news, and that is why a careful writer picks one of them instead of said.

mention is the lightest: something said in passing, without a meeting around it. When the listener is named, it follows to — she mentioned the delay to me. explain carries the reason, so it takes that or a wh-clause: he explained that the file had been lost, he explained why the date had moved.

add puts a second fact after the first: she said the date was fixed and added that the budget was not. admit reports something the speaker did not want to say: they admitted the order had shipped late. announce reports news to a group: the director announced that the office would close on Friday.

claim reports something the speaker has not checked: the supplier claims the part left the warehouse on Monday. point out reports a fact the listener had missed: she pointed out that the contract had already expired. Those last two are the ones to keep apart — claim doubts, point out corrects.

The five phrases divide by how much the speaker stands behind the news: according to puts the weight on a named source, I've been told takes none, apparently and as far as I know admit the gap, and in fact corrects what was assumed.

In writing they take different positions. According to and as far as I know open the sentence (as far as I know, nobody has signed); apparently may open it or sit inside; in fact almost always follows the claim it corrects, with a comma or a semicolon. A report that puts in fact first has nothing to correct yet.

The words that point at the speaker's moment

Time words move with the report

A quotation carries words that point at the moment it was spoken — today, tomorrow, here, this. The report is read at a different moment and from a different place, so those words move too: today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, yesterday → the day before, last week → the week before, here → there, this → that.

The reason is the one behind the backshift: the sentence keeps two moments apart. Leave tomorrow in a report read three days later and the sentence points at the wrong day, which is how a relayed deadline becomes a missed one.

A note on come and go: reported movement follows the reporter's position as well. "I'll come to your office," he said becomes he said he would go to my office, because the movement is toward the reporter now. bring and take move the same way.

The words move in a small, closed set, and every one of them is worth knowing before it is needed: here → there, this → that, these → those, now → then, today → that day, ago → before. A report that keeps here was made in the same room; one that says there was not.

The exception is the one worth remembering: a word that describes something permanent does not move. He said the office is on the second floor keeps is, because the floor has not changed since he spoke.

That exception and the backshift rule are the same rule seen twice: the tense follows the fact, and the time word follows the moment of reading.

Passing it on in an office

One fact, two tellings

The relay is the most common report at work: a manager hears something, and a colleague hears it second. The second telling carries both changes at once — the verb steps back and the time word moves — and it adds the source, because the listener now needs to know who said it.

Three habits make the relay clean. Name the source with say or according to: the client said the launch was delayed, according to the client, the deadline is final. Keep the person with tell when it matters who was in the room: she told the whole team the date had moved. And when the news is uncertain, mark it with apparently rather than passing a guess on as a fact.

The order matches the meeting: what was said, who said it, and what it means for the plan.

A relay that skips the source is the one that causes trouble: the date has moved arrives as a fact, and when it turns out to be a guess, nobody can say where it came from. Apparently the date has moved costs one word and keeps the sentence honest.

Second-hand news also has a habit of losing its tense on the way. The client said the launch is delayed; the relay says the launch was delayed; the third telling says the launch had been delayed — and by then the listener cannot tell whether the delay is over. That is why the relay repeats the words that anchor the time, and why as far as I know is worth saying out loud.

Written down, the relay is the paragraph a status email opens with, and the same three parts appear in the same order.
