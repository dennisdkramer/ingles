The English sentence: who, then the verb, then the rest

English names who first, and never drops the name

Three parts, in a fixed order

An English statement has three parts in this order: who → the verb → the rest. The "who" is the subject, and English never leaves it out.

Trabalho para uma empresa brasileira. shows the trap. The Portuguese verb trabalho already contains eu, so a Brazilian does not say it. English verbs do not contain the person — work is the same word for I, you, we and they — so the person has to stand in front of the verb as a pronoun: I work, never a bare work.

The rule has one consequence you can use from today: before you say anything, choose the subject. I, you, he, she, it, we, they — or a name, or a thing, like my brother or the report.

I'm an analyst.

We work for a Brazilian company.

The marks name the job each word does: the subject, the mode (the small verb that carries the person), the verb, the object. Read the two lines again and watch the order never change.

Portuguese order and English order look almost the same here, and "almost" is what costs a beginner the sentence: the one word Portuguese drops is the word English requires.

Am an analyst.

I'm an analyst.

One verb for two: English "be" covers ser and estar

Portuguese splits be in two; English does not

Ser says what something is. Estar says where it is, or how it is for now. English gives both jobs to one verb, be, and changes only its form: am · is · are.

So Sou analista and Estou no escritório both start with a form of be: I'm an analyst. / I'm at the office. One verb, one decision — which form belongs to the subject.

1. I an analyst.

In speech, in chat and in most email the contracted form is the normal one: I'm, you're, he's, she's, it's, we're, they're. The full form is for emphasis, or for a very slow and formal sentence.

There are only three forms, and they never change with time. If a sentence seems to need a fourth, it is not be that is missing — it is another verb, and the next sections show how to spot that.

be links two things; it does not put an action in time

Three jobs, one verb

  • Identity — who someone is, what something is: I'm an analyst. · He's a developer.
  • Place — where someone or something is: She's at the gym. · They're here.
  • State — how something is right now: We're out of coffee.

In all three, be is the sign between the subject and what is said about it. That is why it is called a linking verb: it links, and it describes no action.

This is the difference that decides whether you need be at all. Work is an action, so it needs no be: We work for a Brazilian company. not We are work… If the verb already does something, be stays out.

The arcs are the point: I'm an analyst has no start and no end on the line. It is true at now and it is true either side of it.

A form of be says nothing about when. I was an analyst and I'm an analyst differ in one letter, and that letter is the only thing putting the sentence in the past. On its own, am · is · are means "this is how it is", and the sentence needs no time word to be true.

That is also why Estou trabalhando cannot be built by putting be and a verb together in this lesson: the -ing form is a different machine, and it comes later in the course.

Two more things the same verb does, both of them useful in the first minute of a meeting:

  • Nice to meet you. — a fixed phrase, said back the same way: Nice to meet you too.
  • I'm Ana. — a name after be; no article before a name, ever.

Compare with the job, which does take an article: I'm Ana. I'm an analyst. The next sections explain why.

Fast speech is where the object form slips: a Brazilian speaker replaces it with the subject form and produces *I called she. After a verb it is always me, you, him, her, it, us, them.

One more use you will hear in every meeting, and it is safe to copy: Send it to me. · I sent it to them. The object follows the preposition, so the same forms come back.

my, your: the owner stands before the thing

The possessive takes the article's place

my = meu, minha. your = seu, sua, teu, tua. It stands before the noun it owns, and it replaces the article: my name, never the my name.

Portuguese keeps the article — o meu nome — and English does not. That single extra word is the most common written error of a Brazilian beginner, and it is easy to hear.

my and your never change with the thing owned: my job, my name, my friend — one form for masculine and feminine, one form for singular and plural.

The my name is Ana.

My name is Ana.

The rest of the family works the same way, and you will hear them in the first meeting: his (dele), her (dela), its (de uma coisa), our (nosso, nossa), their (deles, delas).

Pick the phrase English uses

1. ___ job is at the bank.

2. Is this ___ name? — you are asking the person in front of you.

3. Bruno is ___ friend — he is a friend of mine.

4. Ana and ___ brother work here.

One test answers all four: the word before a noun is the owner, and the owner needs no article in front of it. If you can say o meu in Portuguese, drop the o in English.

A job is one thing, so it takes a or an

English marks one countable thing with a before a consonant sound and an before a vowel sound: I'm an analyst. · He's a developer. Portuguese says sou analista with nothing there; English puts the article in.

The choice is made by sound, not by spelling: an analyst, an hour (the h is silent), a university (it starts with a y sound). At this level, a and an before a job is the case you need most.

A name takes no article at all: I'm Ana. — never I'm an Ana.

The six sentences this lesson is built on

  • I'm an analyst. — identity, with the article.
  • He's a developer. — someone else, so is.
  • She's at the gym. — place. Na academia.
  • We're out of coffee. — a state: the coffee is finished. out of = sem.
  • They're here. — place, plural subject, so are.
  • We work for a Brazilian company. — no be: work is the action. work for = trabalhar para.

Say each one out loud once with the contraction and once in full. The contraction is what a colleague hears; the full form is what a form asks for.

What the line shows

Now I'm a believer.

Three of this lesson's rules stand in five words. I is the subject, spoken and never dropped. 'm is am, contracted — the form a singer and a colleague both use. And a stands in front of one countable noun, which is exactly what Portuguese leaves out in sou crente.

The line also shows why the article is there: believer is a person, one person, and be is naming what he is — the same job as I'm an analyst. The word before it, her face, is a possessive from the last section: her before the thing owned.

The past form saw (vi) belongs to a later lesson. Leave it; take the shape I'm + a + noun.

Am, is or are: the subject picks the form

Six subjects, three forms, one rule: I → am · he, she, it → is · you, we, they → are. Nothing else decides it — not the job, not the place, not the time.

The subject picks the verb, and the rest of the sentence hangs off it. A sentence needs one verb of its own: I'm here and I work here are two different sentences, and only the first one has be.
