What you need to do, and what you have to do

Four forms, one slot

How strong is it, and who says so

Four forms fill the same slot, and the difference between them is strength and who imposes it. Must is the strongest, and it usually comes from a rule or from the speaker: You must sign in at reception. Have to is an obligation from outside, a fact of the world: We have to send it today — the client's deadline is nine.

Need to is the practical one: the task requires it, and nobody is ordering anybody: You need to update the ticket. Should is advice and nothing more: You should check the numbers again.

All four take the base verb and never change shape themselves: must sign, should check, need to update, have to send. No -s on the modal, no did in front of it, and nothing between it and its verb.

One that does change is have to, because have is an ordinary verb: she has to, we had to. Need to does the same: she needs to. The to stays for both.

Which strength does the situation need?

1. A rule of the building, written on the door.

2. A colleague asks whether the test is good. You have an opinion, not an order.

3. The client's deadline is at nine, and it is already eight.

4. The ticket is still open and two steps are missing.

mustn't, don't have to, and the difference that matters

Two negatives that are not the same word

Have to and need to are ordinary verbs with a to after them, so they take the endings the ordinary verbs take: she has to sign in, we had to wait, he needs to update the ticket. Must and should never change.

Their negatives are where the damage happens. Mustn't means it is forbidden — there is a rule against it. Don't have to means it is not necessary — nobody forbids it, nobody requires it either.

Não precisa covers only the second. Não deve covers the first. English keeps them apart with two different structures, and a manager who says you mustn't is not saying you don't have to.

You don't have to come — it is optional, and it would be nice. You mustn't come — do not come. Same subject, opposite world.

make surecheck that something is true or done: Make sure the file is sent today. Not a command to be careful — a task with a result you can verify.

It's better to is the softest rung below should: It's better to check twice than to explain once. It recommends without claiming authority, and in a room of equals it is often the only form used.

The register matters here. You must from a colleague sounds like a manager; from a manager it sounds like the building. Choose the rung, then the word.

The ladder of pressure

From a suggestion to a rule

Read the five forms in order and the effect is obvious: it's better to suggests, should advises, need to states a requirement, have to names an outside obligation, must closes the door. Each step adds pressure and removes a little freedom.

Most of the errors in this family are one mechanical mistake — a to where it does not belong, or an -s on a word that never takes one.

Must, should, can, could, will, would never take to and never take -s. Have to and need to are the opposite: they are ordinary verbs and behave like send — she has to, she needs to.

One more, and it is a register error rather than a grammar one: must used for advice. You must try the new screen sounds like a rule about a screen. Use should there, and keep must for what the building requires.

Where the visitor has to go

Obligations attached to a place

18. You have to be at the station at nine, and you cannot drive — the car park is closed for repairs. You need somewhere to wait with a coffee, and it must be the closest place, directly across Mill Lane from the station. Tick it on the map.

Four forms, four jobs, in one instruction. Have to is the timetable. Cannot is the car park. Need is what the hour requires of you. Must is the test you apply to the map — the closest place, and on the other side of the lane.

Five gaps and one printed example. Turn one is Jess using need to about her own task; the answers move up the ladder from there — have to, then must, then has to for Ana.

Turn six is the freedom: you don't have to wait. Turn nine is the prohibition: anything I mustn't do? Those two are the pair the page warned you about, sitting three turns apart in one briefing.

The last turn hands the answer to a shorter form — Don't share the deck. In speech the prohibition often arrives as an imperative, and it is heard exactly the same way.
