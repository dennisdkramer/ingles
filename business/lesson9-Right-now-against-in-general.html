Right now against in general

am, is or are + -ing means the action is in progress

am/is/are + verbo-ing é a ação em curso: I'm working = estou trabalhando. O be carrega a pessoa e o tempo; o verbo com -ing carrega a ação.

Be carries the person and the time; the -ing verb carries the action

The present continuous has two parts: am / is / are + -ing. I am checking, she is working, they are waiting. One part alone is not the tense: I checking and I am check are both broken.

It means the action is happening around now — it has started and it is not finished: I'm checking the numbers right now. She's waiting for the client.

The simple present is the other side: it states the habit or the fact, not the moment. I check the numbers every Monday.

Read the two lines together. The bar sits across now: that action is in progress. The arcs spread in both directions: that action is true every week.

Same verb, two forms, two meanings — and the line shows which one the sentence has chosen.

How the -ing form is written

Três grafias cobrem quase todo verbo: acrescenta -ing (work → working), tira o e final (live → living), dobra a consoante final (run → running).

Three spellings cover almost every verb

Most verbs add -ing and change nothing: work → working, wait → waiting, rain → raining, stay → staying.

A verb that ends in a silent e drops the e: live → living, write → writing, make → making.

A short verb that ends consonant-vowel-consonant doubles the last letter: get → getting, run → running, sit → sitting.

The spelling follows the sound: the doubled letter keeps the vowel short, and the dropped e was never said in the first place.

The same three rules will come back for every continuous form, so a verb written correctly once stays correct in every tense.

The same verb points at now, or at every week

O mesmo verbo aponta para agora ou para toda semana: I'm working now / I work every Monday. A forma é o significado — be + -ing é agora, o simple present é em geral.

The form is the meaning: in progress against in general

Both forms use the same verb. The form decides what the sentence says about time: I'm checking the numbers right now is this minute; I check the numbers every Monday is the routine.

Two more pairs to hear the difference: Costs are going up. (the change is happening now) against Costs go up in winter. (the pattern). Are you using the new system? (these days) against Do you use the new system? (at all).

The choice is made by what you mean, never by the name of a tense. Ask: is this happening around now, or is it true in general?

Temporary things use the same form: today, this week

O que dura alguns dias também usa be + -ing: today, this week, this month. É uma situação temporária, não um hábito de sempre.

A situation that lasts days still takes be + -ing

The continuous is not only for this second. A temporary situation takes the same form: I'm working from home this week. She's staying at a hotel this week. I usually walk, but today I'm taking the bus.

The time words tell you which form the sentence needs. today, this week and these days go with the continuous; usually, every Monday and never go with the simple present.

The contrast is the lesson: the same person, the same verb, two forms — and the time word says which one the situation needs.

still, usually and right now

Cada palavra de tempo escolhe a sua forma: still = ainda (she's still working), usually = geralmente (simple present), right now = agora mesmo (be + -ing).

Each time word picks its own form

usually, never and every Monday belong with the simple present: I usually walk. She never eats breakfast.

right now, today and this week belong with the continuous: I'm checking the numbers right now.

still says the action continues. It sits between be and the -ing verb: She's still waiting.

Verbs of knowing and wanting stay in the simple present

Verbos de saber, querer, precisar e gostar ficam no simple present: I know, I want, I need, I like — nunca I'm knowing. Um estado não progride, então não há meio de caminho.

A state does not progress, so there is nothing to be in the middle of

Some verbs describe a state, not an action: know, want, like, need, and have when it means own. They take the simple present even when the moment is now: I know the answer. She wants the file. We need more time.

The error to kill is I'm knowing and she's wanting. A state has no progress, so the continuous has nothing to show.

have is the one that changes: I have a car (own) is simple; I'm having lunch (eating) is continuous. The activity takes the continuous; the possession does not.
