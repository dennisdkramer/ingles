If I had the budget: the second conditional

The past form that does not mean past time

if + past, would + base

A second conditional describes a situation that is not true now: If I had the budget, I'd hire another analyst. The if-clause takes the past simple, the other half takes would + base form, and the past form marks the situation as unreal. It says nothing about the past.

Compare it with the first conditional: If I have the budget, I'll hire another analyst says the budget may come. If I had the budget, I'd hire another analyst says it has not come, and the speaker is imagining what would follow. One letter separates a plan from a daydream, and a listener hears the difference immediately.

Portuguese maps closely here: Se eu tivesse o orçamento, contrataria outro analista uses the imperfect subjunctive and the conditional, and English uses the past and would. The mapping is cheap, with one trap: the English past is not a past event, so if I had the budget last year is a different sentence and belongs to the third conditional.

would is never followed by to: I'd hire, never I'd to hire. With I, he, she, it the unreal be is were: If I were you, if she were here.

Two sentences, one budget

If I have the budget, I'll hire another analyst. The budget is possible; this is a plan.

If I had the budget, I'd hire another analyst. The budget is not there; this is an imagined present.

Advice and offers with the same form

If I were you

If I were you, I'd raise it in the meeting is the standard sentence for advice. It names the other person's position as imaginary and puts the speaker in it, so the recommendation arrives as a proposal instead of an order.

were is the unreal form of be with every person: if I were, if he were, if she were. If I was you is heard in speech and is not the form to write.

The same would makes a proposal: I'd offer two more weeks, we'd need a second tester. In a question it asks whether the proposal is acceptable: Would that work for you? The question is not about the future; it is about the other person's answer now.

If I were you, I'd raise it in the meeting.

Would that work for you?

Why the advice sounds polite

An order names the action: raise it in the meeting. The advice names a condition that is false — the speaker is not the listener — and so it offers rather than directs. The listener can answer I will or I'd rather not without either of them losing face.

The proposal sentence works the same way: we'd need two more weeks presents the consequence of a situation, not a demand on the listener. A negotiation is built out of these two moves.

The words for an imagined plan

Six words that frame a proposal

imagine asks the listener to picture a situation: Imagine we had two more weeks. suppose takes something as true for the argument: Suppose the client said no — what then? unlikely means probably not going to happen, and chance is the possibility that it does: There's a small chance the date moves.

worth says the cost or effort is justified: It's worth asking. An alternative is another option. in that case joins a decision to the situation just described: In that case, we'd need two more weeks.

Everyday words in the same shape

rent is the money paid to use a house or an office, and also the verb: we rent a small office. weather is the state of the air outside — the weather would not matter in a covered space.

holiday in American English is a public day off, like Thanksgiving or Christmas. Travel time is a vacation: If I had two weeks of vacation, I'd rent a house near the coast. A Brazilian speaker who says holiday for férias is understood, and sounds British.

A negotiation in two halves

The move that proposes without demanding

A negotiation uses the second conditional twice: once to put an unreal option on the table, and once to ask whether it is acceptable. If we moved the date to the 14th, would that work for you? The listener answers about the proposal, not about the past.

The reply often names the limit in the same form: If I had the budget, I'd add a tester, but we don't have it this quarter. The sentence offers a condition the speaker cannot meet, and the but keeps the proposal alive.

A trade closes the exchange: What if we shared one tester with your other team? — In that case, I'd say yes. Each sentence is a proposal, never a demand, and the decision is made by the person who owns the variable.
