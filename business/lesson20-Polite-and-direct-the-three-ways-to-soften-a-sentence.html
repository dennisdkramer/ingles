Polite and direct: the three ways to soften a sentence

One message, three levels of force

Every request carries a force, and English has three ways to lower it. Direct: Send me the file. Hedged: I think we need the file today. Soft: Could you send me the file when you have a moment? The request is the same in all three. What changes is how much room the other person has to say no.

Three changes do the work. A hedge — I think, maybe, probably, it seems — states an opinion where the sentence would otherwise state a fact. An understatement — kind of, sort of, a bit — makes the problem smaller than it is. A question — could you, would you mind, if possible, right? — gives the other person something to answer instead of something to obey.

Em português, um pedido direto entre colegas é normal. Em inglês, o mesmo pedido sem hedge soa como uma ordem. I think não abre uma opinião: ele tira a frase do terreno da ordem.

The same request, said to a manager and to a colleague

The level follows the room and the cost of saying no. A deadline given to your own team is direct, because the team already knows why. The same deadline given to a manager is hedged, because the manager decides. The same deadline given to a client is a question, because the client can refuse.

Contractions belong to the same choice. I'd like is I would like, and in speech the short form is the polite one while the full form sounds formal or emphatic. We'll look into it commits to less than we will investigate, and it is what a colleague actually says.

Send the final numbers today.

Could you send the final numbers today, if possible?

The second line asks for the same thing and leaves the answer open. The first line is not rude in Portuguese; in English it is an order.
