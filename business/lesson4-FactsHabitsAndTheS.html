Facts and habits: the -s that carries the whole tense

The simple present says what is true in general, not what is happening now

Three kinds of sentence, one tense

The simple present carries three things a working week is made of:

  • A fact — The call starts at nine. · My brother works nights.
  • A habit — She checks the numbers every Monday. · She never eats breakfast.
  • A routine — I work for a tech company. · We usually test on Thursdays.

None of those sentences is about this minute. I work for a tech company was true last month and is true next month. If a sentence is true now, was true before and will be true after, it is a simple present sentence — and the line below shows exactly that.

The word carrying the tense sits under now, and the arcs run past it in both directions: the sentence covers the whole line.

That is the difference this lesson is built on. Estou trabalhando sits on one point of the line; trabalho covers all of it. English keeps the two apart, and the simple present is the one that covers all of it.

A simple present sentence has one verb, and it is the main verb: I work. It never takes be in front — I am work is not a sentence — and it never wears -ing: I working is not one either.

One verb, no helper, and the tense lives in that verb. The next section shows the only place its shape changes.

always and usually: the two words that make it a routine

always(al·ways) — sempre · I always check the numbers on Monday.

usually(u·su·a·lly) — geralmente · We usually test on Thursdays.

Both words describe how often, and both are common in the answer to "what does your week look like?" — the question every colleague asks in the first week.

Read the twelve out loud once, and notice that the English word is shorter than the Portuguese one in almost every line: work for trabalhar, eat for comer, sleep for dormir. The verb itself is small; what carries the meaning is the sentence around it.

Three of them — like, want, need — take a thing after them and nothing else at this level: I like the system. I want two options. I need the file.

-s is the whole difference between I and she

English changes the verb in one place only

Portuguese changes the verb for every person: trabalho, trabalhas, trabalha, trabalhamos, trabalham. English changes it once, in the third person singular — he, she, it, and any one person or thing you name. Add -s, and the other five subjects keep the base form with no ending at all: I work, you work, we work, they work.

That single letter is the whole signature of the tense. It is also the thing that gives a Brazilian speaker away in a shared inbox: I check and she checks are different sentences, and the only difference is on the end of the verb.

A name or a thing counts as he, she or it: My brother works nights. · The call starts at nine. · The bus leaves at six.

The -s has four shapes, and one of them is silent

Four shapes, one letter of meaning

The ending is always -s in grammar, and it is written four ways:

  • Add -s — the normal case: work → works, start → starts, need → needs, live → lives.
  • Add -es after ch, sh, s, x, o: finish → finishes, watch → watches, go → goes, do → does.
  • Consonant + y becomes -ies: study → studies.
  • have becomes has — the one irregular verb at this level.

Do and go take -es because of their final sound: goes, does. You will meet both again in lesson 5, where they become the little verb that carries the negative.

The letter e in -es is not silent, and it is not said as e in Portuguese either: finishes ends in an extra syllable, works does not. Compare the two endings with the key above the word:

checks(checks) — one syllable, the s added to the sound.

lives(lives) — still one syllable; the e is written and not said.

That is why finishes sounds like fi-ni-shiz and works sounds like work-s: the same ending, a different number of beats.

always, usually, never: between who and what happens

The word goes before the verb, never after it

Words of frequency — always, usually, never — stand between the subject and the main verb:

  • We usually test on Thursdays. — nós geralmente testamos
  • I always check the numbers on Monday.
  • She never eats breakfast.

Portuguese often puts the same word after the verb — trabalho sempre, testamos geralmente — and the English order is the reverse of that habit. After a form of be the word moves behind it: She is usually at her desk.

The test is mechanical: find the person, find the verb, and put the frequency word in the gap between them.

never is already the negative

She never eats breakfast. One negative word, one verb. Ela não come nunca has two negative words and is correct Portuguese; English puts one, and the verb stays affirmative: never eats.

So never eats is right and never doesn't eat is wrong — the second one cancels itself out and says she always eats. That is the trap in this section, and the next lesson gives the full machine behind it.

Seven sentences that describe a working week

Every one of them is a fact or a habit

  • I work for a tech company. — a fact, base verb after I.
  • She checks the numbers every Monday. — a habit, -s after she.
  • The call starts at nine. — a thing as the subject, so -s.
  • We usually test on Thursdays. — the frequency word between subject and verb.
  • My brother works nights. — a named person, -s; nights = à noite.
  • The bus leaves at six. — a thing, -s, and a departure time.
  • She never eats breakfast. — never plus the affirmative verb.

Read them as the answer to one question a colleague asks in the first week: what does your week look like? Every sentence has a clock in it, and every verb is in this tense.

Four gaps, three decisions: I takes the base verb, he takes the -s, and never stands in front of the verb it negates. The clock phrases — at six, at two, every Monday — sit at the end, where English keeps them.

Say the four lines about your own week before you move on. The sentence you will need most at work is the first one, and it is entirely made of words from this lesson.

Both routines are habits, and both routines are full of -s: Rafa starts, makes, serves, finishes and Lia works, sorts, leaves. The he or she in each sentence is a person you can name, and the verb agrees with that one person.

Works nights is worth keeping as a phrase: trabalha à noite. It is the same shape as works hard from the song — a verb with its -s and a word after it that says how or when.
