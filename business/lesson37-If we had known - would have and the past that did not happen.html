If we had known - would have and the past that did not happen

This lesson is about a past that did not happen. English describes it with two past forms in one sentence: if we had tested it, we wouldn't have shipped the bug. Both halves point at the same past, and the sentence reports the result of a version of events that never took place.

You have met both pieces already. The past participle is the form you use after have — I've sent it — and the unreal conditional is the one that imagined a different present: if I had the budget, I'd hire another analyst. This lesson moves that imagination one step back. The if-half gains had, the result gains have, and the same shape then carries the three sentences a working week is full of: the result you cannot change anymore, the thing you should have done, and the chance nobody took.

The lesson closes on a client call, because the form on its own is not the hard part. The hard part is saying what went wrong, how big it is and what happens next, in one breath, without losing the client.

The if-half takes had, and the result takes would have

A sentence about an unreal past has two halves, and each half has its own form. The if-half takes had + the past participle. The result half takes would have + the past participle.

If we had tested it, we wouldn't have shipped the bug.

Either half can come first. With the if-half first, a comma (,) separates the two; with the result first, no comma is needed: We wouldn't have shipped the bug if we had tested it.

The if-half's form is the past perfect. The participle after had is the same participle you already use after have: we've sent it becomes if we had sent it, and I've seen the file becomes if I had seen the file. The irregular list does not change — sent, made, bought, caught, seen, been, known — because the form of the participle is the same in both places.

Had does two different jobs in English, and only one of them builds this sentence. In if I had the budget, had is the simple past of the verb have and the sentence describes an unreal present. In if I had checked the budget, had is an auxiliary in front of a participle and the sentence describes an unreal past. The result half moves with it: I'd hire for the present, I would've hired for the past.

In speech, would have is would've, and the negative is wouldn't have. At speed the phrase can sound like woulda; that is what the ear hears, never what the page writes. The written forms are would've and would not have. Would of is not English.

The error to watch is would've inside the if-half. The rule that if never takes will holds at the past as well: if takes had + participle, never would have. The wrong sentence is the one Portuguese produces, because Portuguese puts the conditional in both halves — se tivéssemos testado, não teríamos enviado o bug. English marks the condition once, in the if-half, with the past perfect.

Correction. Each sentence has one wrong verb form in the unreal past. Write the sentence correctly.

  1. If we would have called the client on Friday, we'd have known about the date change. → If we had called the client on Friday, we'd have known about the date change.
  2. If the team has tested the release, we wouldn't have shipped the bug. → If the team had tested the release, we wouldn't have shipped the bug.
  3. If I had see the email, I would've answered it. → If I had seen the email, I would've answered it.
  4. If they had confirmed the date, we would started on Monday. → If they had confirmed the date, we would've started on Monday.

Translation. Translate into English. Portuguese states the condition with its own subjunctive; English states it with the past perfect.

  1. Se tivéssemos testado antes do lançamento, não teríamos enviado esse bug. → If we had tested before the release, we wouldn't have shipped that bug.
  2. Se o cliente tivesse assinado na segunda-feira, o trabalho teria começado naquela semana. → If the client had signed on Monday, the work would've started that week.
  3. Eu teria respondido o e-mail se tivesse visto a mensagem. → I would've answered the email if I had seen the message.

Would've, should've, could've: three results, three meanings

All three forms are a modal + have + the past participle. The modal is what changes the news about the past.

Form | What it says | Example

would have | the result, if the condition had held | If we'd tested it, we would've caught the bug.

should have | the right thing to do, and it was not done | We should've called the client earlier.

could have | it was possible, and nobody did it | I could've caught that in review.

Would've is the second half of an if-sentence. It also stands alone when the condition is obvious or has just been said: I would've called, but my phone died. In both uses it reports a result that did not happen.

Should've is a judgement, made after the fact: the action was the right one and it was not taken. It is not have to — nothing outside the speaker imposed it — and it is the sentence an admission is built on: We should've called the client earlier. That one's on us. The negative says the opposite: We shouldn't have announced the date before the tests ran.

Could've says the chance existed. It does not say the action was the right one; it says the person had the time, the tool or the information and did not use it: I could've caught that in review. A sentence with could've usually adds the reason the chance was there — I had the report — and that reason is what separates it from should've.

One situation, three sentences, three meanings. If we'd run the full test, we would've caught it before the build. The result depended on the test. We should've run the full test — it was our job. That is the judgement. We could've run the full test on Thursday, and nobody did. The time existed; the decision was never taken.

The form after any of the three is have + participle. Should had, could had and would had are not English. In speech the three reduce to should've, could've and would've; shoulda and coulda are what the ear hears, never what the page writes.

Portuguese lines the three up one for one: teria testado is would've tested, deveria ter testado is should've tested, and poderia ter testado is could've tested. The mapping holds, so the work is not the form but the choice: three positions that look the same and mean three different things, and the meaning is decided by what the sentence knows — a condition, a duty or a chance.

Choose. One form fits each sentence, and the reason in the sentence is what chooses it.

  1. If we'd read the log that morning, we ______ found the error before the client did. (a) should have (b) would have (c) could have → would have
  2. ______ called the supplier before the price changed — that was our job, and we skipped it. (a) We would have (b) We should have (c) We could have → We should have
  3. The report was on my desktop all week. I ______ checked the number myself, but I never opened it. (a) could have (b) should have (c) would have → could have
  4. We ______ announced the date before the tests ran. That's the mistake we're paying for now. (a) wouldn't have (b) shouldn't have (c) couldn't have → shouldn't have

At the time and in hindsight: the words for the later view

A report about a mistake holds two viewpoints, and English gives each one its own phrase. One says what the team knew when it happened; the other says what it knows now.

At the time reports the view from inside the past: At the time, the date looked tight but possible. In hindsight and looking back report the view from now: In hindsight, the date was too tight. / Looking back, we moved too fast. The facts can be identical in the two sentences; what changes is who is speaking and when. In hindsight admits a judgement the team did not have then, and that admission is the reason the phrase exists.

All three open the sentence, with a comma after them — the position the hedges for softening a statement already take. Put one in the middle and the sentence still works, but it reads as an afterthought rather than as an admission.

Mistake is the thing done wrong. Regret is the feeling about it, and in writing it is formal and short: We regret the delay. Missed takes the thing that was not seen or not taken — we missed a step in the test, we missed the deadline, we missed that in review. Learn from closes the report with a change, and the change has to be named: We'll learn from this — the full test now runs before a date is confirmed.

Unfortunately is the neutral, formal marker of bad news, and it takes the same first position: Unfortunately, the file is two weeks late. Two weeks behind measures the schedule against its own date, and it slipped hands the movement to the date instead of blaming a person: the file slipped by two weeks.

Bad news goes in four moves, and the soft words never hide the number

A message that carries bad news has four moves, in this order: the news in the first sentence; the size of it in facts; the cause, short and in the forms this lesson just taught; and what happens next, with the date of the next update. The order is not a style choice. A reader who has to wait for the bad sentence reads everything before it as if the message were good, and stops listening when the sentence arrives.

The four moves in one email:

Subject: Release date — two weeks

Hi Dana,

I'm afraid the release slipped a little — two weeks. It's now scheduled for March 14.

We should've run the full test before we set the date. If we had run it in January, we would've found the bug before the build, and we wouldn't have shipped it.

The team is on the fix now, and I'll keep you posted every Friday until it ships.

Best,
Ana

I'm afraid is the polite opener of the bad sentence, not a statement of fear. The line I'm afraid the release slipped a little — two weeks means infelizmente, o lançamento atrasou um pouco — duas semanas. A reader who takes I'm afraid as tenho medo writes a sentence the client cannot use.

Lower the verb, keep the number. A slight delay, a little and it slipped lower the weight of the sentence; two weeks and two weeks behind state the size exactly. The two have to appear together. A slight delay with no number is a message the client cannot plan around, and a number with no soft verb reads as a decision taken without them.

Understatement is the third tool: the sentence calls the result acceptable and names the difficulty in the same breath. Not bad, considering — said about a rough week that still produced a working version. Not ideal and not where we wanted to be do the same for a problem both sides already know about. Understatement works only when the facts are on the table; used to hide a number, it is the sentence the client remembers.

We made a mistake is the business-casual sentence, and it is said to a client and to a manager. Between peers it has an informal double — yeah, we screwed up — and that is a note about who is in the room, never the sentence that goes to a client.

Keep you posted is the promise of the next update, and it needs a time: I'll keep you posted every Friday. On its own it is a sentence the client cannot hold you to.

Fill. Complete the message to your manager with phrases from the bank. Three phrases in the bank are not used.

  2. The file reaches the client on the 14th, so we're ______. → two weeks behind
  3. The delivery ______ because we were waiting for a confirmation nobody had asked for. → slipped
  4. ______, the dates were wrong from the first version. → In hindsight
  5. I'll walk the team through the list and ______ on Friday. → keep you posted

Saying it out loud: the form and the size of the news in one answer

On a call the same four moves arrive as a conversation, and the client asks the question that decides the sentence: what happened, and whose decision was it. The answer puts the form, the fact and the softening into one breath, and the exchange below is the shape of it.

Dana: Ana, thanks for calling. What's the news on the release?

Ana: I'm afraid it slipped — two weeks. We're looking at March 14 now.

Dana: Two weeks. What happened?

Ana: If we had run the full test in January, we would've caught it before the build. We should've run it earlier — that one's on us.

Dana: Could you have caught it in review?

Ana: We could have, yes. The report file changed in February and the review didn't cover it. At the time, it looked like a small change.

Dana: And the client meeting on the 20th?

Ana: We'll keep it and show the figures from a test version. I'll keep you posted every Friday until it ships.

Dana: Okay. Not bad, considering — at least we know now.

Read the exchange for its order: the news in Ana's first line, the size in the same line, the cause in her third, and the new date in her last. Each of the three forms appears once, and no line carries two of them. The reply to Could you have caught it in review? starts with We could have — the short answer repeats the modal and drops the participle, the same way yes, we should answers a question about a duty.
