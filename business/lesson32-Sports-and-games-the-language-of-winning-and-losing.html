Sports and games: the language of winning and losing

Sport words that describe work

Why a meeting sounds like a match

A game and a project have the same shape: a goal, a score, a team, a clock and an opponent. English took the words of the field and used them for the office, so we dropped the ball, we're on a roll and the ball's in your court are ordinary sentences in a standup.

Each phrase keeps its picture, and the picture gives the meaning. Drop the ball is a failure to do the one thing you carried. On a roll is a run of wins with no loss between them. Left field is the side of a baseball field nobody watches, so anything from there is a surprise.

Where a phrase comes from explains why it means what it does, and the origin fixes the meaning in place. step up comes from baseball, where the batter steps up to the plate for their turn — so the phrase means taking the turn that is yours. call it comes from an umpire calling a game off. the final whistle is the referee's, and it ends the match whatever the score. Knowing the picture is what keeps the phrases from blurring into one another.

The phrases are not decoration. A team that hears not a level playing field understands an argument about unequal conditions in three words, and no longer sentence does it better.

What makes them safe to use at work is that each one names a situation, never a person: nobody is called careless by we dropped the ball, and nobody is accused by the ball's in their court.

Two notes on use

We got smoked means we lost badly. It belongs in a group chat with colleagues, not in a report to a client; write we lost badly or the result was poor.

A sport phrase carries an attitude as well as a meaning. Step up tells a colleague the work is theirs to take, and it is direct enough to sound like an order in a formal email. In writing, could you take ownership of the handover? does the same work with less heat.

The result words, and the particle that changes everything

Five verbs and what follows them

The result words differ in what comes after them, and that difference is where most sentences go wrong. You score a number, you beat a team, you win a game or win by a margin, you lose to a team, and a game ends in a tie.

win and beat cannot swap: we won them is wrong, because the game is what you win and the team is what you beat. beat is irregular and does not change in the past: they beat us, never they beated us.

American English says tie for a game with no winner: The game ended in a tie, we tied 2–2.

Two of these verbs also have a passive, and it is the form a report uses when nobody is being blamed: we were beaten by two goals, the match was tied. win is the exception — a game is won, a team is not — so a team is never won; it is beaten, defeated or outplayed.

The object is what makes the difference memorable: beat takes a team, win takes a game, and lose takes to before its team. Say the verb and its object together, and the sentence builds itself.

The margin is a separate phrase: by two goals, by a point, by six percent — a preposition and a number, and it can follow any of the three verbs.

One verb, three particles

drop out means leave before the end, or stop taking part. drop by means visit briefly. drop off means deliver something, or fall asleep. The verb is the same and the particle fixes the meaning, so the particle is learned with the phrase and never guessed.

The same pattern explains call it: call with it means decide to stop, while call on its own means telephone or name.

A particle can also change the object a verb takes: drop the ball keeps the ball as its object, and drop off takes a thing you deliver. Learn the phrase with the object it wants, not the verb alone.

Where the phrase is separable, the pronoun goes in the middle: drop it off, call it off — never drop off it.

Monday standup, with a match in the room

How the phrases sit in a report

A standup has a fixed order: what happened, why it happened, what happens next. A sport phrase fits each part and keeps the sentence short. We dropped the ball on the labels states the failure; that came out of left field states the surprise; the ball's in their court states whose move it is.

The last one matters in a report because it names responsibility without accusing anyone: the sentence says where the next action sits, not who is at fault.

The phrases are informal but not slang, so they belong in a team meeting and in a message to close colleagues. In a written report to a client, the plain sentence is the safer one: we missed the deadline, the supplier changed the template.

The same three parts work for a whole week, not one item: Packaging dropped the ball on the labels (what happened), the supplier changed the template without telling us (why), they have the ball now (whose move). Three sentences, and the meeting can move on.

A sport phrase is also a way of reporting a loss without a face: we were beaten on price names the reason and leaves the team out of it. The passive does that work, which is why a status report leans on it.

What the phrases never do is replace the number. We dropped the ball on the labels says what went wrong; forty boxes says how much. A report with the phrase and no figure sounds like an apology, and a report with the figure and no phrase sounds like a machine.

Keep the tense straight at the same time: the failure took the past simple (we dropped the ball), what follows is still true takes the present (the ball's in their court).

Read the whole thing back once before sending. A standup is heard, not read, so a sentence that sounds like a commentary — we had a good run, then we lost the ball — is doing its job.

Two idioms, two directions

On a roll describes the team and is always positive. Drop the ball describes one failure and is always negative. A single report can carry both: We're on a roll on the product side, but we dropped the ball on the labels.

The pair is useful because it shows the team has both a run of wins and a failure, which is what a real week looks like.

A third phrase keeps the balance in one sentence and is the one a manager says out loud: we're on a roll, but we can't afford to drop the ball again. The first half reports, the second half warns, and neither accuses a person.

The same sentence is the shape of a weekly update, and the order matters: the run first, the failure second, because the listener remembers the last thing best.
