Can, could, would: ability, permission and polite requests

The modal fills the place and never changes

O modal entra no espaço do indicador de modo e nunca muda de forma: can, could, would são iguais para todas as pessoas, e o verbo principal fica na base — she can send, nunca she cans nem she can sends.

One word, one form, always

A modal is a word that fills the place in front of the verb and never changes form: can, could, would, will, may. No -s for she, no -ed for the past, no to before the next verb. She can send it — never she cans, never can to send.

This is lesson 5's place again, with a new word in it. The place carries the tense and the modal is what moves into it, so the main verb stays in base and did never appears: the question is Can you send it?, not Do you can send it?

The marks are the same marks every modal gets: the modal is a mode indicator, and its mode mark lands on can, the verb is the verb, and nothing sits between them. When the sentence turns negative the not joins the modal — can't, couldn't, wouldn't — and the verb does not move.

The words a request is built from

As doze peças de que um pedido é feito — a abertura, o verbo, o objeto e o fecho —, cada uma com o que faz na frase.

Twelve pieces, and what each one does

Borrow and lend are one word in Portuguese and two directions in English. Emprestar covers both, so the sentence has to say who gives and who takes: Can I borrow your charger? — I take it. Can you lend me your charger? — you give it. Both are the same object and the same hour; only the direction changes.

A favour is what you call the small thing itself: Can you do me a favour? It is an opener, and it warns the listener that a request is coming — which is why English uses it before the request rather than after it.

Four ways of asking, and who is listening

Quatro formas de pedir, da ordem ao pedido mais suave: send it → can you send it? → could you send it? → would you mind sending it? Quanto mais suave a forma, mais distância e mais cortesia.

From an order to the softest ask

One request has four forms, and the form is chosen by who is listening. The direct form is an order: Send me the file. It is fine inside a team that knows you and wrong the moment it goes upward. Can you…? is the neutral ask, and it works with anyone you know. Could you…? is the safe default to a manager or a client. Would you mind…? is the softest, and it changes the verb after it: Would you mind sending me the file?

The choice is not politeness for its own sake. It is information about the size of the ask and the distance between the two people — and a native speaker hears which form was chosen before hearing the request.

The two traps inside "would you mind"

Duas armadilhas em would you mind: o verbo depois dele vira -ing (would you mind sending it?), e a resposta inverte — no, not at all quer dizer "claro, eu faço".

The verb turns, and the answer flips

Would you mind sending me the file? does not ask will you. It asks is it a problem for you? So the answer that agrees is No: No, not at all, or No problem. Answering yes means yes, it is a problem — and that is the one mistake this phrase causes in a meeting, because the grammar is right and the answer is backwards.

The second trap is the verb. After would you mind the verb takes -ing, because the phrase ends in a space that wants a noun: mind sending, never mind to send.

The request, from opening to answer

O pedido inteiro, da abertura à resposta: oito falas, e cada uma usa uma peça da seção anterior — could you, sure, thanks.

  3. Jess19. send me the numbers before the call?

Eight turns, and every way of asking is in them. Can I asks for permission to speak. Could you is the ask itself. Let me is Mike offering to check first. Would you mind with copying is the softest addition. Not at all is the answer that agrees.

Sorry to interrupt is worth keeping as one piece. It is how a conversation is entered in English, and it costs nothing: the interruption is forgiven before it happens.

Anytime at the end says ask me again. It is the last turn of half the calls you will make.

The other job: what is possible

O outro trabalho do mesmo verbo: can e could dizem o que é possível ou permitido, e could também carrega o passado — eu podia.

Ability, permission, and the past that could also carries

Can is not only a request. It also says what is possible: I can run that report means I know how, or it is available to me. Could is the same word in the past: I could swim when I was five, I couldn't open the attachment. Both keep the verb in base.

So could has two lives, and the situation picks one. Could you open it? is a request. I could open it yesterday, but not today is ability, past and gone. Nothing in the word tells you which; the rest of the sentence does.

The negative keeps the two jobs apart more clearly. Can't is usually permission or rule: You can't share that file with the client. Couldn't is usually ability or possibility, in the past: I couldn't open the file — the link was dead.

One habit that pays: say I can't when a rule stops you, and I couldn't when something did not work. A manager hears the difference immediately.

Ask, offer, answer — the three moves

Os três movimentos: pedir (could you…?), oferecer (can I…?, I'll…) e responder (sure, no problem). O pedido é feito de ponta a ponta, e a resposta também tem forma.

What a request is made of, end to end

Every request has three moves, and the lesson has given you the words for all of them. Ask: Can I ask a question? for permission to speak; Could you send me the file? for the thing itself; Would you mind checking this? when the ask is large or the person is far above you.

Offer: Let me check and get back to you puts you on the doing side of the request. It is the sentence that turns a request into a conversation, and it is the one a manager remembers.

Answer: Of course, Sure, No problem, and for would you mind — Not at all. Four words, one job: yes, and it is no trouble. None of them promises more than you can do, which is why they are the safe answers.

The refusal has its own form, and it is not on this page's list: I can't today — how about tomorrow? A no with a door in it. That is the sentence that keeps a request alive.
