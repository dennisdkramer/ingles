It, that and one: what each one replaces

It, that and one each replace something different

A meeting repeats things already said without repeating the words. It replaces a thing. That replaces a whole statement. One replaces a noun, so the noun is not said twice.

The three do not swap. The date moved and it moved are two different sentences: the first names the thing, the second says that the thing moved. That moved would mean the whole situation moved, which is not what you meant.

Português aponta com isso, ele, esse e repete o substantivo com mais frequência. Em inglês, repetir o substantivo soa errado, e one é a palavra que evita a repetição.

Twelve phrases that keep a conversation moving

These are the phrases a meeting runs on. None of them names a thing: each one replaces what someone already said, or asks them to say it again.

Three carry a card, because their Portuguese looks closer than their meaning: , , .

I thought so não é eu pensei isso no sentido de opinião: é eu imaginei, bem que eu suspeitava. Long story é é uma longa história — e a resposta certa é não contar.

I thought soI already suspected it, and now you have confirmed it — "The file was late." — "I thought so." Not eu pensei isso as an opinion.
long storyit would take too long to explain now — said to stop the explanation, not to begin one — "Why did it move?" — "Long story."
back to thatreturn to something said earlier — Can we go back to that? The thing itself was named before; that is the pointer.
  • that one — the thing we both just pointed at: Not that one, the other one.
  • the other one — the second of two already named.
  • so do I — I do the same, for an action in the present.
  • neither did I — I did not either, for something in the past.
  • the same thing — one statement, said again in other words.
  • sorry, the what? — ask for one word again; the word you missed goes after the.
  • what do you mean? — ask for the meaning of a whole statement.
  • right then — a small signal that you are starting something new.
  • who said that? — ask who said the statement just heard.

The answer repeats the mode, not the verb

When someone says I've already sent it, the reply that agrees is So have I — not So I have, and not Me too in a meeting. The mode goes first and the subject follows, and the main verb is not said at all, because it was just said.

The negative follows the same order: Neither did I, Neither have I. Neither already contains the not, so the sentence does not add one.

Em português: eu também e eu também não. Em inglês a forma muda com o tempo verbal — so do I, so have I, neither did I — e é isso que você precisa escolher.

I've already sent it. — So I have.

I've already sent it. — So have I.

Read the pair again and notice what changed: the two words swapped places. English puts the mode first in an agreeing reply, exactly as it puts the mode first in a question.

You will lose a word in almost every call, and the mistake to avoid is answering as if you lost the sentence. English has a short form for the single word you missed: put the in front of it and raise the question — Sorry, the what now?

The same move works for the subject of a statement you half heard: Wait, who moved what? You keep the rest of the statement and put a question word in the place you missed.

Peça a palavra, não a frase: sorry, the what? Para o sujeito: who moved what? O resto da frase já foi ouvido e não precisa ser repetido.

  1. JessThey moved the date to the thirty-first.
  2. BrunoWait, who moved what?
  3. JessThe client moved the date. Not us.
  4. BrunoRight then. Understood.

No way is what a colleague says when the news is surprising: They moved the date — no way. Keep it for a peer; that is surprising is the careful version.
