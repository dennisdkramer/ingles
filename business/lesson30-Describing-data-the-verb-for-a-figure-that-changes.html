Describing data: the verb for a figure that changes

Direction and size, in one verb

Every trend sentence needs two facts

A figure that moves over time is described with a verb of direction and a number of size: Revenue grew by 8 percent to 2.4 million, Sales dropped slightly last quarter. The verb says which way the figure went; the number says how far.

The verb carries both the fact and the tense. grow becomes grew, drop becomes dropped, and the irregular ones change inside: rise becomes rose, fall becomes fell. A trend report in the past uses these forms, not the present.

The same fact can be stated as a verb or as a noun, and a report needs both. Revenue grew by 8 percent and there was a growth of 8 percent in revenue point at one figure. The noun form is what a summary sentence uses when its main verb is already taken: Q3 saw a sharp drop in sales, the year showed steady growth. Learn them in pairs — grow and growth, drop and a drop, increase and an increase — because the noun takes the article and the verb takes the ending.

The verb also states the speed when an adverb joins it: slightly, sharply, steadily, by 8 percent. That is what a table of numbers nobody reads cannot do.

The verbs and their direction

Which wayThe verb, and its past formIn a sentence
uprise / rose · grow / grew · increase / increasedRevenue grew by 8 percent to 2.4 million.
up fastdouble / doubled · jump / jumpedWe expect the share to double by Q4.
downdrop / dropped · fall / fell · decrease / decreasedSales dropped slightly last quarter.
up then levelpeak / peakedCosts peaked in March and then plateaued.
levelplateau / plateaued · level off / leveled offOrders plateaued in the summer.

Two prepositions divide the fact: by gives the size of the change, to gives the level it reached. Grew by 8 percent and grew to 2.4 million can stand in one sentence, and neither one replaces the other.

A movement can also be given as a pair of levels: from names where it started and to where it finished — Revenue grew from 1.9 million to 2.1 million — with by left to report the difference between the two. A level reported on its own, with no movement, takes at: We are at 2.4 million for the quarter.

Four ways to say how sure you are

roughly means about, not exactly: It's up roughly 8 percent. at most means no more than this number: We'll add at most two people. A ballpark figure is an approximate number that is good enough for a first conversation. too early to say means the evidence is not enough for a conclusion yet: It's too early to say if it's a trend.

These are not weaker sentences; they are accurate ones. A number reported with certainty it does not have is the mistake a manager remembers. You're guessing, honestly is the informal note under the evidence is thin — say it to a colleague, never in a client report.

Three numbers in one quarter can all differ, and the words keep them apart: a forecast is what you expect, a target is what the plan promises, and a budget is the limit you were given. We forecast 2.1 million against a target of 2.4 and a budget of 2.2 is a sentence a manager can act on, because each figure says what kind of number it is.

Reporting a chart to a manager

The order that answers the question

A report of a figure has an order: the direction and the size first, the exception second, and how sure you are last. Revenue grew by 8 percent to 2.4 million. Sales dropped slightly last quarter. The forecast is a ballpark figure. The listener then knows what happened, where it did not, and how far to trust the last line.

A framing marker at the front says what the number is measured against: Across the whole year, revenue grew by 8 percent. On twelve responses, that's noise. Without the marker the figure sounds as if it came from the whole market, and the listener makes a decision on it.

One rule for the whole report: the verb states direction, and the preposition states the arithmetic. by for the size of the change, to for the level it reached.

The comparison is the part a manager listens for, and English marks it with one word: against, compared with or versus. Revenue grew by 8 percent against a target of 7 says both facts in one line, and compared with last year names the other period without repeating its figures.

A figure that did not move is reported as loudly as one that did: Orders plateaued at 1.2 million, costs held steady, the backlog stayed level. Silence about a number lets the listener assume it changed, and the unchanged number is often the one that makes the trend readable.

Close a report with what follows from it, not with another number: so the plan holds, so we should revisit the price in May. The figures were the evidence; the sentence after them is the reason the evidence was collected.

That structure is why a one-line status email reads the way it does: direction and size, the exception, the confidence, then the decision it supports.
