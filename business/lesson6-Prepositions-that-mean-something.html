Prepositions that mean something

in, on and at answer three different questions

Look at the shape first, then choose the word

Portuguese has one word for three English ideas: em. English never asks "which word means em?" It asks you to look at the thing and see its shape — and once the shape is clear, the preposition is already decided.

  • in — inside something with limits: a drawer, a room, a building, a city.
  • on — touching a surface: a desk, a table, a wall, a street.
  • at — a point you stop at, named as a place: the office, the bus stop, the door.

The same object changes its preposition when the picture changes: the keys are in the drawer while they are inside it, on the table while they are lying there, and at the front desk while the receptionist keeps them.

Where it is: inside, on top, or at the point

One place can take two prepositions, and both can be right

in the office means inside the room — the opposite is out of the office. at the office means the office is the place you are, the answer to where are you? Both are correct English, and one sentence can carry both: I'm at my desk in the office.

Fixed points take at with no article: at work, at home, at the bus stop, at the door.

Vehicles follow the same picture. A bus, a train and a plane have a floor you step onto, so you are on them. A car or a taxi has no room to stand, so you are in it.

The file is on your desk.

I'm at the office.

I'm in the bus.

I'm on the bus.

When it is: a stretch, a day, a clock

The more exact the point, the shorter the preposition

in holds a long stretch: a month, a year, a season, a part of the day — in July, in 2026, in the morning, in the afternoon.

on holds one day or one date — on Monday, on May fifth. When a day and a part of the day stand together, the day wins: on Monday morning.

at holds a clock time — at nine, at half past two, at noon. One sentence can carry two of them: The meeting is on Monday at nine.

I work and sleep in the morning.

at nightà noite — the dark hours. Every other part of the day takes in: in the morning, in the afternoon, in the evening. Night is the one that takes at.

The picture is the one from the first section: in is a space, on is the surface you land on, at is a point on the clock.

"Half past two" is also "two thirty"; the second is shorter and very common in speech.

to or for: where it goes, or what it is for

Something moves with "to"; something is meant with "for"

Portuguese para covers both words, and in English they are not interchangeable. One question decides: is something traveling from one person or place to another?

to is the destination — the person or place a thing goes to. I'm sending it to Ana. Please forward the file to the client.

for is the purpose, the use, or the person who benefits. Nothing has to move: This is for the client. I made a copy for you.

The test: if the thing could travel in an envelope, the person takes to. If the thing exists because of the person — a report written for them, a chair kept for them — it takes for.

Compare: I'm sending the agenda to the client. The agenda moves. This agenda is for the client. The agenda is theirs to use.

A third word, at, never names a receiver: I'm sending it at Ana is not English.

Home, work, the office, the street

Five phrases that take no article

at home and at work take no article and no the. Movement home takes no preposition at all: I'm going home, never to home.

at the office names the place; in the office puts you inside the room. on the street is outside, along the road — American English uses the same phrase for an address: the office is on Market Street.

Transport keeps the picture from the second section: on the bus, on the train, but in the car.
