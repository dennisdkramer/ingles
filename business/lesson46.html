Whose English is it?

What this lesson installs: the fact that reorders everything you have learned about sounding natural — most English in the world is not spoken to native speakers. The target is not the native idiom; it is being understood by somebody whose English is also a second language, which means clarity first and idiom second. Idiom is not wrong, it is a tax: every joke, football reference and phrasal verb is paid for by the listener's attention. Three more things follow. Directness is a dial, and culture sets the default — I'm just being direct is one culture's habit, not a virtue. "Yes" is at least four different things, and the test is never to repeat the question louder. And face is the currency: the wording that lets somebody be wrong in private buys more than any correct sentence you will say today.

1. Heard — ninety seconds that went wrong

Four countries, one call, one decision. Listen ▶ to the clip at natural speed — you have enough English to follow the words, and that is exactly the trap. Then answer two questions.

The chair (British): "Right — let's park the timeline and pick it up offline, the numbers are in the ballpark anyway."
The supplier (Japanese): "That may be difficult. We will consider it carefully and revert."
The engineer (Dutch): "So no date, then. Fine — but we need a date."
The marketing lead (Brazilian): "We can probably make it work — let's talk after."

Quatro formas de não dizer nada. Nenhuma delas é falta de inglês.

A. Two traps

1.Did anyone agree a date?
2.Did the supplier agree?
3."Let's park the timeline" means…
4."In the ballpark" means…
5."We can probably make it work — let's talk after" commits to…

Replay, with two words highlighted: park and consider. Nothing else in the clip changed — the words were all English you know, and the meaning was still lost. That is this whole lesson in ninety seconds: the words were not the problem, the assumptions were.

2. The two audits

A. The idiom audit

Six lines from the call. For each: what it costs the listener, and the plain version that says the same thing.

1. "Let's park the timeline."
2. "The numbers are in the ballpark."
3. "Pick it up offline."
4. "We'll touch base next week."
5. "That's a whole different ball game."
6. "Off the top of my head…"

Não é "nunca usar idioma": é gastar a atenção do ouvinte onde está o sentido.

B. Six readings of "yes"

Write which one it is: agree · I hear you · I will try · I will not say no to your face.

1. "Yes, yes — understood."
2. "Yes, we will do our best."
3. "Yes, that should be possible."
4. "Yes… we will revert to you."
5. "Yes, no problem at all." (fast, moving on)
6. "Yes — if the certificate arrives."

Nenhum destes é mentira. Três não são compromisso.

The test that works on all six: never repeat the question louder. Ask for the next step — "so who does what by when?" — because a person who has not agreed will improvise there, and a person who has agreed will name a date. The follow-up question is the only reliable yes detector in business English.

Only one of the four is a decision. The other three are warm, polite, and uncommitted — and the trap is that the two friendliest replies are the emptiest, while the one that appears rude is the only one you can plan around. Directness is not aggression; indirectness is not agreement.

B. One follow-up that settles all four

A single question, sent to all four, that makes every reply answerable with a date or a refusal. Write it — and make it impossible to answer with "we'll revert".

4. The directness dial

Culture sets the default — not politeness, not honesty. A low-context reader wants the conclusion early and the reason after; a high-context reader wants the relationship and the frame first, and reads a bald no as a rupture in the relationship, not as information.

low-context
norte da Europa, EUA		the sentence itself; the relationship is not the mediumnothing — it is the answer, and everyone moves on"No — Tuesday 12th. Is that a problem?"

The dial is not a moral scale. A high-context reader who receives a low-context no does not think you are honest; they think the room has broken. A low-context reader who receives three paragraphs of framing thinks you have not decided. Choose the setting for the reader, then keep it for the whole exchange — switching mid-thread is what reads as manipulation.

5. Done — the same message, three ways

for a low-context reader
"We cannot confirm Friday. We can confirm Tuesday 12th. If Tuesday is too late, tell me today and I will escalate."
conclusão primeiro, razão depois, ação nomeada
for a high-context reader
"Thank you for pushing this — I know the date matters to you. As things stand, Friday is difficult for the team. Tuesday 12th we can commit to, and I would rather offer you a date we keep than one we miss. Would Tuesday work for you?"
relação, enquadramento, depois a informação
when you outrank the room
"Here is where we are: Friday is not achievable. I have asked for Tuesday 12th instead, and I will own the delay with the board. Any objection, come to me directly."
a autoridade substitui a delicadeza — e assume o custo
Notice what did not change: the fact (no Friday), the alternative (Tuesday 12th), and the ownership (I will escalate / I will own it). The facts are constant; only the distance and the order move. That is the dial — and it is why a learner who has only one setting is not direct or polite, just stuck.

The high-context column is not longer on purpose — it is framed: something true and warm first, the hard information in the middle, and a route out at the end. If your version cannot be read as an invitation to keep talking, it is probably a rupture.

6. Face — the wording that saves it

Face is the currency of every high-context room, and it is bought with four small moves: take the blame first, offer the exit, criticise in the third person, and never correct someone in front of their boss.

The situationYour rewrite
You did not understand the supplier's refusal and want to name it in the call.
You must tell the marketing lead that her date cannot hold.
You disagree with the chair's estimate, in front of the board.
You need to correct a colleague's number — with their director present.
The four phrasings: "Perhaps I've misunderstood — I had the twelfth" (blame first) · "If that's not possible, tell me and we'll find another way" (the exit) · "Some teams have found that this approach runs late" (third person) · "Can I check one figure with you after this?" (private, and it names the reason). None of them is weak; all four keep the information and hand the other person a route out.

7. Reading the room — with a caution

Silence, pace and interruption are information, not rudeness — but the page refuses to give you a lookup table, because the table is wrong as often as it is right. Three judgements, then the honest instruction underneath.

1.You propose a date and the line goes silent for six seconds.
2.The Dutch engineer interrupts you twice in a minute.
3.The supplier says nothing for the whole call and then e-mails a refusal.
4.The marketing lead talks over the agenda for four minutes.
5.The reliable response to all four is…
6.The caution is stated because…
The instruction, not the table: silence in a four-country call can be thought, rejection, translation, or a dropped connection. You cannot know which from your chair — you can only know it after two meetings, in writing. So: treat the pause as neutral, ask once, and confirm the decision in writing the same day. Nothing in that procedure depends on guessing a culture correctly.

8. Accommodation — three moves, not an accent

Accommodating is not mimicry. It is three moves — slower (not louder), shorter clauses, fewer idioms — plus writing the key line down and checking it back with "so who does what by when?". One sentence, four accommodated versions, spoken:

The versionWhat changed
"Let's park the timeline and pick it up offline — the numbers are in the ballpark." the original: three idioms in eleven words
"We will not decide the date today. The numbers look roughly correct. I will send the details in writing." idioms gone; three short clauses; one action
"Date: not today. Numbers: roughly correct. Next: I send it in writing." spoken summary — three labels, no grammar load
"So — no date today, numbers roughly right, and I'll write it up. Is that the same as you understood?" the check appended — the move that makes the other three count

9. The decision, in writing

One decision came out of that call — or rather, one decision is now possible because the vagueness has been named. Write it as the record: decisions, owners, dates, and the two things that were not agreed.
