A real condition about the future

The condition takes the present, the result takes will

A first conditional names one possible future. The if-clause takes the present simple, and the other half takes will + base form: If the client confirms, we'll start on Monday. The condition is not yet true, but English puts it in the present because the clause names a fact that will exist at that moment.

The future is marked once, in the result. If the client will confirm is the error that marks a non-native speaker, and it comes straight from Portuguese se o cliente confirmar becoming confirmará in the speaker's head. The English clause never takes will.

A zero conditional states something true every time, with no exception: If you press this button, it saves automatically. Both halves take the present simple, and there is no will anywhere. The pair below shows the two groups side by side, because the verbs are the only thing that separates them.

The rule behind both is one rule, and it reaches every clause that names a future moment rather than a future fact: I'll call you when I get there, we'll review it before the client arrives, stay on the line until the file uploads. None of those clauses takes will either, because none of them is the promise. The promise sits in the other half, and English marks the future once.

Where the two halves sit

The clause can move, the comma moves with it

The if-clause can come first or second. First, it is followed by a comma: If it rains, we'll move it. Second, there is no comma: We'll move the meeting inside if it rains. The two sentences say the same thing; only the order of information changes.

The result half does not need will only. A modal or an instruction works in the same place: If it rains, we can meet online, If the file is too big, send a link instead. What stays fixed is the present simple inside the condition.

If it rains, we'll move it.

We'll move the meeting inside if it rains.

Reading the two marked lines side by side is the fastest check there is: the comma exists only where the condition comes first, and the present-rains sits in the same place in both.

In a slide or an email subject the condition comes second, because the proposal is what the reader is looking for: We'll start Monday if the client confirms.

The condition can be negative

If it doesn't rain, we'll stay outside. The negative goes on the verb in the if-clause, and the other half stays positive. Nothing moves to the result half.

In a meeting the if-clause is often the part you are unsure about, and the result half is the part you are proposing. Say the proposal first when you want the listener to keep it: We'll start on Monday if the client confirms.

Between colleagues the result half often carries a modal instead of will, and each one lowers the promise: we should be ready Friday, we can start as soon as the file lands, we'd need the numbers by Thursday. The condition stays in the present in all three, and only the strength of the promise changes.

A negative condition has a positive result, and the other way round: If the client doesn't sign, we don't ship. Only one not is needed, and it belongs to the half that carries the uncertainty.

The condition can also hide in a noun: In case of rain, we meet inside drops the clause and keeps the meaning, and a sign or a heading uses that shorter form.

Two negatives in the same sentence cancel, so the listener hears the opposite: keep one not, always.

The words that build a condition

Six words, one job each

Each of these words carries one condition, and each changes what the if-clause does. Open the card at the word for its meaning.

otherwiseif not — it joins the consequence to the negative of the condition just given.

in case is not if

Take an umbrella if it rains means you take it when the rain starts. Take an umbrella in case it rains means you take it now, because rain is possible later. The first is a reaction; the second is a preparation, and a professional sentence about a risk usually wants the second.

unless saves a negative: Unless the client confirms, we can't start is If the client does not confirm, we can't start. The verb after unless stays positive, so unless the client doesn't confirm says the opposite of what the speaker means.

unless and otherwise are the two negative frames, and they stand on opposite sides of the same sentence. Unless we hear back today, we'll postpone puts the negative condition first; We need an answer today, otherwise we'll postpone names what is missing and puts the consequence after it. Both report one risk, and the second is the shape a status email usually takes.

Planning a launch on a condition

Every sentence is a condition or a commitment

In a planning exchange one speaker gives the condition and the other gives the commitment: If the supplier confirms today, we'll launch on Monday. The condition stays in the present, and the commitment carries will.

When the date is uncertain, the condition is what protects the plan: As long as nothing breaks, we'll be ready Friday. When the risk is a preparation, the word is in case: Book the room now, in case the calendar fills up.

The wrong version of this conversation is a series of wills: If the supplier will confirm, we will launch. The listener hears a non-native sentence and the plan sounds less certain than it is.

The answer to a condition has a shape of its own. A colleague who asks What if the date moves? expects the plan that covers it — Then we'll move the announcement too — and not a repeat of the question. The word then is what makes the answer read as a commitment rather than a shrug.

Should has one more job here, and it is the formal one: Should the client confirm today, we'll launch on Monday means the same as If the client confirms today, with if dropped and the word order inverted. It belongs in writing to a client, where the shorter form sounds blunt.
