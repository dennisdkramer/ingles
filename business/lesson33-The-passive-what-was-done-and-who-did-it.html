The passive: what was done, and who did it

The thing done becomes the subject

The shift, in one pair

An active sentence puts the doer first: Two reviewers verify every change before the release. A passive sentence promotes the thing acted on to the subject and leaves the verb as be + the past participle: Every change is verified before the release.

The facts did not change; the subject did, and the subject decides what the sentence is about. A report, an audit trail and a process description name the thing that was done, because the things are what a reader tracks and the person who did them is unknown, obvious, or recorded somewhere else.

The past participle is the form the perfect already uses — submitted, delivered, signed off, sent — and the passive puts it after be instead of after have. Regular verbs add -ed; irregular verbs do not, and some of them repeat the past form (send → sent, make → made) while others do not (write → written, do → done). The pair that differs is the one to memorise, and the lesson lists six of them together.

The be carries the tense and the agreement: is verified now, was verified then, has been verified up to now. The participle never changes, because the time is already in the be.

The invoice was sent on Monday.

All changes are verified before the release.

Read the marks: the subject is the thing acted on, be carries the tense, the participle is the verb, and the doer — when the sentence names it — follows by. The second sentence has no doer at all, which is the subject of the next section.

by, or nobody at all

The doer is optional — and that is the point

When the doer carries information the reader needs, the passive names it after by: The complaint was handled by the support team in one call. When the doer is unknown, obvious, or already recorded, the passive leaves it out: The invoice was sent on Monday, The fix was delivered at nine.

The same construction reports a decision without naming the person who took it: It was agreed that we'd move the date. It is an empty subject, was agreed is the passive, and the decision belongs to the group. The frame is fixed — It was agreed / decided / confirmed / reported that … — and it is how minutes, memos and announcements are written.

The frame takes a second half that keeps its own tense, which is where a sentence like it was agreed that the date would be moved gets its shape: the agreeing happened then, the moving was still to come.

Three moments where the doer is left out on purpose. Unknown: nobody knows who did it. Obvious: the change is verified by us, and saying so adds nothing. Politeness: The mistake was made in the first version states what happened without pointing at a person — and a manager hears the difference immediately.

by names the doer; with names the tool or the method. The file was sent by the system credits the machine, while the file was sent with one click says how it happened. One sentence can carry both: The report was checked with the new script by the analyst on duty.

Read the pair as a question of credit and of method: by answers who, with answers how, and a report needs both only when both are news.

One caution, and it weighs more at this level: dropping the doer to avoid responsibility is a recognised habit, and readers notice it. Use the passive for the thing, not to hide the person who should be named.

The words a passive sentence is built from

Ten participles, and what each one means

by and by mistake share a word and nothing else: one names a doer, the other says the action was not intended. Keep them apart by asking what would follow by — a person or a machine, never an adverb.

The passive takes the time expressions the course already taught — on Monday, before the release, nightly, by nine — and be carries the tense while they stay where they were: The invoice was sent on Monday. All changes are verified before the release. The tests are run nightly.

On time is worth a note: it means at the moment that was agreed, not early. The fix was delivered on time is a promise kept; in time only means before it was too late. A report that says on time is reporting a promise, and a report that says early is reporting a bonus.

Seven participles that do not take -ed

Half the list changes and half does not, and the passive takes the third column either way. The deck was written and the second option was chosen use a form that is not the past; the invoice was paid, the review was held and the file was sent use a form that repeats it. So neither guess is safe: a learner who adds -ed produces was writed, and one who trusts the past form produces was wrote.

The third column is the one to look up, and it is the same column the perfect uses: I have written it and it has been written share both words.

Two more join the same list, because a report uses them daily: build → built → built and sell → sold → sold. The forms that repeat are the ones English made early, and the ones that change are the ones a reader notices.

One participle, five tenses

The be carries the tense, the perfect and the modal

At this level the passive is not three forms but five, and only be changes: is submitted (a rule), was submitted (finished, and the day is known), has been submitted (finished, and the result is what matters now), had been submitted (finished before another past moment), will be submitted (later), must be submitted (a requirement). The participle is the same word every time, because the time and the obligation already sit in front of it.

Agreement moves with the subject as it always does: The report is submitted, The reports are submitted, The numbers were checked. In the perfect forms be becomes been and the auxiliary carries the tense: has been, had been.

The five forms also differ in what they let a report leave out: is submitted states a rule with no date, has been submitted states a result with no date, and was submitted is the only one that usually brings a date with it.

That is why a process document is written in the present passive and a weekly report in the past: the first describes what always happens, the second what happened once.

Two errors carry most of the weight, and both sit at the front of the verb. Leaving be out leaves the sentence with no tense of its own: The report submitted by two reviewers is not a sentence. Using the past form instead of the participle breaks the pattern twice: was wrote is not English, and was submit is not either.

The repair is one question: where is the be, and is the word after it a past participle? In the perfect the question is where been is.

One more habit keeps the five forms apart: say the auxiliary out loud before writing. is, was, has been, had been, will be, must be — six openings, one participle, and the sentence is built before the pen moves.

A modal never takes a participle on its own: must signed off is missing the be, exactly as will delivered would be.

Read the four faults in the exercise as the four questions this page has asked: is been there, is the participle right, does be agree, and is there a be after the modal?

Answer them in that order and the repair is mechanical, which is what a report needs at eleven at night.

Who did it, and does it matter

Five passives missing from one conversation

Look at turns two and three together. Jess asks Who sent it? — active, because the person is what she wants. Mike answers in the passive, because the answer is a machine and the invoice is what matters.

That is the whole choice, made in a real exchange: name the doer when the doer is new information; leave it out when it is not. The last turn is the third use: It was agreed that every deck gets two reviewers reports the decision without naming who agreed, which is how a meeting's outcome is written down.

Turn five is the polite passive: it was not checked by anyone else states a gap without blaming Ana.

The same exchange shows why a report is written in the passive and a conversation is not. Jess asks Who sent it? in the active because she wants a person; Mike answers It was sent on Monday because the invoice is what he can report, and the sender turns out to be a machine.

Compare the two halves of turn four: The invoice was sent has no doer at all, and the deck was written by Ana names one. The difference is not politeness or style — it is whether the name carries information the listener did not have.

Turn two and turn four also carry the tense difference the lesson taught: is sent automatically every month is a rule, and was sent on Monday is one finished occasion. Both are passives, and the be is the only word that changed.

The last turn is the frame minutes use: It was agreed that every deck gets two reviewers. The decision is reported, the person who proposed it is not, and the sentence is complete without either — which is exactly what a meeting note needs.
