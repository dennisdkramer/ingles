Repair

What this lesson installs: the two repairs, and the fact that they are one skill. Repairing yourself — I meant the Tuesday before the go-live, not this Tuesday — and repairing the other person — do you mean the client's date or ours? — are the same act: you noticed a gap and closed it out loud instead of hoping. In real conversation they alternate inside one minute, so the page trains them together. The spine is a clock: repair at second three and it costs a clause; at second thirty it costs the paragraph; unrepaired it costs a week of work. One more thing sits underneath, and it is the expensive one: nodding when you have not understood. Nodding is the only error in this page with a cost measured in money.

1. The call that was never repaired

Eleven turns, and the whole fortnight is decided in the fifth one. Listen ▶ first — it is fast, it uses three acronyms, and one number matters. Then find the line where nobody asked.

Ana: "So it's the Tuesday before the go-live, not the go-live itself. Handover's the Wednesday."

You: "Sure."

Ana: "Right — the UAT pack has to be signed off by then, and legal want the DPA annex in the same bundle."

You: "OK."

Ana: "The 'by then' is the tricky bit, because the annex usually takes five working days, and we've got four."

You: "Understood."

Ana: "So either legal move, or the bundle goes without the annex and we take the risk. Your call, really."

You: "Fine — I'll sort it."

Ana: "Great. I'll copy you into the thread with the auditors."

You: "Perfect."

Ana: "And the Q3 numbers go in the same pack, but that's Priya's."

What was actually asked of youWhat you did
sign off the UAT pack by the Tuesday before go-livechased the go-live date instead
get the DPA annex into the same bundle, in four days, when it takes fiveasked legal for it on the Thursday — a day late
make a call: legal moves, or the bundle ships without the annextold the client the bundle was complete
What it cost: eleven days, one escalated e-mail from the auditors, and a conversation with your director that began "so you didn't ask?". The repair belonged in the fifth turn — sorry, by then means the Tuesday? and the annex is four days? — which would have cost one clause and produced the right task. Unrepaired understanding is not a language problem; it is a bill that arrives later.
Count the nods. Five turns answered with sure, OK, understood, fine, perfect — and not one of them was true. This is the whole lesson in one observation: the failure was not a missing word, it was a missing question, and it happened at second three, when the gap was still one clause wide.

The good answers are all short and all end in a question or a guess: "By then means the Tuesday, yes?" · "So the annex has four days, not five — is that the risk?" · "You mean I choose between legal and the annex?" None of them is polite filler. All three buy the whole task.

3. Two repairs, one skill

One chart, four rows, and the last column is the part nobody teaches: the moment after which the repair stops being cheap.

TypeWhat you sayWhat it does to the conversationToo late when…
self-repair"I meant Tuesday, not this Tuesday." you correct yourself before anyone else has to; costs one clauseyou have already acted on the wrong version
self-repair, restart"Let me start that again." buys a clean sentence and removes the wreckage of the first onethe listener has already started guessing what you meant

The two middle columns are the same move from opposite sides: somebody noticed a gap and said so out loud. The fourth column is why the clock matters — every one of these is cheap in the first three seconds and expensive after the meeting.
The three-second rule, stated plainly: the wobble is cheapest at the instant you feel it. Waiting for a natural pause is waiting for the moment when the pause is no longer natural; waiting for the end of the call means the summary you write will contain the error, politely formatted.

B. Going around the missing word

Never stop mid-sentence. Three routes: function (what it does), category (what family it belongs to), opposite (what it is not).

1. annex —

2. sign-off —

3. penalty clause —

4. go-live —

5. stakeholder —

6. backlog —

The rule under the drill: a paragraph finished with an approximate word beats a perfect word that arrives after the silence. Nobody in the history of meetings has been impressed by a pause; people are impressed by the person who kept the sentence moving.

5. The hypothesis question

The strongest clarification move in English is not a plea, it is a guess. Could you explain that another way? hands the whole job back and usually produces the same words, louder. Do you mean the client's date or ours? is answerable with one word and shows exactly where you are stuck. Six ambiguous statements — offer a guess and ask, in one breath, every time.

The statementYour guess (a hypothesis, not a question)Your question
"Send it over before the end of the week if you can."
"We should probably loop in the wider team."
"The number looked high to me."
"Let's park that for now."
"It's the same as last time, broadly."
"I'd rather we didn't over-engineer this."
The shape is fixed: guess, then question — "So you mean by Friday, but not Friday afternoon — is that right?" Guess first because a guess is a contribution and a question is a cost. Notice what the guess also does: it lets the other person say yes and move on, which is the whole point of a meeting.

7. Done — the summary that saves it

The professional version of did I get that? is three parts, in this order: what is needed, by when, because. Say it back at the end of every call and the other person repairs your understanding for free — and learns, in that moment, that you can be trusted with the task.

PartYour sentence
1 · whatSo you need .
2 · by whenby .
3 · becausebecause .
Model: "So you need the UAT pack signed off and the DPA annex in the same bundle, by the Tuesday before go-live, because legal need five days and we have four." Thirty-one words, and every one of them is checkable. If the summary is wrong, the fix costs one sentence now instead of eleven days later.

8. The three-second clock

Four moments, one rule: the repair starts before the pause ends. Select each line, record ⏺ your turn within three seconds of the snippet — the guess first, the question second.

#What you hearYour repair starts…
1"We'll need the CBAs before sign-off." with a guess, not a plea
2"Same cadence as last quarter, roughly." with a number you propose
3"Legal can move if we ask them nicely." with the risk named
4"It's the Tuesday before the go-live." by repeating the date and checking it
Record all four in one take. If a repair takes more than three seconds to start, it will not happen in a real room — and the silence is what the other person reads as agreement.
