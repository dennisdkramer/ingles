The past you can point at

The past is behind now, and -ed is the whole tense

The finishing form

The simple past says one thing: the action finished before now. In an affirmative sentence the -ed is the whole tense — nothing else is added. I sent the file yesterday. She called me twice. The verb carries the time on its own.

Two shapes do that job. The regular form adds -ed: called, watched, started. The irregular form has no ending at all: sent, met, bought, went. Both are past, and neither of them takes did.

Time words point at the finished moment: yesterday, last week, last night, a year ago, on Monday, this morning. The sentence works without them, but with them the listener knows exactly where to stand and look.

Portuguese maps this cheaply — enviei is also one word. The cost sits somewhere else: in the irregular forms, and in what happens the moment the sentence turns negative or asks a question. That is the third section of the page.

The arrow leaves sent and lands in the dark half of the line. The action has a place on the line, and the place is before now.

How -ed is written. Most verbs take it as it is: call → called, watch → watched. A verb that ends in a consonant after one vowel doubles it: plan → planned, stop → stopped. A verb that ends in a consonant and -y turns the y into i: try → tried, study → studied.

The spelling rules are worth ten minutes once, because they are the same three every time. The irregulars are the list you carry — the chart in the third section is that list.

was and were: be changes shape instead of taking a helper

The one verb that does not take -ed

be is the exception, and it is the most common verb in the language. It does not add -ed and it never takes did. It swaps its form: was with I, he, she, it and were with you, we, they.

Everything else follows from that. The negative is wasn't and weren't — the past is already in the verb, so did has nothing to do. The question moves the verb to the front: Were you at the office?

The last row is the one you already know from there is / there are, moved to the past: There was a call at nine. There were five people in the room.

The verbs that carry the past with no -ed

The list you carry instead of a rule

Eight verbs carry the past with no ending, and they are eight of the ones a working week uses most. Some change one letter — send → sent, meet → met. Some change almost everything — buy → bought, go → went. There is no rule to apply; there is a list to use until it is known.

Two checks keep the list honest. The time word tells you the sentence is past (yesterday, last week, a year ago). And the verb in the sentence is the past form itself — no did, no -ed beside it.

One ending, three sounds

The -ed is written every time — and said in three ways

The ending is always written -ed, and it is said in one of three ways. After a whisper it ends in a whisper: walked(walked), watched(watched). After a sound that hums it hums: called(called).

After t or d the ending becomes a syllable of its own, because those two sounds cannot stand beside themselves: started(star·ted), needed(nee·ded).

The five are spoken at the foot of this section. Read the key first, guess the sound, then press and check your guess.

Read the keys in the way the page always reads them: the grey letters are written and not said, and the dot is a break between syllables. Walked and called keep one beat from beginning to end — the ending is greyed because the syllable was already there. Started and needed gain a beat, and the key shows the word broken again.

Say the three pairs out loud before you answer the dictations. The ear learns this faster than the page can explain it.

did takes the past, and the verb goes home

One past per sentence

When the sentence turns negative or asks a question, the past moves into did — and did is already past, so the main verb must go home to its base form. She didn't call back, never She didn't called back. Did you get my email?, never Did you got my email?

This is lesson 5's rule at a new time. The slot carries the tense and the main verb returns to base; the slot now holds did where it used to hold do or does. Two pasts cannot stand in one sentence — if you hear did and an -ed together, one of them has to go, and the one that goes is the ending on the verb.

The short answer keeps did alone: Did you get it? — Yes, I did. The verb does not come back in the answer.

Your week, said in the past

Four shapes, and the one you need

Everything on this page is one of four shapes. The verb alone carries the past: I sent it. The verb be swaps: we were late. Did carries the past in a question or a negative and the verb goes home: did she call?, she didn't call. And a short answer keeps did alone: yes, she did.

A status update is usually three of those in a row, in this order: what happened, what did not happen, and what the answer to a question was. She called at nine. She didn't send the numbers. Did we follow up? Yes, we did. That is a whole Monday in three sentences, and each one has its own shape.
