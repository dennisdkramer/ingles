2. Two years, not “since two years ago”

Build the sentence in Portuguese first — “treino há dois anos” — and the English version arrives with the wrong preposition and total confidence.

A. The rule, in three lines

Pattern: I've been doing CrossFit for two years. I've been doing CrossFit since 2021.

Means: for + a length of time (two years, six weeks, a month). since + the point where it started (2021, March, I was twenty-two).

Use when: the thing has not stopped. “Since two years ago” is “desde dois anos atrás” wearing English clothes; the point is already inside since, so the “ago” has nowhere to go.

4. Even though I had a cold

The concession — “yes, but I did it anyway” — takes four different shapes in English, and Portuguese hands you “mesmo que”, “apesar de” and “embora” for all four. The cue in the sentence picks the form; you don't.

A. The cue decides the form

The CueWhat it forcesExample
even thougha fact that is true, and the result happened anywayEven though I had a cold, I finished every event.
even ifa possibility — it may or may not happenEven if I'd been healthy, I'd still have missed the cut-off.
although / thoughthe same fact, quieter and more writtenAlthough I was sick, I warmed up for twelve minutes.
despite / in spite ofa noun or an -ing form — never a clauseDespite the cold, I made it to the bar. / Despite feeling awful, I competed.
buttwo full clauses, balanced against each otherI had a cold, but I still competed.
The trap is the translation: despite I had a cold is not English — despite cannot take a subject and a verb, so the clause has to become a noun (despite the cold) or an -ing form (despite feeling awful). And even though is a fact, even if is a guess.

6. Ninety minutes before my heat

A. What I did, and in what order

warm up stretch weight vest a few minutes a little time

A warm-up raises muscle temperature and heart rate; it is what makes the first rep feel like the fiftieth. A stretch does a different job — it lengthens a muscle, and it warms nothing up. I did twelve minutes of one and four of the other, in that order, and then put on the 9-kg weight vest, which is why the jumping felt heavier than training ever did.

Between events I had a few minutes, not a little time. “A few” counts what you can count — minutes, reps, athletes. “A little” measures what you can't — time, chalk, luck, patience. So: a few minutes, a little time.

8. e-VENT, ATH-lete, com-pe-TI-tion

English puts the beat on one syllable and lets the others go quiet. Land the beat one syllable early and the word is still understandable — and still wrong. The word is e-VENT, not “E-vent”.

A. Where the beat lands

The wordWhere the beat isWhat goes wrong
e-VENT second of twothe first vowel almost disappears: ih-VENT
ATH-lete first of two“a-THLE-te” invents a third beat and a third vowel
com-pe-TI-tion third of fourthe first two beats get flattened into one
CAT-e-go-ry first of fourthe last vowel turns into a full “o” it never gets
in-ter-ME-di-ate third of fivemy classification, and my least favorite word of the weekend
re-CU-per-ate second of four“re-cu-pe-RA-te” — the stress walks to the end
WARM-up first of twothe noun is WARM-up; the verb warms UP
Same written word, different beat, different job: a WARM-up is what you do before the event, and you warm UP before the event. English moves the beat to the front for the noun and leaves it on the particle for the verb.

9. The post-mortem

Sixty words, in his voice, with the numbers left in. Writing it is the part that makes the vocabulary yours.

A. Write your own Sunday

Use five of these: sore muscles, sore loser, double under, out of breath, bleachers, warm-up, held me back, a piece of cake, come up short, get over, either way. Include one sentence with for or since, one with even though or even if, and one number (I missed… / I still had… to go / I came up… short).

Model: “I came up eight short on event one, even though the rope is usually a piece of cake. I've been doing double unders for two years, and the cold held me back. Either way, I went alone and I came back with numbers.”
