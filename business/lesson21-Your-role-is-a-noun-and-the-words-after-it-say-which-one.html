Your role is a noun, and the words after it say which one

Your job is a noun, and the words after it say which one

Analyst is a noun. On its own it does not identify you: many people are analysts. The words after it say which analyst you are — the analyst in the pricing team, the analyst who prepares the forecast.

The noun is the head of the phrase. The words after it hang off the head, and each group answers a question about it: which analyst, where in the company, about what.

O português faz a mesma coisa, com as palavras depois do substantivo: o analista da equipe de preços. A ordem é a mesma — o que muda é a palavra de ligação.

An introduction has two parts, in this order: the name, then the role. Two noun phrases for one person, one after the other, with a dash or a comma between them — I'm Ana — I'm the analyst who prepares the forecast.

The role half is where a Brazilian stalls. Who prepares the forecast is part of the role, not a new sentence: the sentence does not end at analyst. Say the whole phrase, or the listener gets a job title and no information.

I'm the analyst who prepares the forecast.

The marks name the grammatical function of each word: the subject (I), the mode ('m), the object (analyst), and the words that follow that object. The role is in the last two.

I'm Ana, I'm analyst who prepares the forecast.

I'm Ana — I'm the analyst who prepares the forecast.

Nine phrases answer the question "what do you do?"

These are the phrases colleagues use at work, and each one says a different thing about the role. The three with a button open a card: they are the ones a Brazilian gets wrong, because the closest Portuguese phrase is not the closest meaning.

names the person who decides. names the person whose work it is. names the person who keeps it running day to day.

Em português as três caem em responsável por e cuido de. Em inglês cada uma responde a uma pergunta diferente, e a diferença aparece quando alguém precisa saber quem decide.

in charge ofthe person who decides and answers for the result — I'm in charge of the monthly report. Authority, not only work.
responsible forthe work is yours to do, and someone may check it — I'm responsible for the models. The task, not necessarily the decision.
look afterkeep something working from one day to the next — I look after the invoicing. Care, not authority.
  • part of — one of several inside a larger thing: I'm part of the pricing team.
  • on the team — named as a member, among the others: Ana is on the team.
  • I work in — the area you sit in, never at: I work in pricing.
  • I run — in charge of it over time: I run the Monday call.
  • the one who — identifies a person by what they do: I'm the one who prepares the forecast.
  • these days — now, compared with before: I'm on the revenue side these days.

The three phrases are not a scale from polite to strong. Each one answers a different question a colleague is asking: who decides?, whose work is it?, who keeps it running?

In a meeting, the answer changes what the other person does next. I'm responsible for the models gets you a reviewer. I'm in charge of the models gets you the decision, and the questions that come with it.

Use in charge of quando você decide e responde pelo resultado; responsible for quando o trabalho é seu mas alguém pode conferir; look after quando você mantém funcionando.

The first minute asks a question, and the answer returns it

You will hear What do you do? more than any other question at work. It is not a request for your job title; it is a request for the head word and the words after it.

The answer ends by returning the question — And you? or What about you? Two words give the question back, and the other person does not have to find an opening.

The same answer works on a new boss and on a colleague: I prepare the forecast, and I work with the sales team.

Responder e devolver a pergunta é o que separa uma resposta de uma conversa. Duas palavras fazem isso: And you?

  1. JessWhat do you do, Ana?
  2. AnaI look after the models, so I'm on the revenue side. And you?
  3. JessNothing has changed for me — I still run the Monday call. What about you?

Nothing has changed is the careful answer to what's new. Between colleagues you know, it comes out as same as always: keep the shorter form for a colleague, never for a boss or a client.

Two habits for the end of the sentence:

  • Never stop at the job title. I'm an analyst says what your job is; I'm the analyst in the pricing team says which analyst you are.
  • Return the question. And you? is two words and it costs nothing.
