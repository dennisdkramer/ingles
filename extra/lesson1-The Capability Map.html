What we can teach with

The visuals a lesson is built from, each drawn on this page.

Every word inside a block below is example content. What a block contributes is its shape: the markup in the reveal under it, its look, its span.

How a page gets built

Arrange it, and give it a look

A section's content is arranged however its teaching needs it. A row is one line, and its direct children are its cells; a cell holds as many items as its content needs — one, two or several — and its share of the line is settled by that content against the cells beside it when the page loads. A section can hold as many rows as it asks for, or none: a block, a chart or a paragraph can stand directly in it. .row is only the utility for a line of chips or buttons — never a layout row.

A section can contain multiple ideas, a block can contain multiple activities, teaching elements and rows of content. Division without need is noise. A division earns its place by helping the student read the page — which can mean setting elements apart so they are seen as distinct, two tenses side by side or a contrast — and never by separating elements that need no separation.

Every element below is drawn with the markup that builds it, and the drawing is the default: what the element contains, what text it carries, where that text sits, and whether it carries a title at all. Its item count is the example's own, never a count to copy. A chart takes no title of its own — the sentence beside it names it.

data-look is declared on the block, never inferred from what it holds, and changes the paint only.

Looks, and why each is chosen: float for text that applies to everything in the section and belongs to no single block · tint for content about the track's own voice — how a word taught in that voice compares with the same word in another voice, what it really means or the use cases a slang word has · outline for content important enough to stand out from the page · stripes for an activity the student answers, whose top rule turns red · tape for a note pinned to the page rather than taught in sequence · dark for content that is a night, a screen or a stage. One tint and one outline per section at most. A section is an <h2>: the sheet numbers it, so it is never typed, and it restarts the lettering under it. An unset block is paper with the 2px accent rule. The markup box you are reading is <details class="reveal" open><summary>…</summary><div class="body"><div><pre>…</pre></div></div></details>.

Choosing a visual

The mapping

The first column is the occasion, the second the visual that answers it, and every name opens it drawn. A concept with no row is one no visual shows yet. None of these items is mandatory or even preferred: a text-only explanation is equally valid if it accomplishes the teaching goal of its block. The lesson teaches; a visual is one of the ways its example is drawn. The order of the items here means nothing — it is not a ranking, a precedence or a sequence, and nothing is more important for coming first.

Standalone visuals

A chart, a list, a dialog, a drawing, a recording or a strip is a thing of its own. Each sits inside a block with the prose that reads it, or stands outside at its own width.

.viz frames three things: the dialog, the player and the map — a chart and a strip carry a frame of their own. It is never a second .block.

A chart can stand outside a block or sit inside one, and either way the text beside it has to explain it or point at it — a chart nobody refers to is a picture the student has to guess at. The chart below is that pair: the sentence introduces it, and the chart centres itself in the column it is given.

A column header is content, and it earns its row the way every other word does: it must name something the student has to tell apart — a contrast, a category, or the question a column answers (Written / Said, Now / Past, Which one?). A header that only says what a column obviously holds — The sentence, What it means, The form, The piece, Letter — says nothing and is not written: the header row is collapsed to the same 3px rule the chart below uses.

The stylesheet carries the same note, above its collapsed-header rule, because every chart on the site is held to it: no heading is the default, and an omitted header row is collapsed, never deleted — <thead><tr class="bare"><th colspan="N"></th></tr></thead>, where N is the table's real column count. The chart keeps its top edge, and the row says nothing it does not need to say.

Marked activities with controls

An activity is the part of a page the student writes in, and it carries its own instruction in whatever block holds it. An activity is a unit the student completes — never a control on its own. The four controls — a gap, a radio, a checkbox, a written field — are the parts an activity is built from and are chosen by what the student must do; they are not activities themselves. A keyed gap is preferentially carried inside one of the forms below — a dialog turn, a chart row to answer, a strip under its panel, a line to translate, a sentence to correct, a line to write from what they hear — and a lone gap is only ever a component beside other teaching in a block, never an activity in its own right. The rows below hold the forms: numbered chunks, a chart whose cells are answered, and under the chunks a correction and a dictation — the last two for no beginner, and the sentence they ask for always tuned to the lesson's level. The two blocks after this one, side by side, are four options to pick one from and a matching pool. The last block in the section is fill-in-the-blank: a line the student completes — typed into a gap, or picked from three or four options that fill the same blank. Every item the student answers is numbered — <span class="qnum">1.</span> beside the control it numbers, and the count runs through the activity block, so another activity starts again at 1 — so a teacher can name the item they mean.

data-answer keys a gap, | separating alternatives; --gap-w is 1.3× the width of the longest accepted answer, the field's own padding included, written in em — a gap with no key takes 1.3× the prompt written beside it, or 9em. Never 100% and never calc(): a field as wide as its block has stopped showing its answer, and on its own line it stops being a gap. A radio group's key is data-a on .qitem; engine2.js marks the right option and writes .ans beside a typed field.

Each of these can stand as a block of its own or sit inside a block with the prose, the chart or the drawing it accompanies; every row below is the in-block size. A block may carry more than one of them, and any mix of teaching, activity and content: division without need is noise.

Games

Don’t add a game just to have one. A game is a skin over an activity the lesson already wanted: the activity is chosen first and the game is how it is presented, so a reskinned game is that element in the ledger and never an element of its own. Nothing counts a game and nothing requires one. A game is a flavour element: it is used occasionally, and only where it fits a particular activity well enough that the activity would be worse without it. A set of items that trains fine as a list stays a list.

A .game draws its own edge, so it stands at page level and is never put inside a .block. Every game reads the same content — .game-list > .qitem, each with a .qstem and radio options, data-a marking the right one — so that list is what prints and what the student answers if the script never runs; data-game chooses only the presentation, never the content. A game in a quiz section carries data-retry="0"; a training game carries nothing. A .listen in the item's stem makes the round SPOKEN: the runner says its data-say on the frame the round opens and keeps a 🔊 replay on the HUD, so a game can ask "which word did you hear?" — data-sound="0" mutes both, and bingoCard is the one presentation that times its own clip and is left alone.

Learned during build generation

What building a lesson has taught the build

Every line here is a mechanism of this site, learned by building a lesson on it, and it holds for the next lesson as much as for the one that found it. A line states what the machine does or what the build must do — never a fault, a session or a date. Read the block at the start of every lesson; the lesson that teaches one more line adds it here before its own row is closed.

  • A row is sized at load, not authored. equalize.js writes the widths: a table keeps its widest row, a drawing never passes its own size, and prose takes the remainder above 200px — but beside a table or a drawing the prose stops at its widest line, and that cell takes the slack. An authored flex or width on a cell is stale and the solver overwrites it. A question cell or a list cell also keeps a floor at its widest authored item, and it is spaced at load: the gaps between its numbered items (or between its list's items) are opened or closed until the cell meets the row's tallest cell that cannot space, each gap held between 6px and its authored gap + 48px. The solver strips that spacing from the saved clone only; the live page keeps it.
  • Every vertical gap is its container's, at one value per level. @layer spacing gives the content area's grid a 20px row gap, a frame's direct children 20px, and a cell's direct children 14px; a cell's first child and its answer fields are 0, so every cell starts flush and a short cell's blank sits below its content — except in a two-cell row, where a gap of more than 12px that width cannot close centers the shorter cell's content instead — and a question cell's or a list cell's own gaps are spread or compressed before that decision, so centering decides only what spacing cannot close. No element carries a vertical margin or padding of its own at those levels; h1 and h2 keep theirs.
  • A row's authored balance can only be read before the solver runs. equalize.js equalizes a row by redistributing widths, then by spreading a question cell's own gaps, then by centring a two-cell row's shorter cell — so a height measured on the finished page reports the solver's work, not the author's. EqualizeRows.clean() restores the authored page (widths, align-self, justify-content, the spacing lever's margins); with every cell held at align-self:flex-start each rect is that cell's own content, and two cells differing by more than 30px there are a row whose shorter cell is missing content. npm run align reads it at 1280 and 1024px.
  • A squeeze is read in the solved state, a height in the authored one. The solver hands width to whatever needs it — a table keeps its widest row, a drawing never passes its own size — so a chip run or an answer line in the cell beside them can be left narrower than its content and wrap. npm run align reads the page twice: the solved widths first (~~, wants 479px in 189px (chips on 6 rows)), then clean() for the authored heights. A squeeze is repaired by the structure — the row broken, the wide cell given the line — and never by an item, which answers a shortage of content and not of width.
  • A page that loads the solver is hidden until its rows are sized. data-eq on <html> makes the sheet hold .content-area at visibility:hidden; equalize.js solves once, then writes eq-ready when the fonts settle or after 1.5s. The page loads under a 50% veil with its paper, frame, header and content transparent; at eq-ready the veil fades evenly for 1s while the paper, frame and header fade in, and at 0.8s the content begins its own 1s fade (1.8s). A 4s/5s CSS fallback shows a page whose script never ran; the editor's body.layout-on and body.editing stay visible. The header wrapper holds the brand, the control bar and its legend, so the fade and every header rule reach all three.
  • A block reports its own progress. data-state="done" on a .block is the sheet's green frame (#80 STATE), and on a page whose <html> carries data-done engine2.js writes it: every question in the block has an answer on it — a gap with at least one character, a written field with text, one option picked in each radio group, at least one box ticked in each group of checkboxes — and a question emptied takes the state away again. A teaching block holds no control and never reports. The saved clone strips the state, so a file never keeps the report of the student who saved it.
  • The control's name is its key, and the key is a number — a<section>c<block letter><item> (a7c2 is section 7's third block, second item), one number for a whole radio group. A control left unnamed falls back to the words around it plus a {block, offset} position.
  • That offset counts blocks on the page, and a game inserts its stage into its own block when it loads — so a position saved before a game ran and read after it can be off by that stage. A number does not move; a position can.
  • Two controls in one cell take two numbers. Both share the cell's words and its first offset, and Complete draws one key pill per control: two fields in one cell need two numbers, or one answer is drawn over the other.
  • The index files are build artifacts that ship with the page. npm run build:lessons writes lessons.json and tracks.json from the page filenames, and --check refuses a stale pair; a lesson pushed without that run is live but unlisted.
  • Every page carries the sheet's stamp, and a solver page carries the solver's too. build-lessons.mjs writes the sheet's stamp into every page that links it — the lesson pages and the root pages, and any page the manifest does not list — and equalize.js?v=… into every page that loads it — the hash of the file itself — so a changed stylesheet or solver is fetched on the next load and a stale stamp fails build:check. The shell's script slot lists the other engines; a changed engine still needs a hard refresh.
  • This map is built to be extended. Every direct child of .content-area is a row cell and its data-span sums to 12 (12 when unset); a pre in a markup box holds escaped tags only, because a real one inside it is parsed as markup; node qa/cmcheck.mjs checks both, and the dead links.
  • A chart row is answered by a keyed gap. The typed form of chartanswer carries data-answer in the row's answer cell with its .qnum beside it; a row of options is markable only when its cell holds a .qitem with data-a, so a chart of bare radios has no key the engine can read.
  • A game records the control under it, not its own name. Items answered with options are mc in the ledger; items answered by typing a word are fill, because the field is what the student writes in.
  • The map redraws inside every .qitem. Two questions about one map are two drawings, and each checkbox keeps its place because its position is a percentage of the drawing, not of the cell.
  • A dictation's answer can be a gap. .listen speaks its data-say text and the field that follows it takes data-answer; a one-word answer needs no textarea.write, and the block is still a dictation.
  • A timeline family is a full-width child of its block. .tlgrid holds one <div> per figure with its <h4> name; inside a half-width row cell the pair is too narrow to read, so it sits under the prose rather than beside it.
  • The register total counts every <h2> in .content-area. A page with N teaching sections, a quiz section and the notebook reads reg:good/N+2, and a quiz heading that names the check ("The check: …") is still a section, because only quiz or test in the heading unmarks it.
  • Novelty is arithmetic on the ledger, not a judgement. batchcheck requires one element outside the union of the previous three rows, and skips the rule once those rows already hold all twelve palette elements — nothing is fresh then, and no markup choice repairs it — so churn and coverage carry freshness from that point.
  • A section subject is exactly three letters. The section pattern is ^S\d+-([a-z]{3}), so ex can head a block but never a section: a production section is recorded as rcp or mng, and an unparsed segment is reported as segment not understood rather than guessed at.
  • The quiz's freshness is counted separately. qel is compared with the previous two quizzes, not with the page's three-row window, so a quiz needs one element that neither of the last two quizzes used even when the page already satisfies churn.
  • Order is a run-length check, not a rule about shape. The section subjects are compared with the previous three rows: three in a row warns and four fails. The repair is a subject that is honest about what the section does — a rules section with an exception is exc, a tenses section is tim — never a reshuffle of the page to dodge the count.
  • An option set is reflowed by its own attribute. data-layout="row" on a .qopts set holds the set on one line, data-layout="grid" with style="--cols:2" wraps it into two columns, and a set with neither stacks one option per line — so four short options read as 4×1, 2×2 or 1×4 by the length of the words rather than by habit, and the stem and its options stay in one cell. The rule is written after the .qgrid rules it refines, so it wins on source order at equal specificity.
  • A flip card's prompt is its back. The deck prints the text of the flipped face in .flip-ask, so the student reads the back's words and turns the card whose front matches them; the instruction states that direction, and the pairs are chosen for the decision they force rather than for the rule they repeat.
