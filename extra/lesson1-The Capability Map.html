Lesson Authoring Guidelines

Write the whole lesson in full, as the words the student reads — every paragraph, every dialog, every list, every activity item with its answer, every chart's rows. A placeholder, a note to self, or a line saying what a section will contain leaves the lesson unfinished: pass 2 builds what is written and cannot build what was only meant. Open the page by stating what the lesson teaches, in the first block: a scene, a dialog or a story is an example of content already stated, never the introduction.

Write it as it would read printed: what follows the opening, where the student practises, how the lesson closes — decided out of what this lesson teaches, never out of how the last page was built. A raw lesson that could be moved into another lesson's place by changing its words is a copy.

Divide the lesson where the teaching needs a division, and nowhere else. Nothing fixes how many sections the lesson has. Every division earns itself by what changes across it — a new concept, a contrast that only lands when its two sides are apart, a shift from explaining to practising — never by the length of the page, never by how many concepts the roadmap lists, and never by the shape of the lesson before it. A section holds everything its teaching needs: several ideas, several activities, several examples.

Name each section by the point it teaches, not the topic it covers. would have: the result of a condition that did not happen names a point; Past hypotheticals names a topic and is a table of contents entry. Write the heading in words the student already has, with no number in front of it — the number belongs to the ledger's row, never to the page.

The roadmap's list of concepts and vocabulary is what the lesson must teach, never a plan for how to divide it. A roadmap that names Concepts and Vocabulary on separate lines does not ask for a section of each. Teach a word where the lesson first needs it, inside the section that uses it; a section of words alone is justified only when the set is the subject — the prepositions of place as a system, the days of the week — and then it teaches the set as a system.

Explain every concept, then make the student practise it. A concept with no sentence stating its rule is a label, a table row, a chart cell or a drawing, never a taught concept; a concept the student never practises is unfinished. Explain each point past its rule: the reason the language works that way, the Portuguese contrast it carries, the neighbour it is mistaken for — the sentence the student would say instead, and the word in it that makes it wrong here — and one note the student could not have inferred. Where nothing beyond usage explains a form, that is the explanation: name the form, say it is fixed, and stop — an image borrowed from another page is not a reason.

Teach thoroughly, train what you taught, and test with the quiz. Write as much as the lesson needs — nothing caps its length. Show a point again where something about it changes: another form, another meaning, a setting where the rule fails, or a wrong form tempting. A point that never appears outside the sentence that introduced it was stated, not taught.

Make every sentence about the language a statement of fact: is it still true with every example deleted, would every example on the page agree with it, and does any ordinary English sentence break it? Read a note for what it adds — a test, a mistake it stops, the word's real use.

Use direct, literal language for every sentence that explains — no borrowed image, no playful word use, and no term that is not verifiably in use. Teaching a figure of speech, an idiom or a slang word is not using one: the lesson may take any of them as its subject. The standard vocabulary of grammar (the past, before the verb), a word used with the sense the register gives it and a quoted line of speech are not borrowed images. A gloss, a definition or a translation is verifiable — it comes from a dictionary or from the way the word is used, and it never adds a claim the word does not have. Cut every sentence that pads, hedges, restates the one before it, or explains what the example beside it already shows. A statement that a form is fixed, and has no reason beyond usage, is information, not hedging. An explanation that leaves the student unable to use the sentence unaided is lacking at any length; a short one that states the rule and its reason is not.

Where the lesson's subject is a figure of speech, an idiom or a false cognate, name it in plain words, say what it means and show it in an example — the explanation itself never borrows the figure — and hold a question, a stem and an option to the same test as the explanation.

Name the language the way a grammar names it, so the student can look the term up. The auxiliary that fills the place between who and what happens is the mode indicator — do, does, don't, doesn't — and what it does is carry the tense, and the person with it, which is why the negative and the question are the places it appears; the modals take that same place. On its own the base verb already carries the simple present, the -s is the third person singular, and when a negative or a question arrives that -s moves onto the mode indicator and the main verb returns to base. No page calls it the little verb or the little word: a coined name leaves the student holding a label with no word in it. The same rule governs every name — place, not slot; form, not shape; the past participle, never a third form; meaning where shape would come to mind; the quiz, not a check; and a line of a conversation is a line, a reply, a question or a statement, never a turn.

Choose the vocabulary the student must own at the end of the lesson by importance, relevance and accessibility rather than flavour, and teach, train and test it until they can use it. Teach the roadmap section's Examples the same way, because they were chosen to show the lesson's main points: add more examples around them if the teaching needs them, never instead of them. Explain, gloss or translate on the page every word the student has not met — in the explanation, an example or an item. Show a concept, a pattern or a sound rule through whatever words demonstrate it, and keep those words out of the lesson's vocabulary.

Name every element the lesson uses, with the details it needs to exist, in a comment — <!-- p1: … -->, which renders nothing and is deleted in pass 2. Name the sentence whose parts the student must see. A timeline is one way to show where a tense sits in time; it is a choice, never a requirement, and plan what the drawing shows in words: where the action sits on the line, how long it lasts, and where the tense sits. Choose a song's line and quote it here: it must contain the form or the words the lesson teaches and be pointable at on the page, and plan the section with it — the line, what it shows, what the student predicts, the production that follows. Pass 2 fetches the recording. Name the words a key sits above, the rows of a chart, and who speaks each line of a dialog. A page that teaches a sound puts the sound on the page, and at least one item asks the student to hear it or to say it.

Answer four questions before choosing any activity. Can these concepts be grouped or ordered so each explains the next? How much of the student's success depends on understanding the rule, how much on training the use until it is automatic, and how much on memorising an item? Is this the place to introduce, compare or note a synonym, antonym, homophone, homograph, false cognate or idiom the roadmap's item brings with it? And only then: what is the best way to organise, display and teach it?

Choose each activity by what the student must do, and write one defence for it, in the same comment, before using it — what the student must produce, and why this activity is the instrument for that work rather than another. A defence that cannot be written means the activity is wrong; a defence that would read the same for the activity beside it means one of the two was never chosen. It is an activity, so it is a gap is not a defence. Ask a question the student can get wrong for a reason the lesson has taught — the irregular form, the case that breaks the rule, the pair that is confused — because an activity nobody can fail teaches nothing, and an activity whose every item follows one rule only restates the section's own sentence as a question.

Make every activity answerable by the student — if they are expected to infer or deduce, say so on the page — and never ask an item for a judgement on an axis the page has not defined before it. Match an item's options to the lesson in concept, and to its length and complexity: a one-word answer is not offered beside three-word distractors, and a distractor is wrong for a reason the page has already covered, never because it is absurd, foreign to the scene or obviously the odd one out. Shuffle a set of options so the right one is not always in the same place, and make nothing a copy exercise: the student produces from memory or meaning, never by transcribing what the page has just printed.

Never describe your own text. A sentence that says what the page does — each form appears once, no line carries two of them, none of these items repeats a sentence from the page, the section below shows — is a claim about the page, and a reader who finds it false stops trusting the rest. Write the page and do not narrate it: an instruction tells the student what to do (complete each sentence with one phrase from the bank), and never describes the exercise.

Close the lesson with a quiz — the last section before the notebook — that asks for every concept the lesson taught and the vocabulary that is new, in sentences the page has never shown, and key every item: passing it must require having learned the lesson, not having read it. Size it by the points, not by the page: a point that took a whole section is asked more than once, and a quiz with one item per section samples the lesson.

Difficulty is not decided on this page: the level guidance in PEDAGOGY-MAP.md is the only statement of it and outranks everything here. The markup pass 1 writes is .pt — Portuguese is marked so qa/batchcheck can read it, and where and how much of it the page carries is that same level section's — and a <!-- p1: … --> comment; nothing else in this draft is markup. Everything else is words — headings, paragraphs, lists, an activity as its numbered prompts with their keys, and a chart as its rows inline in the text, one row per line, cells separated by | , a header line first or - for none.

Read what you have written, once, before the draft is finished — every claim the page makes about itself, checked against the page: delete it or make it true. Rewrite every sentence you would have to read twice as a sentence with a subject and a verb.

Checks

Read the draft back against the roadmap and the register, and send what you find back to pass 1 — a fault in flow or teaching is repaired in the words, not in the arrangement. qa/batchcheck reads the ledger against the pages and enforces what may not repeat.

  • Read the roadmap section line by line: name where each concept is explained and where the student practises it. A concept with no sentence stating its rule is a label, a table row, a chart cell or a drawing; one the student never practises is unfinished.
  • Read the register: remove a scene, person, word or topic that belongs to another track, however well it shows the grammar.
  • Read the quiz: it is the last section before the notebook, it tests every concept the lesson taught on material the student has not seen, and every item carries its key.
  • Read three examples and three drill items at random — the wording around them is not the evidence — and name the level they demand and the register they speak.
  • Read the section headings alone, in order, with the body hidden: each names the point its section teaches, and each may assume only what the headings before it established. Past hypotheticals names a topic; would have: the result of a condition that did not happen names a point. No heading carries a number.
  • Read the first block on its own. It states what the lesson teaches; if it is a conversation or a scene, the page opened on an example of something it never said.
  • Read the difficulty against the lesson's number, as PEDAGOGY-MAP.md's level section states it, in both halves: nothing depends on English the course has not taught, and at least one item's answer is not printed beside it and one must be produced rather than picked.
  • Cases that break a rule are always taught and included in training and testing.
  • Search the page for the names this page bans — third form, little verb, slot, shape standing for a form, turn for a line — and for a number standing where a grammatical name belongs.
  • Read the section subjects and their order against the ledger's last three rows, never against the previous page — that page is closed. If they are the same sequence, this page is that page's skeleton with new words. The list pass 2 corrected, the blocks and the elements are compared at verify, once they exist.
  • Read the wording as a judgement, not a measurement: any sentence that pads, hedges, restates the one before it, or explains what the example beside it already shows is cut.
  • Apply the language test to the wording, the stems and the options: rewrite a word used in a sense a dictionary does not give it, a sentence that describes an effect instead of naming the form, and a sentence that would be false once the examples are deleted, as the plain statement each replaces.
  • Try to break every rule the draft states: write the ordinary English sentence that contradicts it. If one exists, the rule is too wide — narrow it until nothing normal breaks it.
  • Read the wording, the stems and the options for figures of speech: a word used for something it does not literally name is rewritten as the plain statement it replaces. The standard vocabulary of grammar (the past, before the verb, the line leaves the point), a word used with the sense the register gives it, the idiom the lesson is teaching, and a quoted line of speech are not figures.
  • Read every .pt sentence and every translation stem as a Brazilian colleague would — a calque, a wrong tense or a missing subjunctive is a defect.
  • Read the examples of speech: is every form the course has contracted written contracted, have after a modal included, and is the reduced spelling used only as the thing not to write?
  • Read the page for the register's locale list: two occurrences warn, three fail, and a word the register marks as another variety of English is a mistake at any level.
  • Read the defences in the p1 comments, one per activity, and read them together: two that are the same sentence in different words mean one of the two was never chosen.
  • For a lesson about time, check that a timeline block is planned with an example and what the drawing shows, in words — where the action sits, how long it lasts, where the tense sits on the line.
  • For a page that teaches a sound, check that the sound is on the page and that at least one item asks the student to hear it or say it.
Pass 2 — the page

The demo lesson. Everything below is one lesson, built in the order a lesson is built, and it is the model for the pages: the words inside these blocks are a lesson's words, never the page's. Its voice is business. A block marked Kid or Adult is the same beat in that track's voice, so one act can be read in three rooms.

How the blocks sit on the page

A section is a .rows row and a block is one cell of it. One block in a row fills the width; two split it; three is the widest a lesson goes. A block never sits inside another block: it may hold prose, a chart, a figure or a set of items, and the next block is its sibling, in a row of its own.

Group by what the student does next, not by what the block holds. The rule and the drawing it is read with share a row, as two cells. The training that tests that rule starts a new row. A second voice of the same beat is its own row, under the first. A single cell is the default; two is for a rule beside its drawing; three is for the six looks and for nothing else.

Every markup box sits in its own .rows.markup-row, under the block it documents, never beside it — and it is closed until a builder opens it.

The options, and what each one is for

An option is chosen by what the content has to do, never to vary the paint. A block with no data-look is what a block looks like; a look is added when the thing inside it is a note, an activity, a quotation or the rule a section turns on. Never add an option to fill a line, to finish a row, or because the row above it carries the same phrase — an element that is not doing the work it is named for is taken out, never styled.

Each drawing below is the markup that serves the option, at its smallest, with what the option is for and what it is not for. The six looks follow as cards of their own, each painted exactly the way it paints a block.

Choice sets. One element, .opts, with one switch: unset it stacks, data-layout="row" puts the set on one line, and compact is the row that shares a line with other words.

<div class="opts"> — the default, and the answer to most sets: each option on its own line, read one after another. Not for two or three one-word options, which leave a column of white space.

data-layout="row" — for short options the student compares at a glance. Not for options of a phrase or more: on one line they either wrap without a plan or run past the cell.

class="opts compact" — for a set that belongs on the same line as the sentence or the cell it answers, inside a chart row or after a stem. Not for a set the student must weigh: the smaller target and tighter spacing cost the reading.

Charts. Three switches, each answering a different question about the table underneath.

<tr class="bare"> — when no column needs a heading, because the student can see what each holds. The row is collapsed to a 3px rule, never deleted, so the chart keeps its top edge. Not for a column whose contents are not obvious: the student then reads two columns with nothing to tell them apart.

<th class="vlab" rowspan="2"> — when the table holds two or more families and the word naming each must stand outside the frame. Not for one family: a label that names the only thing in the table repeats the sentence beside it.

<tr class="grp"> — when rows divide into groups and each group needs a phrase across them. It is a group heading inside the table, and it belongs where the groups divide. Not for a remark under the last row: a phrase added at the bottom of a table is a sentence about the table, and the sentence beside the chart is where that goes.

The knobs. Five variables, each set on the element it sizes and each drawn at its own card: --gap-w the answer gap's width (#fill), --cols the columns of a grid of options (#mc), --lines the height of a written field in text lines (#dictation), --comic-h every panel of one comic (#strip), --strip-h one panel of it.

tint

For the one block a section turns on — the definition, the rule everything after it is read against. Not for an example, and not twice in one row: two tinted blocks stop marking anything. data-look="tint".

outline

For the block the page returns to — a checklist, a summary, a reference read after the lesson. Not for teaching text, and not for a warning: the option carries no meaning of its own. data-look="outline".

stripes

For an activity — the block the student writes in. Not for text: stripes behind three paragraphs make them harder to read. data-look="stripes".

tape

For a note pinned onto the page — a tip, an aside, a reminder read on the way past. Not for the lesson's own teaching, and never two in one row. data-look="tape".

float

For text that applies to the whole section and must not look boxed. Not for an activity: a block the student writes in is a block. data-look="float".

dark

For a block that reads as another medium — a quoted message, a screen, a transcript. Not for emphasis, and never for the page's own text. data-look="dark".

Arrange it, and give it a look

Arrange a section's content however its teaching needs it. A row is one line, and its direct children are its cells; a cell holds as many items as its content needs — one, two or several — and its share of the line is settled by that content against the cells beside it when the page loads. A section can hold as many rows as its content needs, or none: a block, a chart or a paragraph can stand directly in it. A level-1 or level-2 line holds four columns, and a level-3 row holds two — a block is as wide as the widest row of cells it holds, so one whose rows are a single cell and then two takes two of the four at every point in its height. Four across the page is as much as can be read side by side; two is all a level-3 line shows, and a level-3 row is private to its cell and never counts toward the line above it. .row is only the utility for a line of chips or buttons — never a layout row.

Split the page only where the split helps the student read it — two tenses side by side, a contrast, an element set apart so it is seen as distinct — and never to separate things that need no separation. A section can hold several concepts, and a block can hold several activities, teaching elements and rows of content: splitting without need is noise.

Pass 2 may make defensible structuring improvements: split a section carrying two things, merge two that teach one, rename a heading that names a topic, add the section the training needs, drop one the content no longer fills. Each one is defensible by a reason the student's reading gains. Then correct the ledger row to the page as it was built.

Build what the notes name, and delete them when you have. A note says what its element carries — a chart's rows, who speaks each line, the words a key sits above, the sentence to mark, the song's line — and the element is built from that, never re-decided.

Assess each defence as you arrange the page. It says why this activity is the instrument for that work, so a defence that no longer holds means the activity or its place is wrong, and one that exposes work the page never asks the student to do is where you may add. Add instances, never a proposition — another example of a rule the page already states, the exception that rule carries, a contrast between two things it has already named — written in the drafted level, register and voice. Nothing of a note or a defence reaches the student's text.

Every element in this section is drawn with the markup that builds it, and the drawing is the default: what the element contains, what text it carries, where that text sits, and whether it carries a title at all. Its item count is the example's own, never a count to copy.

Declare data-look on the block, never infer it from what it holds: it changes the paint only.

Put every item under a section or a block in a row, and let a row be a line whose direct children are its cells. Put a paragraph, a list, a chart, a drawing or a block that arrives directly under a section's heading in a .rows with a cell of its own before anything else is done, and write a block's own children the same way one level deeper — so any item can be picked up and placed beside another, and nothing on the page is a bare leaf. Let a cell hold several items, stacked, when they belong together: the point of the cell is that something can be dragged beside it, not that it holds one thing.

Open a block with its h3 — its letter, its anchor and its place in the index are drawn from that headline. Put text that introduces or applies to a whole section or block at the top of its container, and it may be a row of its own. Centre a lone element at the end of a section, or give it the full width, whichever reads better. Author no height anywhere: the vertical spacing comes from style.css at each level, and from equalize.js inside a row.

Group rows by what the student reads together, and never place a cell because of its size. A cell holds what belongs together, and choose the cells beside it because they are read against each other — the rule and the exercise that tests it, two contrasts, the activity and the note that reads it — never because the two are the same length or because the line would otherwise be half empty. Author no flex and no width on a cell: the page sizes the line when it loads, and an authored width is stale on the next screen. Give the width to whatever needs it — a cell holding a chart, a table or long answer lines takes its share first — and give a block whose content is wide a neighbour only when that neighbour is read against it; otherwise it takes the line.

Use a visual or an activity only where it earns its place. Do not use one that only repeats the sentence beside it, or whose removal would cost the student nothing; a block holding text and nothing else is the normal case, and let a visual with nothing to illustrate stand outside a block rather than giving it one. Read how full a cell is and then leave it alone unless a change can be named for the student — move it beside the thing it belongs with, give a drawing the width it needs, or bring the item it explains into the same cell. If no such reason can be written, leave it: white space under a short cell is a normal lesson page, and content invented to fill a cell is worse than the gap. Read no height here. What is read here is the arrangement of cells; a height is measured in the Align step, on the rendered page.

qa/pos.mjs emits the .pos-* marks for a sentence the page already wrote; paste them, never write a mark by hand. qa/audio.mjs finds, fetches, cuts and times a recording and prints the figure with its data-t values, its data-duration and the provenance row for assets/README.md; on a lesson page the source is ../assets/<name>.mp3. A highlight that fires early or late is moved with --cue-shift, never by editing a data-t. PronKey.render builds the pronunciation key above a word, and it is the only way a sound is shown on a page — never a respelling, a capital stress or an invented notation.

Standalone visuals

A chart, a list, a dialog, a drawing, a recording or a strip sits inside a block with the text that reads it, or stands outside at its own width.

Marked activities with controls

An activity is a unit the student completes, and its instruction is written inside the block that holds it. Write the instruction to describe what the control actually does, in the direction the student works it — whether the prompt carries the answer and the student finds the word, or carries the word and the student supplies the answer — and write it after reading the engine that runs the control, never from the activity's name. The four controls — a gap, a radio, a checkbox, a written field — are what an activity is built from, chosen by what the student must do; none of them is an activity on its own, and a lone keyed gap is a component beside other teaching, never an activity. Choose the control by the length of the answer: a gap holds anything that fits on the line, and a written field is for an answer that cannot — a paragraph, a list, several sentences — because a gap that has to scroll has stopped showing the answer. An item and the control that answers it are one element — <div class="qitem"> holding the prompt and its answer line — never two paragraphs: a question in one element and its answer in another is a pair the editor can drag apart, and a question further from its answer than from the next question cannot be read. Every item the student answers is numbered — <span class="qnum">1.</span> beside the control it numbers, the count running through the activity block so the next activity starts again at 1 — so a teacher can name the item they mean.

data-answer keys a gap, | separating alternatives; --gap-w is 1.3× the width of the longest accepted answer, the field's own padding included, written in em — a gap with no key takes 1.3× the prompt written beside it, or 9em. Never 100% and never calc(): a field as wide as its block has stopped showing its answer, and on its own line it stops being a gap. A radio group's key is data-a on .qitem; engine2.js marks the right option and writes .ans beside a typed field. The line holding the gap is a plain <p>: .ans is the engine's own pill, written after a checked field, and never a class a page writes.

Align

Once every lesson in the batch is finished and its checks are green, npm run align -- --track=<track> --lessons=<range> measures the pages on the rendered page, and the batch is aligned before it is committed and pushed. The pass reads the working tree through a local static server, the bytes about to be published, never a deployed copy.

Heights come from the authored page and widths from the solved one. equalize.js makes a lopsided row look level, so a height read from the finished page measures the solver: the pass calls EqualizeRows.clean() for the authored heights and reads the solved widths, where a cell is actually squeezed. A row is uneven when its two cells differ by more than 30px of content — the tolerance a reader does not notice — and a row of one cell is not measured. A cell short of width is repaired structurally: the row is broken, or the wide cell is given the line. A cell short of content takes content.

The repair goes in the shorter cell, and it is content — but only after the pairing is checked. First read the row against the rule that made it: if the two cells were paired for size, balance or a filled line, the pairing was never a claim about content and the row is broken. Where the pairing is real, an activity takes another item of the kind it runs — the case it has not asked for, the exception the section named — and a teaching cell takes the example, the contrast or the note the section stopped short of; it may also take an open-licence picture, cropped to what the cell asks for, kept in assets/ with its credit line. Rewrapping comes last: a line is not cut to reach a height, and a headline that comes out cut short is reworded shorter and never left cut.

Inside an activity is read as well: a question standing far taller than its neighbours in a .qgrid is the same imbalance, and the repair is a question moved out of the grid onto its own line, never a question shortened to fit a share of the page. The pass repeats until it prints no row above 30px; then npm run page, batchcheck and build:lessons run again, and the batch is committed and pushed with the alignment stated in the commit message.

Learned during build generation

What building a lesson has taught the build

Every line here is a mechanism of the site, learned by building a lesson on it. A line states what the machine does or what the build must do — never a fault, a session or a date. Add a line here when a lesson teaches you one, before that lesson's row is closed.

  • A row is sized at load, not authored. equalize.js writes the widths: a table keeps its widest row, a drawing never passes its own size, and text takes the remainder above 200px — but beside a table or a drawing the text stops at its widest line, and that cell takes the slack. An authored flex or width on a cell is stale and the solver overwrites it. A question cell or a list cell also keeps a floor at its widest authored item, and it is spaced at load: the gaps between its numbered items (or between its list's items) are opened or closed until the cell meets the row's tallest cell that cannot space, each gap held between 6px and its authored gap + 48px. The solver strips that spacing from the saved clone only; the live page keeps it.
  • Every vertical gap is its container's, at one value per level. @layer spacing gives the content area's grid a 20px row gap, a frame's direct children 20px, and a cell's direct children 14px; a cell's first child and its answer fields are 0, so every cell starts flush and a short cell's blank sits below its content — except in a two-cell row, where a gap of more than 12px that width cannot close centers the shorter cell's content instead — and a question cell's or a list cell's own gaps are spread or compressed before that decision, so centering decides only what spacing cannot close. No element carries a vertical margin or padding of its own at those levels; h1 and h2 keep theirs.
  • A row's authored balance can only be read before the solver runs. equalize.js equalizes a row by redistributing widths, then by spreading a question cell's own gaps, then by centring a two-cell row's shorter cell — so a height measured on the finished page reports the solver's work, not the author's. EqualizeRows.clean() restores the authored page (widths, align-self, justify-content, the spacing lever's margins); with every cell held at align-self:flex-start each rect is that cell's own content, and two cells differing by more than 30px there are a row whose shorter cell is missing content. npm run align reads it at 1280 and 1024px.
  • A squeeze is read in the solved state, a height in the authored one. The solver hands width to whatever needs it — a table keeps its widest row, a drawing never passes its own size — so a chip run or an answer line in the cell beside them can be left narrower than its content and wrap. npm run align reads the page twice: the solved widths first (~~, wants 479px in 189px (chips on 6 rows)), then clean() for the authored heights. A squeeze is repaired by the structure — the row broken, the wide cell given the line — and never by an item, which answers a shortage of content and not of width.
  • A page that loads the solver is hidden until its rows are sized. data-eq on <html> makes the sheet hold .content-area at visibility:hidden; equalize.js solves once, then writes eq-ready when the fonts settle or after 1.5s. The page loads under a 50% veil with its paper, frame, header and content transparent; at eq-ready the veil fades evenly for 1s while the paper, frame and header fade in, and at 0.8s the content begins its own 1s fade (1.8s). A 4s/5s CSS fallback shows a page whose script never ran; the editor's body.layout-on and body.editing stay visible. The header wrapper holds the brand, the control bar and its legend, so the fade and every header rule reach all three.
  • A block reports its own progress. data-state="done" on a .block is the sheet's green frame (#80 STATE), and on a page whose <html> carries data-done engine2.js writes it: every question in the block has an answer on it — a gap with at least one character, a written field with text, one option picked in each radio group, at least one box ticked in each group of checkboxes — and a question emptied takes the state away again. A teaching block holds no control and never reports. The saved clone strips the state, so a file never keeps the report of the student who saved it.
  • The control's name is its key, and the key is a number — a<section>c<block letter><item> (a7c2 is section 7's third block, second item), one number for a whole radio group. A control left unnamed falls back to the words around it plus a {block, offset} position.
  • That offset counts blocks on the page, and a game inserts its stage into its own block when it loads — so a position saved before a game ran and read after it can be off by that stage. A number does not move; a position can.
  • Two controls in one cell take two numbers. Both share the cell's words and its first offset, and Complete draws one key pill per control: two fields in one cell need two numbers, or one answer is drawn over the other.
  • The index files are build artifacts that ship with the page. npm run build:lessons writes lessons.json and tracks.json from the page filenames, and --check refuses a stale pair; a lesson pushed without that run is live but unlisted.
  • Every page carries the stylesheet's stamp, and a page that loads the solver carries its stamp too. build-lessons.mjs writes the stylesheet's stamp into every page that links it — the lesson pages and the root pages, and any page the manifest does not list — and equalize.js?v=… into every page that loads it — the hash of the file itself — so a changed stylesheet or solver is fetched on the next load and a stale stamp fails build:check. The shell's script slot lists the other engines; a changed engine still needs a hard refresh.
  • This page is built to be extended. Every direct child of .content-area is a row cell and its data-span sums to 12 (12 when unset); a pre in a markup box holds escaped tags only, because a real one inside it is parsed as markup; npm run map checks both, and the dead links.
  • A chart row is answered by a keyed gap. The typed form of chartanswer carries data-answer in the row's answer cell; a row of options is markable only when its cell holds a .qitem with data-a, so a chart of bare radios has no key the engine can read.
  • A game records the control under it, not its own name. Items answered with options are mc in the ledger; items answered by typing a word are fill, because the field is what the student writes in.
  • The street map redraws inside every .qitem. Two questions about one street map are two drawings, and each checkbox keeps its place because its position is a percentage of the drawing, not of the cell.
  • A dictation's answer can be a gap. .listen speaks its data-say text and the field that follows it takes data-answer; a one-word answer needs no textarea.write, and the block is still a dictation.
  • A timeline family is a full-width child of its block. .tlgrid holds one <div> per figure with its <h4> name; inside a half-width row cell the pair is too narrow to read, so it sits under the text rather than beside it.

The rule is written after the .qgrid rules it refines, so it wins on source order at equal specificity.

The deck prints the text of the flipped face in .flip-ask, so the student reads the back's words and turns the card whose front matches them; the instruction states that direction, and the pairs are chosen for the decision they force rather than for the rule they repeat.

Where a thing is, and what shape you give it

A place is a point, a space, or a line

Portuguese and English point at a place in two different ways, and almost every mistake in this lesson comes from the difference. Portuguese names the place and lets the rest of the sentence say whether you are there or going there: Estou no banco, Vou no banco. English puts the whole difference in the preposition, so at the bank and to the bank are two different sentences — and I go in the bank, which is what no becomes when it is carried across, describes somebody walking into a wall.

So English asks one question about every place: what shape am I giving it?

  • at — a point on the map: at the station, at the corner of Mill Lane, at the front desk, at work
  • in — a space you are inside of: in the bank, in the café, in a taxi, in May
  • on — a line or a surface: on High Street, on the fourth floor, on the bus, on Friday
  • to — no shape at all, only an ending: go to the bank, get to the office, walk to the corner, send it to Ana

The three shapes are one building seen three ways. The bank on High Street is a point when you wait for a client outside it, a box when you step inside to the counter, and part of a street when you tell somebody where it is: at the bank, in the bank, the bank on High Street. Nothing about the word bank changed. You changed the shape.

And to never competes with the other three, because it is not saying where anything is — it is saying where the movement stops. One sentence can hold both: I walked to the bank and waited at the counter.

The words this lesson needs: bank (o banco) · station (a estação) · corner (a esquina) · street (a rua) · block (o quarteirão) · directions (as instruções) · front desk (a recepção).

Now the Portuguese. Estou no banco is true at the door, at the cash machine outside and at the counter inside, because no only says which place. English makes you decide what your listener does with the information: I'm at the bank tells them to look for you outside or at the entrance, I'm in the bank tells them to come in and find you. That is the difference between being found and being waited for.

When you move, the shape drops out and to takes over: Vou no banco → I'm going to the bank. Say I'm going in the bank and you have not made a grammar mistake — you have described a different minute, and your friend will expect you back outside shortly. That is why the preposition is worth a whole lesson: your listener builds the picture, and in, at and on are where you draw it.

Habit, or the trip you are on

One verb in Portuguese, two forms in English

Eu vou ao banco covers the habit, the trip that is happening now, and the trip you arranged for tomorrow — one Portuguese verb, three English sentences. English does not leave the choice to the listener: the form of the verb is the choice. I go to the bank is a habit; I'm going to the bank is this trip. The two are not interchangeable, and the wrong one is not a small mistake: it changes what you claim.

I go to the bank every Friday is true of every Friday there will ever be. Nothing about it started, and nothing ends it. Said with no time phrase at all — I go to the bank — it is still that statement: the way things are. So I go to the bank now is the sentence Portuguese hands you, and in English it means something else: now has an edge and the present simple has none.

I'm going to the bank now has the edges: the trip started, it ends when you are there. That is also why the continuous states a plan — I'm going to the bank tomorrow — an arrangement already made, which is closed in the same way.

And repetition decides nothing on its own: Estou indo ao banco todos os dias essa semana → I'm going to the bank every day this week, because this week puts an end on the series. Take the end away and the present simple is the only form left: I go to the bank every Friday, no last Friday, nothing to finish. So the question is not one trip or many. It is has the sentence got an edge? If a listener can ask "and next Friday?" and you answer "yes, as always", use I go. If the answer is "no, just this week", use I'm going.

Both mistakes are one mistake: I'm going to the bank every Friday gives a habit an edge it does not have, and I go to the bank now takes the edge off a trip that has one. Both come from using the present for whatever is true at the moment of speaking, which is what Portuguese lets you do and English does not.

To, and the person at the end of it

Where a movement ends, and who receives

To marks a destination, and this lesson needs two kinds of destination from it: a place where a movement stops, and a person a thing stops at.

The first is a place: go to the bank, get to the office, walk to the corner. Get to is the pair to keep — it is the arrival, so I get to the office at nine says when you are there, not when you leave.

The second is a person: I sent the directions to Ana. Ana is where the directions end, as the bank is where your walk ends. She is the indirect object — the receiver — and the directions, the thing that travels, is the direct object.

I sent the directions to Ana.

English also puts the receiver straight after the verb and drops to: I sent Ana the directions. One event, one receiver, two orders — and the arrow lands on Ana either way. What the second order changes is where the thing goes: to the end of the sentence.

I sent Ana the directions.

Two conditions decide which order is available. The verb has to give or send — give, send, show, tell, lend, offer, pass, bring, hand, teach — and the thing has to be a full noun phrase, not it or them. Send the file to me becomes send me the file; with the thing as a pronoun the short order is gone: send it to me, never send me it.

The verbs that refuse it are the ones where nothing changes hands: explain, describe, say, report, suggest, announce. Me explica a regra has exactly the shape of the short order, and explain me the rule is what comes out of it. The sentence is explain the rule to me, and there is no shorter one.

Portuguese keeps one word in front of the receiver, para, and keeps it there: Mandei as instruções para a Ana. Word for word that is I sent the directions for Ana — the directions are for Ana to use, and nothing says she has them. The receiver takes to or no preposition at all; and where Portuguese says me manda o mapa, English agrees with it: send me the map.

The word directions(dər·ec·xns) means the instructions for a route — the word the clerk uses when you ask at the desk.

The five phrases no rule decides

Two the rule reaches, three it cannot

Five phrases are worth more than any rule you could invent for them, and they divide into two honest groups.

The two the rule does reach. A street is a line, so you stand on it: the bank on High Street, waiting on the corner. And a month is a container, so a date sits in it: in May, in October. Brazilian Portuguese gives you na rua and em maio, and only the English shape of the place decides which word you use.

What separates on the street from in the street is not grammar but what you are looking at. On the street is the street as a line you walk, live or work along: there's a café on the street. In the street is the roadway itself, the space between the curbs: the kids were playing in the street, I met him in the street — out in the open, not inside anywhere. Both are correct and they are not the same picture.

The three that are fixed. At night and at noon against in the morning and in the afternoon: no rule predicts which three of the six take at, and a student who learns the six has everything the rule would have given him. At work is your job, not the building: estou no trabalho covers both, English does not. On the bus tells you the vehicle you can stand up in: a bus, a train, a plane take on; a car and a taxi take in.

A teacher who invents a rule for these — at night, because night is a point — hands you something that fails on de manhã, which is in the morning. The honest sentence is shorter: this one is fixed, here is the sentence it lives in, learn it and move.

The same town after dark

After dark the grammar does not change, but the sentences do: the questions stop being about which street and start being about what is open, what is lit and what is safe. Learn those three words and a strange town at night becomes walkable.

open — Is the bank open at night? No, but the cash machine on the corner is. · lit — from lit up, the past participle of light: Walk on the lit side, past the hotel, and the entrance is at the end of the block. The opposite is dark, and the sentence that keeps you out of trouble is keep to the lit side. · safe — Is this street safe after nine?

Then the same four letters turn up in the street's other language, and the connection is worth having. Lit began as shorthand for literature — printed, written, worth reading — and from there it came to mean excited and then simply very good: That bar is lit on Fridays. One lit comes from a lamp and one from a shelf of books, and a student who knows both hears the joke twice: once when the street is lit by a lamp, and once when a friend says the party was.

Portuguese splits the day and leaves you to choose: à noite is at night, but de noite is either at night or in the evening, depending on the hour you mean. Choose by the clock, not by the Portuguese: in the evening until about nine, at night after that, and at midnight, never in it.

Coming back: go, get, come

The same movement, plus back

Back adds the return to a movement you already know, and the three verbs divide the return between them. Every sentence below is built from phrases this lesson has already given you.

Go back is the trip: Go back the way you came, up Mill Lane. The sentence is about the movement away from here and towards the place you came from, and the route back needs no street names.

Get back is the arrival: I get back to the office at six. Get brings the end of the movement with it, so the sentence answers "what time are you there again?" The visitor asks the same verb at the desk — How do I get back to the station after the meeting? — and the place you left takes from: I get back from the meeting at six.

Come back brings you towards the person you are talking to: Come back to the office when you're done — I'm here until six. If the speaker is at the place, the return is come; if the speaker is not, it is go.

The past tense changes nothing: We went back to the hotel after dinner is the same trip, and She gets to work at eight thirty has no back at all, because she never left — a destination, not a return.

At the desk: What time do you ?

get back to return to a place, said of the arrival: What time do you get back? It is get + back, so the place takes to: get back to the office. Portuguese voltar does not decide this one: go back is the trip, get back is the end of it, come back is a return to the person you are talking to.

Voltar covers all three, and the choice English forces is the one Portuguese leaves out: where the trip ends, and where the speaker is standing. The test: ask "is the sentence about the trip, about arriving, or about coming to me?"

A route names three things

A route a stranger can follow names the street you start on, the street you turn into, and the side the place is on. The dialog below has all three: on Low Street, right on Mill Lane, left on High Street, on your left at the corner. Take one part away and the stranger walks the wrong way.

This is the town the desk is in. High Street runs east-west along the top and Low Street runs the same way along the bottom. Mill Lane and Park Lane cross both of them, Mill Lane on the west side and Park Lane on the east. Above High Street, west to east: the station, the café, the park. Between the two streets, west to east: the bank, the library, the post office. Below Low Street, west to east: the bus stop, the market, the hotel.

The street needs no new word in Portuguese: vira à direita na Mill Lane uses na for the street you turn into, as na Low Street does for the one you walk, and English puts on on both because both are lines. What does not cross is the side: on your left and à sua esquerda both make you say whose left it is, because a route is given to a person standing somewhere. And keep going straight is continua reto — one verb in Portuguese, two words in English, and the second one, straight, is the shape of the line.

What the question asks for, and what the answer names

At a desk you ask one of three questions, and each one asks for a different kind of answer.

How do I get to the station? The answer is a route: the street you start on, the street you turn into, and the side the place is on.

Where's the post office? The answer is a position: on Low Street, at the corner of Mill Lane, in the bank building.

Is there a café near here? The answer starts with whether the thing exists — there's one, or there isn't one near here — and then gives the position.

Portuguese has all three questions too, and the words do not line up: Onde fica…? asks for a position, Como chego…? asks for a route, and Tem… aqui perto? asks whether the place exists. A student who carries Onde fica into English asks Where is the station? and then gets a position where he wanted the route.

Both ends, and the way

From and to: a movement with two ends

From … to … names both ends of a movement: to is the end you are going towards, from is the end you left. The way is the route itself — on the way is during the movement, the way back is the route home.

This pair crosses from Portuguese without trouble: de segunda a sexta is from Monday to Friday, and do banco até a estação — até, as far as — is from the bank to the station. What does not cross is até itself: where the sentence names a time instead of a place, até is until — até sexta is until Friday, and to Friday is not a sentence.
