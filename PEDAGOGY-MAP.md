## [SOURCE] A page's authority is the language itself — the grammar of English, the grammar of Portuguese, and the usage a dictionary records

- **A classroom device is not a source.** A mnemonic, a "just remember it as…" image, a coursebook's framing or order, a famous lesson's example, or "this is how it is usually taught" belongs to the page it came from: it is not evidence, it is not quoted, and it is never the sentence that explains why.
- **Knowledge of teaching is not banned, only demoted from evidence.** It decides what to teach, in what order, what an item asks, how much practice a point gets, and which wrong form tempts the student. It does not decide what is true.
- **A reason is any true answer to "why is it like this?"** — a rule of English, the Portuguese contrast, a fact about how the form is used, the logic of a system the point belongs to, a test the student can apply, or the mistake the form prevents. **Usage is one of those answers, not the failure of one:** where nothing beyond usage explains the form, the page states that plainly and names the form.
- **An example under this block states something that is always true**, or it is not written: a claim that a form is wrong, or that a phrase does not exist, is written only where no ordinary English sentence contradicts it.

## [PROCESS] — the process is detailed in [I/O] 3, in this order
The lessons are built by working through `extra/lesson1-The Capability Map.html` — which states how, and every rule of the work is there — in sequential, independent steps:
1. **[Pass 1]** writes the words,
2. **[Checks]** reads them back,
3. **[Pass 2]** builds the page,
4. **[Verify]** reads what was built and the lesson page is completed.
5. At the end of the batch of lessons or task, each lesson that was authored is **[Aligned]**, committed, and pushed.

This file states the process and high-level items that change with each lesson. **It outranks CAPABILITY MAP wherever the two could be read as disagreeing**.

## [LEVEL] — The lesson number is a curriculum position, and it fixes the page's difficulty. **This section outranks every other consideration of difficulty.** The roadmap says what the lesson contains; the register says whose voice and which world; this section says how hard each item is — and no phrase in the roadmap and no flavour in the register lowers it or raises it.
- **"STUDENT LEVEL" is relative to the lesson's number** — it considers the English concepts and vocabulary the course has taught up to that point, and the first language of the target student. It determines how and when Portuguese (marked `.pt` for identification and italicization) should be used (when necessary for the student as elaborated below or when it is defensibly more clear or efficient to do so), what the English sentences that state a rule, a reason or a contrast may assume, and the 'difficulty' of activity questions and their answers/options:
	- **Difficulty is what the student must do — never how rare the words are.** A page is hard when the student must hold a rule across a whole sentence, choose between forms that compete, move several things at once, produce rather than select, etc.
	- **What maps directly** onto Portuguese is easy for this student; what maps onto it and misleads is hard; what has no counterpart has nothing to support it. Frequency says nothing here: "descend" is easier than "go down" although it is the rarer word, and the present perfect is harder than the past perfect although it costs a native speaker less — the mapping misleads in one and holds in the other. 'Easier' here means it can be used at a lower level, 'harder' means more difficult. A vocabulary word, verb tense, or structure which equates cleanly to Portuguese is presented as such.
	- **Ability grows with the lesson number** — The page may assume every concept, form and word the course taught through the lesson before it, and nothing else. The expectations for the student and thus their expected 'level' sits on a gradient across all the lessons in the course:
		- **Lesson 1 assumes the student knows no English** — In the first few lessons, explanations must be in Portuguese, simple pictures can be useful to aid student absorption — the student is introduced to the sounds and structure of English. They learn that letters like the 't' in 'what' are fluid and minimized. They learn the question words, 'to be', and get a deep understanding of each item as it is introduced (e.g. 'get' is not presented as 'pegar' or glossed over — it has no single Portuguese equivalent, it marks a change of state, and the page says exactly that. 'take' is also not 'pegar' — it relates to something being separated from its owner or from its original position). Contractions are used early and maintained throughout the course. "What is" becomes "What's", and "name is" becomes "name's" in explanatory text and activities alike after the student owns those contractions.
		- Around **Lesson 10**, the student may be able to understand some phrases, and some explanatory text has shifted to English, but the wording must remain extremely simple so that the student can understand. Portuguese is still used frequently, but starts to appear as a translation of a more complex English explanation — the student may learn the concept from the Portuguese, but they start to see how the two languages equate by comparing the text.
		- By **Lesson 20** they have become comfortable with many common phrases and can follow a simple explanation in English without issue, and an explanation that adds a second step — a test, the Portuguese contrast, the mistake the form prevents — is worth its length in Portuguese or English, and **direct translations of new vocabulary words (which have one definitive and equivalent translation) remain an efficient presentation method throughout the course.**
		- By lesson 35, **most of the sentences that explain are in English — now at an intermediate level**, utilizing language that the student has actually been taught as much as possible. Portuguese explanations are rarely used, but Portuguese phrases can be used for comparison — and for translation activities.
		- By **lesson 50 the student should have achieved functional fluency**, and even the most complex explanations are written in English. Every page in the course must read as the step it is — a lesson 12 that could be printed at 30, or a 30 that could be printed at 12, was not written for its number.
- **Depth is what an explanation contains, never how it reads.** A deeper explanation adds a second step — a test, a contrast, the mistake the form prevents, the reason a form exists. A picture, a story or a comparison may sit beside the explanation; none of them stands in place of the reason, and where the reason is usage the explanation says so.
- **An inference about what the level forbids is not a rule.** Only this section sets difficulty, and it never limits how many words an item may hold or how long a phrase may be: a page whose items are all one word because the lesson number is low has invented a constraint nobody wrote.
- **Contractions are used as soon as the course has taught them, and the form a lesson teaches is no exception** — a lesson on `would have` writes `would've` in its examples of speech and gives the full form where it explains the form. Writing `would have` throughout, or showing the reduced spelling `woulda` with no contracted form anywhere, is not the register this course teaches.
- **Portuguese is written as a Brazilian colleague would say it**, never translated word for word from the English: every gloss, contrast and translation stem is idiomatic, and a calque, a wrong tense or a missing subjunctive is a defect at any level.
