## [PROCESS] — the process is the map's, in this order
The lessons are built by working through `extra/lesson1-The Capability Map.html` in its own order: **Pass 1** writes the words, **Checks** reads them back, **Pass 2** builds the page, **Verify** reads what was built, and the batch is **Aligned** once every lesson in it is finished. That page states how, and every rule of the work is there.

This file states only what changes from lesson to lesson — the inputs above, `[LEVEL]` below, `[COVERAGE]`, and what the ledger has already spent — and **it outranks the map wherever the two could be read as disagreeing**.


## [LEVEL] — the lesson number is a curriculum position, and it fixes the page's difficulty
- **This section outranks every other consideration of difficulty.** The roadmap says what the lesson contains; the register says whose voice and which world; this section says how hard each item is — and no phrase in the roadmap and no flavour in the register lowers it or raises it.
- **Everything the page asks the student to read, answer or produce — the prose, the prompts, the activities, the quiz — is authored at a level that is accessible but mildly challenging for the student who has arrived at that lesson.** Accessible: nothing depends on English the course has not taught, nothing is explained by a word the page has not defined, and the instruction is written in words that student can follow at this number. Mildly challenging: the page never prints the answer beside the question, and every item asks for one thing more than the student could do without thinking about it. Accessible alone teaches nothing; challenging alone cannot be read.
- **"STUDENT LEVEL" is assumed, and it is relative to the lesson's number and to the English concepts and vocabulary the course has taught up to that point.** **Ability grows with the lesson number**: lesson 1 assumes the student knows no English, and by lesson 50 the student should have achieved functional fluency. The page must read as the step it is — a lesson 12 that could be printed at 40, or a 40 that could be printed at 12, was not written for its number.
- **The number is both floor and ceiling.** The page may assume every concept, form and word the course taught through the lesson before it, and nothing else; and it must demand the work of a student who has had that many lessons.
- **Difficulty is what the student must do — never how rare the words are.** A page is hard when the student must hold a rule across a whole sentence, choose between forms that compete, move several things at once, produce rather than select, and notice where the rule does not apply. It is easy when the student fills one word per line with the answer printed beside it, however advanced the topic's name.
- **Difficulty rises with the number on the door, and rising is measurable.** From lesson 20 a teaching example carries a subordinate clause and a named circumstance, never a two-word frame; from lesson 26 it runs to at least twelve words and asks the student to hold one thing from its first half to its second; from lesson 34 a sentence may nest a condition inside another clause or change tense mid-sentence. The count of work rises with it: a quiz at 26 or later holds at least eight items across at least three shapes, at least one of them producing a whole sentence rather than choosing a word, and at least one that fails a student who learned only the main rule — the exception, the contrast, or the case the rule does not cover. An item at 30 whose answer is one known word, or a drill whose sentences could be printed at 10 with the nouns changed, is the page walking back to a level it has passed.
- **Portuguese is used to explain concepts and give direct translations only when the student's [LEVEL] is insufficient that they should be expected to understand the necessary explanation in English** — and the lesson number is the only measure of that level: the same explanation is Portuguese at 1 and English at 30. Portuguese — when used — is marked `.pt` for identification and italicization.
- **An inference about what the level forbids is not a rule.** Only this section sets difficulty, and it never limits how many words an item may hold or how long a phrase may be: a page whose items are all one word because the lesson number is low has invented a constraint nobody wrote.
- **A page can be advanced in content and beginner in difficulty, and that is a defect.** Reported speech at 44 is not a table of shifts plus one-word gaps: it is the system (what moves, why, in what order), the switch that turns the shift off, the exceptions, and the student producing paragraphs of relayed speech.
- **The explanation is not the material.** The prose that explains a concept or a vocabulary item may be plain: it should sit at the lesson's level, and it does not have to be advanced. What must be at the level is the English the student handles — every example sentence, quoted line, dialog turn, gap answer, drill item and quiz item. A page that explains reported speech elegantly around "I'm tired." is a beginner page wearing advanced prose, and it fails this section even when every paragraph reads beautifully.
- **The examples are also the register.** Those same sentences are written in the register's voice: in the REGISTER's scenes and with its own words from its lists, never in a neutral course voice that merely describes them. A block whose examples could be printed in any track is a register failure even when its topic fits the track.
- **What maps directly** onto Portuguese is cheap; what maps onto it and misleads is expensive; what has no counterpart is built from nothing. Frequency says nothing here: "descend" is cheaper than "go down" although it is the rarer word, and the present perfect costs this student more than the past perfect although it costs a native speaker less — the mapping breaks in one and holds in the other.
- **Sentence complexity is what the sentence asks the reader to hold at once** — a clause inside a clause, a tense that changes mid-sentence, a reference the reader must remember to the end, a form whose Portuguese counterpart does not match.
- **Importance can outrank level.** A concept the track cannot do without belongs in the lesson that needs it even when it is expensive, and then the support around it grows — more teaching before the item, more training after it. The item is not lowered and the level is not moved; the support adds teaching and training, and it never restores an earlier lesson's scaffolding.
- Optimize concept/element/content grouping/order according to the needs of the student. [Introduce, compare, or make note of] [synonyms, antonyms, homophones, homographs, false cognates, relevant idioms/expressions, etc.] that are relevant to the lesson content.
- Every (new word/form/concept) the student must understand is presented before it is used; if a block wants the student to *infer*, the page says so. 
- Teach the rule, the reasoning (the mental model), and its direct consequences.
- **A teaching point is explained past its rule.** Beside the rule, the reason and the Portuguese contrast, each point carries **the neighbour it is mistaken for** — the sentence the student would say instead, and the word in it that makes it wrong here — and **one note the student could not have inferred**: how often and where native speakers use the form, what the same idea sounds like in a mail as against a meeting, the words that travel with this one (*meet a deadline*, *miss a deadline*, *the deadline slips*), the false friend, or the small word whose presence changes the meaning of the whole line. The note is a fact about the language, never a remark about the lesson; a paragraph that restates the rule in other words is what the map's prose test cuts, and a teaching section with no note has taught only what its heading promised.
- **Each point is shown at least three times, and the three do different work**: one example in its plainest setting, one where something around it changes what it means — a negative, a question, a second clause, another subject — and one where the rule does not apply or where the wrong form tempts. The examples live in the register's room with the register's people, and they do not share a frame: three lines with one subject and one verb are one example written three times. Where the lesson's word brings related phrases with it (*a supplier pays an invoice*, *a client settles an invoice*, *the invoice falls due*), the page names the two or three it will need and says how they differ.
- Multiple-choice options are shuffled so the right one is not always in the same slot. No copy-exercises: the student produces from memory or meaning,
- **A matching and an unscramble are dealt in a full shuffle.** In a matching, no row's letter is the meaning at that row's place in the pool; in an unscramble, no chip (word or phrase) sits at its own place in the sentence. Moving one item is not a shuffle — the whole order is re-dealt, so the answer is the only order the items are not in. The number on a chip is the display order, never the answer's order.
- **An item never asks for a judgement on an axis the page has not defined before the item**, and no invented terminology is used to name or classify a form: a term the student has not met is taught before it is used, and a term nobody outside a textbook uses is not used at all.
- **The options of an item match the lesson in concept and the level in length and linguistic complexity**: a one-word answer is not offered beside three-word distractors, and a distractor is wrong for a reason the page has already covered — never because it is absurd, foreign to the scene or obviously the odd one out.
## [COVERAGE] — the roadmap section is a floor, not a summary
- **Every concept, construction and example the roadmap section names is accounted for in the page**, and every vocabulary item it lists is used where the register licenses it. The lesson is not finished because the section has been mentioned: it is finished when each concept has been taught, contrasted, trained and tested.
- **The section's `Examples` are required teaching, not illustrations.** Each phrase the roadmap lists is put in front of the student as taught material: taught before it is trained, and reused wherever the lesson needs an example of the form it shows. They were chosen because they show the lesson's main points, so a page that swaps them for sentences of its own has taught something else — more examples may be added around them, never instead of them.
- **The vocabulary is the words the student must own at the end of the lesson, and it is chosen by importance, never by flavour.** Each listed item is a word the lesson teaches, trains and closes with; where two candidates compete, the more useful one wins — a pronoun, a common verb, or a word the student will use tomorrow outranks a word that merely illustrates the point. A word that only appears inside an example (the `at` of "I work at the mall", the `mall` itself) is explained on the page so the sentence is understood and is **not** a vocabulary item and is not tested. A concept the lesson teaches — a suffix pattern, a sound rule — is shown through whatever words demonstrate it; those words **are not part of the "vocabulary" for the lesson nor necessary to teach or train**, because they are there to make the concept visible. The mix across a track (about half its working words, a quarter everyday life, a quarter the wider world) describes what the course covers as it advances; it never licenses a flavour word ahead of a basic one, and the first lessons are the basics first.
- **A concept is not taught until the student could produce it unaided.** The five parts that get them there are the method in [TEACH → TRAIN → QUIZ], and every one of the five is required: a point with fewer than five has been mentioned, not taught, and a point with none of them has not been written yet.
- **Nothing is first met inside an activity.** Every word, form and idea an activity asks the student to answer for has already been taught or introduced — on this page, or by a lesson the course has taught. An activity may accompany the teaching it belongs to, and it may reuse what the page has just explained, but the first explanation of anything never appears inside it: an item that teaches while it tests is unanswerable for the student who has read nothing, and unmarkable as evidence for the teacher.
- **Thin is measured by consequence, not by length.** An explanation that leaves the student unable to say the sentence unaided is thin at any length; a short one that states the rule and its reason is not. Where a concept is expensive, its support grows — more teaching before it and more training after it — because importance outranks level.
- **The page ends by using every concept it taught on material the student has not seen**: mixed, new sentences, in the register's world — never the lesson's own examples again.
