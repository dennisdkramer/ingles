## [PASSES] — the three passes, in this order; the first pass decides nothing about form
### [1a] CONTENT — the raw lesson, before any shape
- **This lesson is imagined from scratch, and no part of its structure is inherited.** The number of sections, their order, their shapes, the first block, the form its training takes and every example are decided for this lesson alone, out of what this lesson teaches — never out of how the last page was built. A raw lesson that could be moved into another lesson's place by changing its words is a copy: rewrite it until its sequence is the one this content needs.
- **The page opens on the point it teaches.** The first teaching unit states what the student is about to learn; a scene, a dialog or a story is an example of content already stated and never the introduction. A page that opens on a conversation has shown the student something before telling them what it is for.
- **One section, one teaching point, and the heading names it.** The voice decides how the point is said and never blurs what it is. If a section cannot be summarised as "this section teaches …", or two headings could be swapped without the page changing, that section is a tour of the concept's uses rather than a step in a sequence: rewrite it until the one thing it teaches is on its face. Clarity and simplicity of the teaching point outrank the track's flavour every time — slang, scene and joke are cut the moment they make the point harder to find.
- The teaching sequence is written first, and every word the student will read, hear or answer is written with it: the headings, the explanations, the examples, the dialogs, the activities with their instructions and their keys, the word lists, the chart contents, and the lines a recording will carry.
- **A lesson about a tense draws the tense.** The word marks and the timeline are content, not decoration: the sentence is marked so the student can see which word carries what, and the line is drawn so they can see where *now* falls. A sentence the tagger mis-marks — the verb the lesson is about tagged as a noun — is rewritten until it marks the words the lesson is about; the marks are never dropped and never corrected by hand.
- **A song is considered here, and its section is planned here.** If a popular song carries the lesson's form in a line that can be pointed at, 1A writes that section — the line, what it shows, what the student predicts, the production that follows — and leaves the player for 1B, which cuts and times it.
- **Training trains the element it sits beside.** It may sit inline with that element or in a section of its own; its shape is chosen by what the student must do, and the shapes vary across the lesson — a lesson whose every activity is the same shape has not imagined its practice.
- **Every training block is justified in one sentence before its shape is used: what the student must produce, and why this control is the instrument for that work rather than another.** A gap, a radio, a checkbox, a written field, numbered chunks, a matching pool, four options, a chart whose cells are answered — each does one thing well and several badly. A defence that cannot be written means the shape is wrong, and the answer is a different shape rather than a weaker sentence. A defence that would read the same for the block beside it means one of the two was never chosen. "It is an activity, so it is a gap" is not a defence; "the student has to discriminate between two forms that both look right, which a gap cannot show" is.
- **Four questions come before any shape is chosen, and the shape is the answer to them**, not the starting point: **(a)** can these concepts be grouped or ordered so that each one explains the next? **(b)** how much of the student's success here depends on understanding the rule, how much on training the use until it is automatic, and how much on memorising an item — that mix decides whether the section needs explanation, repetition, recall, or all three? **(c)** is this the place to introduce, compare or remark on a synonym, antonym, homophone, homograph, false cognate or relevant idiom the roadmap's item brings with it? **(d)** given (a), (b) and (c), what is the best way to organise, display and teach it — and only then, which element does that?
- No metaphor where a fact belongs, no coined term, and never two tenses presented as one paragraph's single idea.
- Nothing about appearance is decided here: no `data-span`, no `data-look`, no visual's markup, no section invented to fill a row, no frame. A block is opened only where a teaching unit ends.
- **The prose is judged here, not measured later.** Read back what you have written and cut every sentence that pads, hedges, restates the one before it, or explains what the example beside it already shows. What is left hands over the rule, the reason and the contrast, and nothing else; a longer explanation is not a fuller one.
- **The ledger is read before the first heading is written.** Four separate rules, each with its own window, all read off the last rows: **one element the previous 3 lessons did not use** (every lesson); **no element in more than half this lesson's activity blocks**; **no teaching device in more than `max(1, floor(0.35 × its sections))` sections**; and **no four block subjects in a row that already ran inside one section of a recent lesson**. From lesson 10 the slower rules bite too — at least 60% of the eleven elements inside the last 5 lessons, and all eleven inside the last 10. A shape chosen because it was not on that list is still chosen for this lesson's content or not at all: the ledger forbids, it never nominates.
- This is the pass that decides whether the lesson is good. A block whose content is thin cannot be rescued by the shape it is given later, so the [COVERAGE] rules below are met on the words alone.
### [1b] ARRANGEMENT — the shapes, once the words exist
- **Adjacent things of similar height go into one flex row, and that is the default**: two blocks, or two items inside a block — the rule with the chart it is read with, the sentence with the sentence beside it, the question with the field that answers it. `.rows` shares the width and matches the height across its cells, so **nothing carries a size of its own**; `.cols` is for interchangeable cells that must wrap. Nothing is measured.
- `data-span` and `data-look` are set now and only here. **A span is a repair, never a plan**: 1A has already paired what belongs together, and `data-span` is written only where that row reads badly — too tight for its content, an element squeezed below the width it draws at, a lone block with half a line empty beside it. One tint and one outline per section at most; `data-look` changes the paint only and never marks importance.
- Each element's markup is copied from the example capability-map.html draws for it, never from memory.
- The tests are the gate: an element that only repeats the sentence beside it, or whose removal would cost the student nothing, is not used. **A block of prose with no element is the normal case**, and an element with nothing purposeful to accompany it stands outside a block rather than being given one. A chart clarifies an example; it never carries the teaching.
- The tools run here — `qa/pos.mjs` for the marks, `qa/audio.mjs` for a recording, `PronKey.render` for a key — and what they print is pasted, never retyped. The player a [1a] song section left a place for is built here.
- **The row is finished here.** Append to this lesson's line the elements it actually built, with a count for each block (`el:gap:3,mc:1`), the quiz's elements (`qel:`), and the register score (`reg:in-voice/all`) judged **section by section** against the register's voice. The element that holds a control carries `data-activity="<its element id>"` — `gap`, `chunks`, `stripgaps`, `chartanswer`, `dialoggap`, `mc`, `matching`, `correction`, `translation`, `dictation`, `flip` — which is the id of the markup box the builder copied it from, so the ledger and the page cannot disagree. A game is a skin over one of these and records the element underneath it. `gap` is the one token without a box of its own: it is the single keyed field, and its markup is the `input.gap` line any box above already shows.
### [1c] WORDING — after the page is built
- Prose may be shortened to remove a wrapped stub at the end of a line, or lengthened to fill one, only where the lesson does not get worse for it; a block shorter than its neighbour in a row may gain an example or a question to even the heights.


## [AUTHORING] - 
- Each lesson is imagined and generated in a unique and varied way, the order and type of its sections and blocks are never a standard - they are conceived independently as the optimal way to deliver the specific lesson and/or the specific elements within it. No lesson may reuse the structure of another, and no lesson repeats the structure of the page before it.
- Teaching is carried by sentences that explain and examples that show; a visual is one way an example is drawn, chosen because it answers a question the sentence cannot, never because a section is expected to have one.
- **Every gloss, definition and translation a student is asked to accept is verifiable.** It comes from a dictionary or from the way the word is actually used, and a native speaker would sign off on it. A gloss may shorten a definition; it may never add a claim the word does not carry, and it may never put the word with an object or in a collocation English does not use — a card back that reads plausibly but is not the word's meaning teaches an error that the activity then drills into memory.
- The tone and vocabulary described in the REGISTER is maintained throughout. The ROADMAP teaching concepts, vocabulary, and examples are thoroughly explored, explained, trained, and tested — there is no limit to distinct, useful teaching and/or training content: if the student is expected to need a lot of training to master a concept or memorize crucial vocab, then the lesson should reflect and fulfil that. Depth, thorough explanation (**clarity**, never filler or prose for its own sake) and training are better than less of them — all of it in an engaging and relevant lesson for the <TRACK>-REGISTER.md [PERSONA].
## [LEVEL] — the lesson number is a curriculum position, and it fixes the page's difficulty
- **This section outranks every other consideration of difficulty.** The roadmap says what the lesson carries; the register says whose voice and which world; this section says how hard each item is — and no band, no phrase in the roadmap and no flavour in the register lowers it or raises it.
- **Everything the page asks the student to read, answer or produce — the prose, the prompts, the activities, the quiz — is authored at a level that is accessible but mildly challenging for the student who has arrived at that lesson.** Accessible: nothing depends on English the course has not taught, nothing is explained by a word the page has not defined, and the instruction is written in words that student can follow at this number. Mildly challenging: the page never prints the answer beside the question, and every item asks for one thing more than the student could do without thinking about it. Accessible alone teaches nothing; challenging alone cannot be read.
- **"STUDENT LEVEL" is assumed, and it is relative to the lesson's number and to the English concepts and vocabulary the course has taught up to that point.** **Ability grows with the lesson number**: lesson 1 assumes the student knows no English, and by lesson 50 the student should have achieved functional fluency. The page must read as the step it is — a lesson 12 that could be printed at 40, or a 40 that could be printed at 12, was not written for its number.
- **The number is both floor and ceiling.** The page may assume every concept, form and word the course taught through the lesson before it, and nothing else; and it must demand the work of a student who has had that many lessons.
- **Difficulty is what the student must do — never how rare the words are.** A page is hard when the student must hold a rule across a whole sentence, choose between forms that compete, move several things at once, produce rather than select, and notice where the rule does not apply. It is easy when the student fills one word per line with the answer printed beside it, however advanced the topic's name.
- **The bands below decide one thing only: how much Portuguese may carry the instruction and the explanation. They are a quantity, never a rule.** Where anything else in this file, in the roadmap or in the register says otherwise, it wins and the band is ignored; and no band may be read as licensing a short answer, a single concept, a simpler item or a milder page.
  - **1–10, foundation** — Portuguese, or two-word English, may carry the instruction and the explanation.
  - **11–25, building** — Portuguese where the English mapping misleads and for the one word that would otherwise stop the page; the rest of the instruction is short English.
  - **26–40, consolidating** — English carries the instruction and the explanation; Portuguese appears where it misleads (a false cognate, a form with no counterpart) and nowhere else.
  - **41–50, advanced** — English only; a Portuguese aside is the exception, and the page has to have a reason for it.
- **A page can be advanced in content and beginner in difficulty, and that is a defect.** Reported speech at 44 is not a table of shifts plus one-word gaps: it is the system (what moves, why, in what order), the switch that turns the shift off, the exceptions, and the student producing paragraphs of relayed speech.
- **The explanation is not the material.** The prose that explains a concept or a vocabulary item may be plain: it should sit at the lesson's band, and it does not have to be advanced. What must be at the band is the English the student handles — every example sentence, quoted line, dialog turn, gap answer, drill item and quiz item. A page that explains reported speech elegantly around "I'm tired." is a beginner page wearing advanced prose, and it fails this section even when every paragraph reads beautifully.
- **The examples are also the register.** Those same sentences carry the track's voice: they are written in the REGISTER's scenes and with its own words from its lists, never in a neutral course voice that merely describes them. A block whose examples could be printed in any track is a register failure even when its topic fits the track.
- **What maps directly** onto Portuguese is cheap; what maps onto it and misleads is expensive; what has no counterpart is built from nothing. Frequency says nothing here: "descend" is cheaper than "go down" although it is the rarer word, and the present perfect costs this student more than the past perfect although it costs a native speaker less — the mapping breaks in one and holds in the other.
- **Sentence complexity is what the sentence asks the reader to hold at once** — a clause inside a clause, a tense that changes mid-sentence, a reference the reader carries to the end, a form whose Portuguese counterpart does not match.
- **Importance can outrank level.** A concept the track cannot do without belongs in the lesson that needs it even when it is expensive, and then the support around it grows — more teaching before the item, more training after it. The item is not lowered and the level is not moved; the support adds teaching and training, and it never restores an earlier band's scaffolding.
- Optimize concept/element/content grouping/order according to the needs of the student. [Introduce, compare, or make note of] [synonyms, antonyms, homophones, homographs, false cognates, relevant idioms/expressions, etc.] that are relevant to the lesson content.
- Every (new word/form/concept) the student must understand is presented before it is used; if a block wants the student to *infer*, the page says so. 
- Teach the rule, the reasoning (the mental model), and its direct consequences.
- Multiple-choice options are shuffled so the right one is not always in the same slot. No copy-exercises: the student produces from memory or meaning,
- **An item never asks for a judgement on an axis the page has not defined before the item**, and no invented terminology is used to name or classify a form: a term the student has not met is taught before it is used, and a term nobody outside a textbook uses is not used at all.
- **The options of an item match the lesson in concept and the level in length and linguistic complexity**: a one-word answer is not offered beside three-word distractors, and a distractor is wrong for a reason the page has already covered — never because it is absurd, foreign to the scene or obviously the odd one out.
- Portuguese is used to explain content, compare/contrast grammar/vocabulary between the languages, or give translations when the necessary information in english would not be well understood by the student at the level of the lesson that contains it or when it is significantly more efficient to explain something with portuguese.
## [COVERAGE] — the roadmap section is a floor, not a summary
- **Every concept, construction and example the roadmap section names is accounted for in the page**, and every vocabulary item it lists is carried where the register licenses it. The lesson is not finished because the section has been mentioned: it is finished when each concept has been taught, contrasted, trained and tested.
- **A concept is not taught until the student could produce it unaided.** For each concept the page carries the rule; the reasoning (the mental model) and what follows from it; where Portuguese maps, misleads or has no counterpart; worked examples in the register's own scenes; training in which the student produces it rather than recognizes it; and a check that would fail had the student only read the explanation. A label, a table row or a passing mention is not teaching.
- **Thin is measured by consequence, not by length.** An explanation that leaves the student unable to say the sentence unaided is thin at any length; a short one that hands them the rule and its reason is not. Where a concept is expensive, its support grows — more teaching before it and more training after it — because importance outranks level.
- **The page ends by using every concept it taught on material the student has not seen**: mixed, new sentences, in the register's world — never the lesson's own examples again.

## [TEACH → TRAIN → QUIZ] — the three obligations, in this order
- **Teach** the elements of its content thoroughly — each concept and vocabulary item the lesson owns, with the rule, its reasoning, its consequences and the Portuguese contrast where the mapping misleads — and present each before the student is asked to use it. **Train** what was taught: the student practices it, producing from memory or meaning, on material that is new to the page rather than a copy of the explanation, and the training follows the teaching it belongs to. **Training may sit inside the teaching section it belongs to or stand in a section of its own** — the lesson chooses, for the flow its content wants, and neither shape is the default. **Quiz** at the end: the lesson closes with a quiz (the last section before the notebook) that tests the lesson's content as a whole — every concept the lesson taught appears in it, alone or inside a sentence that combines it with another, on material the student has not seen, with its keys written — so that passing it requires having learned the lesson, not having read it.

## [MUSIC] — a section built on a recording, when the lesson carries one
- **The song is chosen by the lesson's structure, never by taste.** The line must carry the form or the vocabulary the lesson is teaching in a way that can be pointed at on the page. A line that merely paraphrases it is not a candidate, and a well-known song whose line teaches nothing is not one either. The phrase is found by web search — LRCLIB cannot search lyric text — and then proved with `--finds`, which prints the line number; a line number is never passed unproved.
- **Two runs, the second one pinned.** Run `qa/audio.mjs` once without `--out` to see which upload it paired (it checks the length within 2 s), then repeat with `--lrc-id` pinned, because the site's results move between calls and an unpinned run can pair a different edit of the same song.
- **The tool prints, the page pastes.** `qa/audio.mjs` writes `assets/<name>.mp3` itself and prints the figure markup with its `data-t` values, its `data-duration`, and the provenance row. Nothing is retyped and **no `data-t` is ever edited by hand**: a highlight that fires early or late is moved with `--cue-shift`, an excerpt sitting wrong inside the recording is moved with `--early`, and either flag leaves the command that reproduces the page. On a lesson page the source is `../assets/<name>.mp3`, and the printed row goes to `assets/README.md`. The player script is already in `_shell.html`; nothing is added to the page for audio.
- **Music is a great teaching tool and should be used in many lessons where it is sufficiently relevant - it is an addition, it accompanies but does not take the place of needed teaching or training content.** It has a teaching point that its heading names, and it runs the lesson's own sequence on that line: the student predicts what they will hear, hears it, the line is marked and read, the rule is stated as it appears **in this line**, and the student then produces the same structure from words that are not the song's. The recording is evidence, never decoration — if the section would teach the same thing with the audio removed, it is not a section and the line was not relevant.

## [CHECK] — before the build, read the page back against its strongest inputs
- Re-read the roadmap section line by line and name the block that teaches each concept and the block that trains it. A concept with no teaching block is a defect; a concept with no training block is unfinished. (Catches the concept that was mentioned and dropped.)
- Re-read the register. A scene, person, word or topic that belongs to another track is removed, however well it carries the grammar. (Catches an adult lesson drifting into business English.)
- Find the concept that is only a label, a table row, a chart cell or a drawing, with no sentence stating its rule. (Catches the thin explanation the student cannot reproduce.)
- Read the quiz: it is the last section before the notebook, it tests every concept the lesson taught on material the student has not seen, and every item carries its key. (Catches a lesson that ends in practice and never proves the learning.)
- Read three example sentences and three drill items at random — the prose around them is not the evidence. Name the band they demand and the register they speak: if they could be printed in a lesson of an earlier band, or in another track's book, the page failed where the student actually works, however good the explanation was.
- Read the section headings alone, in order, with the body hidden. Each must name the one thing its section teaches, and each may assume only what the headings before it established. A heading that names a topic rather than a point ("Reported speech", "More uses", "In the wild") is the tour this check exists to catch. (Catches a page that is full of the concept and vague about what it is teaching.)
- Read the page's difficulty against its number: name the band, then name what the page demands — the length of its answers, whether it re-teaches a lesson the student has already had, whether a lesson 5 could read its instructions. A page in the advanced band that asks for one word at a time, prints the answer beside the gap, or explains what the student already knows is beginner scaffolding wearing advanced content. (Catches a lesson that is advanced in content and beginner in difficulty.)
- Read the same page for the other half of the number rule: **accessible** — the teacher is beside the student and may explain anything the prose leaves out, so an instruction that is hard to read alone is not a defect; what is a defect is wording that needs a word the page never gives them, or an item the student could not complete **even after** the teacher explains it — and **mildly challenging** — is there at least one item whose answer is not already printed beside it, and one the student must produce rather than pick? A page that fails the first cannot be taught from; a page that fails the second is a page of drills a student already knows. (Catches the lesson that is readable and empty, or unreachable even with help.)
- Read each visual for the element that draws it: the frame of a dialog, a player or a map is `viz`; a chart is a `table.chart`; a card is a `label.flip` carrying its own width; a gap is an `input.gap` with `data-answer`. A shape that renders but draws no frame, or draws two, means the visual was described rather than built. (Catches the dialog that is only a list of turns.)
- Read the first block on its own. It names and states what the lesson teaches. If it is a conversation or a scene, the page has opened on an example of something it never said. (Catches the lesson that starts with a dialog.)
- Read the section shapes and the first three blocks against **the last three rows of the ledger**, never against the previous page — that page is closed. If the subjects and their order are the same, this page is that page's skeleton with new words. (Catches the lesson that was copied rather than imagined.)
- Read the **prose** on its own, as a judgement and not a measurement: is any of it padding, hedging, restating what the previous sentence said, or explaining what the example beside it already shows? The prose exists to teach — hand over the rule, the reason, the contrast — and anything in it that does none of those three is cut. A short page that explains directly beats a long one that circles.
- Read the page for the register's `locale` list: **two occurrences warn, three fail.** A word the register marks as another variety of English is a mistake at any level, however natural it sounds. (`npm run batchcheck` counts them.)
- Score the register **section by section**: which sections are defensibly in this track's voice — its room, its people, its words — out of all of them. Below 75% warns, below 60% fails. A formal business voice teaching street slang is the failure this score exists to name, and so is prose nobody would say. The number goes in the ledger.
- Read the defences, one sentence per training block, and read them together. Each names the work its control does and why no other control does it; two defences that are the same sentence in different words mean one block was never justified. A page whose every training block is selection has taught nothing the student can produce, however different the shapes look. (Catches training that repeats one form because a shape was assumed rather than chosen.)
- Append the row before the lesson is closed: the elements with their counts, the quiz's elements, the register score. A lesson with no row cannot be checked by anything, and the next lesson has nothing to avoid.
- For a lesson about a tense: find the timeline and the marks, and check they mark the words the lesson teaches. A tense described in prose and never drawn is a tense the student cannot see. (Catches the lifeless tense lesson.)
- **Any lesson that explains a verb tense or any other element of time carries at least one small block with a timeline**: an example case, and what the shape of that case means said in words — where the action sits, how long it lasts, whether *now* is inside it. That block is where a lesson about *do* says the simple present is *in general* and not *agora mesmo*, and shows it on the line. (Catches a tense explained as a form with no picture of what it means.)
- Check every block, class and attribute on the page against capability-map.html. A shape the map does not draw is not used, and a class remembered from another page is not used. (Catches markup that renders but no longer matches the contract.)
- Read the music section, if the page has one: the line it quotes was proved with `--finds`, its `data-t` values are the tool's and were not edited, its provenance row is in `assets/README.md`, and the section teaches something the page could not teach as well with the sound off. (Catches a clip that decorates a section instead of carrying its point.)
- Open the finished page from the index, never by its address: a lesson URL typed or shared lands on sign-in by design (`engine1.js` verifies the gate in `sessionStorage`), so a page that "will not open" that way is working correctly. (Catches a run that repairs a gate that is doing its job.)
- Then `npm run build:lessons`, commit, and push.
