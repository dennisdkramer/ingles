## sound

- `sound/vowel-no-fixed` — The five vowel letters a, e, i, o, u each spell several different sounds, so a letter never tells you the sound. → L1
- `sound/short-vs-long` — A single vowel letter reads short when a consonant closes the syllable and reads its own name when a silent e closes the word (hat / hate, bit / bite). → L2
- `sound/vowel-pairs` — Two vowel letters written together are one sound (ee, ea, ai, oo, ou), never two sounds in a row. → L2
- `sound/contractions-be` — I'm, you're, he's, it's, we're and they're are the spoken forms of be and the only forms that fit normal speech. → L3
- `sound/contractions-negative` — Don't, doesn't, isn't, aren't and can't are the spoken negatives, and the two-word form only carries emphasis. → L7
- `sound/can-vs-cant` — Can shrinks to a weak "k'n" in fast speech while can't keeps its vowel, so the loud word is the negative. → L14
- `sound/ed-sound-rules` — The -ed ending is /id/ only after t or d (wanted, needed) and /t/ or /d/ everywhere else. → L19
- `sound/gonna-reduction` — Gonna is how "going to" sounds before a verb, and the full form belongs in writing that lasts. → L26
- `sound/wanna-gotta` — Wanna is "want to" and gotta is "got to", and both are chunks you cannot conjugate. → L31
- `sound/kinda-sorta` — Kind of and sort of reduce to kinda and sorta, and they soften the word after them. → L31
- `sound/linking-consonant-vowel` — A final consonant slides onto the next vowel, so "turn it off" sounds like one word. → L31
- `sound/weak-forms-of-to` — To, for and of shrink to a schwa in speech, which is why "to" sounds like "tuh". → L31

## cognate

- `cognate/suffix-codes` — A cognate's ending is a code you can read before you say the word: -tion reads ção, -ly reads mente, -ity reads idade, -ous reads oso. → L1
- `cognate/partial-trap` — Some cognates match in form but not in meaning (actually is not atualmente, push is not puxar), so the shape is a hint and never a translation. → L2

## method

- `method/one-rule-then-drill` — Every lesson states one rule you can repeat out loud and then drills it, and the rule is what you keep. → L1

## hook

- `hook/be-state` — Be links a subject to a state, a thing or a place, and it never takes a main action verb after it. → L3
- `hook/get-wildcard` — Get covers obtain, become, arrive and receive, so when the exact verb is missing, get is the guess that usually works. → L3
- `hook/it-empty-subject` — Where Portuguese leaves the subject empty, English fills it with it: it's cold, it's eight, it's raining. → L3
- `hook/have-own` — Have means possess and never needs do in the affirmative: I have a bike, not I do have a bike. → L4
- `hook/do-action` — Do stands in for any action already understood and is the verb for tasks and chores. → L4
- `hook/make-vs-do` — Make means create or produce a result and do means perform a task, so PT "fazer" splits into two verbs. → L4
- `hook/go-place` — Go means movement toward a place, so a place follows it, and with no place go is not the verb you want. → L4
- `hook/there-is-are` — There is and there are announce that something exists and they replace PT "tem" with nothing before them. → L5

## article

- `article/a-vs-the` — A and an introduce one of many while the points at the one already known or the only one there is. → L5
- `article/no-article-general` — Plural and uncountable nouns take no article when you speak in general: I like games, not I like the games. → L5

## mode

- `mode/skeleton` — Every English sentence is subject + mode + verb + object + place + time, and the mode slot is where the grammar lives. → L6
- `mode/empty-mode-verb-marks` — When the mode slot is empty the verb itself carries the marker, which is the -s of he, she and it. → L6
- `mode/verb-base-form` — Once the mode slot is filled, the verb after it is the base form and nothing about the verb changes. → L6
- `mode/filled-mode-marker-moves` — When do or does fills the mode, the -s leaves the verb and sits on the mode: he doesn't play, never he doesn't plays. → L7
- `mode/do-support` — English inserts do as the mode whenever there is no auxiliary to move, so negation and questions borrow it. → L7
- `mode/negation-dont-doesnt` — Don't and doesn't negate actions while isn't and aren't negate be, and the two kinds never mix. → L7
- `mode/imperative-base` — A command is the bare verb with no subject: open the box, pass it, look. → L23
- `mode/imperative-negative` — Don't + base verb forbids an action, with no subject and no -s anywhere. → L23
- `mode/lets-invite` — Let's + base verb invites a group to act together and always includes the speaker. → L23
- `mode/polite-imperative-softeners` — Please, can you and could you soften a command, and the bare verb with no softener is the blunt default. → L23

## present

- `present/third-person-s` — He, she and it take -s on the verb, and I, you, we and they take nothing. → L6
- `present/facts-not-now` — The simple present states a fact, a habit or a general truth and never an action happening at this moment. → L9
- `present/general-truth` — Statements about the world in general use the bare form with a plural subject: cats hate water. → L9
- `present/habit` — A repeated action is simple present even when it happened today, because the repetition is the point. → L9
- `present/scheduled-fact-future` — A timetable can use the present for the future because the time is part of the fact: the stream starts at eight. → L9
- `present/frequency-adverbs` — Always, usually, often, sometimes and never go before the main verb and after be. → L10
- `present/routine-chain` — A routine is told in simple present, in order, joined by then and after that. → L10
- `present/every-and-once` — Every day, once a week and twice a month express frequency as a phrase at the end of the sentence. → L10
- `present/simple-vs-continuous` — The same verb changes meaning with the form: the simple present is the fact and the continuous is the action in progress. → L12
- `present/not-now-trap` — "I study English" is a fact about you and never what you are doing now, so it cannot answer "what are you doing". → L12
- `present/question-simple-vs-continuous` — "Do you play?" asks about a habit and "Are you playing?" asks about right now. → L12
- `present/state-verbs-no-ing` — Know, like, want, need, hate and believe name states, so they never take -ing. → L13
- `present/state-in-now` — A state verb uses the simple present even for this moment: I want water now. → L13
- `present/have-possession-vs-action` — Have as possession never takes -ing while have + an event does: I'm having lunch is fine, I'm having a bike is not. → L13
- `present/senses-simple` — See, hear and smell use the simple present for what your senses report, and the -ing form means something else entirely. → L13

## continuous

- `continuous/be-plus-ing` — The continuous is be + verb-ing and it says the action is in progress at the moment you speak. → L11
- `continuous/right-now` — PT "estou estudando" is I am studying: the -ing is the only thing marking "now". → L11
- `continuous/temporary` — The continuous also covers a temporary situation around now, like living somewhere for a month. → L11
- `continuous/ing-spelling` — Double the final consonant after a short stressed vowel (getting, running) and drop a silent e before -ing (making). → L11
- `continuous/with-always` — Always + continuous marks annoyance at a repeated habit: you're always losing the charger. → L12

## prep

- `prep/at-night-in-the-morning` — In the morning and in the afternoon take in, at night takes at, and a day takes on. → L10
- `prep/to-direction` — To marks movement toward a place or a target: go to, talk to, give it to. → L15
- `prep/to-infinitive-marker` — To before a verb is the infinitive marker and not a direction, as in I want to play. → L15
- `prep/for-purpose` — For marks purpose or the person who receives something: for fun, this is for you. → L15
- `prep/para-split` — PT "para" splits into to for direction and target and for for purpose and beneficiary, and choosing wrong is the classic error. → L15
- `prep/on-contact` — On marks contact with a surface or a connection: on the table, on a call, on the app. → L16
- `prep/in-container` — In marks being inside a larger thing: in the box, in the game, in a month. → L16
- `prep/at-point` — At marks a point in space or time with no inside: at the door, at eight, at school. → L16
- `prep/time-preps` — In takes a month, year or part of a day, on takes a day or date, and at takes a clock time. → L16
- `prep/verb-plus-prep-unit` — Some verbs carry a fixed preposition as one unit: look at, wait for, depend on, listen to. → L17
- `prep/prep-changes-meaning` — Changing the preposition changes the meaning (look at, look for, look after), so the pair is the unit to learn. → L17
- `prep/prep-at-end-in-questions` — In a question the preposition can stay at the end with its object understood: who are you waiting for. → L17

## question

- `question/do-does-front` — A yes/no question puts do or does in front of the subject and leaves the verb in base form. → L8
- `question/short-answer` — A short answer repeats the mode and never the main verb: yes I do, no I don't. → L8
- `question/wh-front` — A wh-word comes first and the same question order follows it: where do you play. → L8
- `question/subject-who` — When who is the subject of the question, no do is added: who plays, never who does play. → L8

## past

- `past/be-was-were` — Was and were are the past of be: I, he, she and it take was, and you, we and they take were. → L18
- `past/there-was-were` — There was and there were announce what existed, and the following noun decides which one. → L18
- `past/was-there-question` — Questions put was or were first: were you at the game. → L18
- `past/be-past-vs-simple` — The past of be is was or were and never did, so "I did be tired" does not exist. → L18
- `past/regular-ed` — A regular past adds -ed to the base verb and that is the whole change. → L19
- `past/past-time-markers` — Yesterday, last night, two hours ago and in 2020 put the action in the past, and only past forms follow them. → L19
- `past/irregular-verbs` — The most frequent verbs do not add -ed but change form completely, so they have to be learned one by one. → L20
- `past/did-back-to-base` — After did or didn't the verb returns to base: did you go, never did you went. → L20
- `past/did-questions` — Past questions and negatives use did + base, and the past marker never appears twice. → L20
- `past/irregular-families` — Irregular pasts group by pattern (sing sang, drink drank, begin began), so learn the pattern and not each verb alone. → L20
- `past/finished-time-rule` — A finished time word forces the past simple and blocks any reading that reaches to now. → L21
- `past/past-narrative` — A short story is told in past simple, one finished action after another. → L21
- `past/did-vs-was` — An action goes to the past simple and a state or a description goes to was or were. → L21
- `past/used-to-habit` — Used to + base names a past habit or state that is no longer true now. → L42
- `past/used-to-vs-past-simple` — Used to stresses "not any more" while the past simple only reports one finished action. → L42
- `past/used-to-be-vs-be-used-to` — Used to + base is a past habit and be used to + -ing is being accustomed to something. → L42
- `past/used-to-question-negative` — Did you use to and I didn't use to drop the -d as soon as did appears. → L42
- `past/continuous-background` — Was or were + -ing sets the scene or an action in progress when something else happened. → L43
- `past/when-vs-while` — While introduces the longer background action and when introduces the short interruption. → L43
- `past/continuous-vs-past-simple` — The past continuous was already in progress while the past simple happened and finished. → L43
- `past/two-continuous-and` — Two continuous actions joined by while were running at the same time. → L43

## count

- `count/countable-vs-uncountable` — Countable things take a number and uncountable ones (money, water, time) do not, which decides every word around them. → L24
- `count/some-vs-any` — Some goes in statements and offers and any goes in negatives and questions. → L24
- `count/much-vs-many` — Many goes with countable plurals and much with uncountable nouns, both mostly in questions and negatives. → L24
- `count/a-lot-of-both` — A lot of works with countable and uncountable nouns, which is why it is the safe default in speech. → L24

## future

- `future/gonna-mode` — Gonna is a future mode: be + gonna + base verb says what is going to happen. → L26
- `future/going-to-place` — Going to + a place is movement and not future, because a place follows it and not a verb. → L26
- `future/gonna-go-distinction` — "I'm gonna go to the shop" is future + movement at once, so it is not a contradiction. → L26
- `future/will-sudden-decision` — Will marks a decision made as you speak, a promise or an offer. → L27
- `future/will-vs-gonna` — Gonna is a plan already made and will is a choice made now, so the difference is when it was decided. → L27
- `future/wont-negative` — Won't is the negative of will and the two-word form is for emphasis only. → L27
- `future/will-question-offer` — "Will you...?" asks a favour while "Are you gonna...?" asks about a plan. → L27
- `future/continuous-arrangement` — Be + verb-ing with a future time marks a fixed arrangement: I'm meeting him at eight. → L28
- `future/time-expression-required` — A future meaning needs a time word somewhere, or the sentence reads as happening now. → L28
- `future/in-two-weeks` — In + a length of time means after that much time, which is where PT "daqui a" lands. → L28
- `future/next-vs-last` — Next week and next month look forward and last week looks back, all with no article. → L28

## phrasal

- `phrasal/particle-changes-meaning` — The particle is not decoration: get up is leave bed and get off is leave a vehicle, so root and particle are learned together. → L29

## perfect

- `perfect/have-participle` — The perfect is have or has + past participle, and the participle is the third form and not the -ed past. → L32
- `perfect/irregular-participles` — Irregular verbs often have a third form different from the past: go went gone, do did done. → L32
- `perfect/no-time-anchor` — The perfect never names a finished time, because a finished time hands the sentence to the past simple. → L32
- `perfect/contractions-ive` — I've, you've, he's and we've are the spoken forms, and "he's" reads as either he is or he has from the next word. → L32
- `perfect/duration-from-past` — PT "trabalho aqui há cinco anos" is I have worked here for five years: a duration up to now takes the perfect. → L33
- `perfect/vs-simple-present` — The simple present cannot carry a duration from the past, which is the biggest PT to EN trap in the course. → L33
- `perfect/still-yet-duration` — The perfect says the action started then and is still true now, so it replaces PT's simple present with "há". → L33
- `perfect/experience-ever-never` — Ever and never with the perfect ask about experience across a whole life, with no date attached. → L34
- `perfect/asking-with-ever` — "Have you ever...?" is the experience question, and the answer usually drops to the past simple once a detail appears. → L34
- `perfect/just-already-yet` — Just is a moment ago, already is sooner than expected, and yet covers everything up to now in negatives and questions. → L34
- `perfect/vs-past-when-question` — "When did you...?" demands the past simple and "Have you...?" demands the perfect. → L34
- `perfect/for-length` — For takes a length of time: for two years, for ten minutes. → L35
- `perfect/since-point` — Since takes the starting point: since 2020, since Monday, since I was nine. → L35
- `perfect/how-long-question` — "How long have you...?" is the duration question, and the simple present cannot ask it. → L35
- `perfect/ago-vs-since` — "Two years ago" is a finished point for the past simple while "for two years" is a length for the perfect. → L35
- `perfect/past-perfect` — Had + participle marks the earlier of two past events. → L46
- `perfect/past-perfect-order` — "When I got there the game had started" lets had say which event came first, so no before is needed. → L46
- `perfect/before-experience` — Had never + participle sets an experience before a past moment: I had never seen it until then. → L46
