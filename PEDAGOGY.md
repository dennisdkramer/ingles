## Curriculum

Each lesson builds on the previous one — it reuses vocabulary and structures the student already knows and introduces one new thing at a time. Do not skip or reorder. The track is **business-casual**: the grammar and vocabulary of a native speaker's normal working day — phrasal verbs and common expressions first, formal/"term of the art" equivalents only when the everyday register genuinely needs them.

### Stage 1 — sentence skeleton + first real talk + the phrasal-verb system

1. **Introductions, to be & the sentence subject** — cognates, am/is/are, a/an, vowels; the subject is required (no pro-drop); the dummy "it" ("It's 8pm", "It's raining").
2. **Question words** — who/what/when/where/why/how with "be"; small-talk launch ("How's it going?", "What's up?" with its 3 senses, "How are things?"); first repair chunk ("Sorry, could you say that again?").
3. **"do" & the simple present (questions)** — do/does; job small talk ("What do you do?"); "do" as a main verb.
4. **Simple present & the sentence skeleton** — third-person -s, don't/doesn't, frequency; the fixed word order [subject] [mode] [verb] [object] [place/who-with] [time]; routine verb thread (get up, go to work, have breakfast, take the bus, do, make).
5. **Phrasal verbs & particles — the core system** — verb + particle = new meaning; particles up/over/back (get up, look up, hit up, send over, look over, get back, go back); real contact phrases with register (hit me up / give me a call / let me know).
6. **Present continuous vs simple present** — "now" (I am studying) vs "in general" (I study); "What are you working on?".

### Stage 2 — past & future + the communication toolkit

7. **Plurals & this/that/these/those** — regular/irregular plurals, demonstratives.
8. **There is / there are & place prepositions** — in/on/under/next to/between…; particle recycle (over there).
9. **Past simple** — was/were + -ed; past small talk ("How was your weekend?"); past of thread verbs and phrasal verbs.
10. **The future: will / going to / "gonna"** — plans vs on-the-spot decisions; reduction launch "gonna" (a MODE, not a verb) + "wanna"; "Any plans for the weekend?".
11. **Past continuous vs past simple** — a long action in progress interrupted by a short one ("I was working when you called").
12. **Present perfect (1)** — experience + duration ("I have worked here for 5 years" — the flagship PT trap); for/since; "Have you ever…?".
13. **Present perfect (2) vs past simple** — finished vs unfinished time; ever/never/already/yet/just; discourse markers actually/basically.
14. **Comparatives & superlatives** — opinions launch ("I think … is better", "In my opinion"); hedges kinda/sorta.
15. **Modals: can / must / have to / should** — reductions gotta/hafta/dunno; repair launch ("Could you speak slower?", "I didn't catch that", "What do you mean by…?"); polite requests (Could you…? / Would you mind…?).
16. **Countable/uncountable + quantifiers** — some/any/much/many/a lot of/a few/a little (business: information, advice, money, time).
17. **Prepositions of time & movement** — at/on/in, to/from; recycle the particle thread.
18. **"Get" deep-dive & make/do** — get as the coringa (receive/become/arrive/understand + get back to, get up, get along with, get over); make vs do.
19. **Conditionals (1st & 2nd) + would** — if I have time… / if I were you…; would for polite offers.

### Stage 3 — intermediate grammar + the communication toolkit

20. **Present perfect continuous** — "I've been working here for two years"; "How long have you been…?"; discourse marker "So…".
21. **Past perfect & narrative order** — "I'd already sent it when…"; discourse-marker home: first/then/after that/finally/anyway; telling a work story in order.
22. **Future (full system)** — will / gonna / present continuous for arrangements / present simple for schedules; recycle gonna/wanna; linking "whaddaya"/"couldja"; fillers honestly/I mean/you know.
23. **Modals of deduction & possibility** — must / might / could / can't ("He must be in a meeting"); recycle repair.
24. **Everyday passive & get-passive** — "The email was sent," "It got lost," "It's done" — recycles "get".
25. **Reported speech & reporting verbs** — "He said he'd call," "She asked me to send it"; say/tell/ask.
26. **Conditionals review + wish/if only** — zero/first/second/third; "I wish I had more time," "If only I'd known"; opinion recycle.
27. **Question formation & indirect questions** — subject vs object questions; "Do you know when…?"; repair/softening home: "Could you tell me…?", "Just to confirm…".
28. **Relative clauses (everyday)** — who/that/which/where; defining clauses; omitting the pronoun.
29. **Gerunds & infinitives** — "I enjoy working," "I need to finish," "I stopped smoking" vs "I stopped to smoke"; high-frequency verb patterns.
30. **Phrasal verbs (2): work & communication** — get back to, follow up, go over, wrap up, reach out, fill in, point out, bring up; separable vs inseparable (multi-sense, register-aware).
31. **Phrasal verbs (3): plans, problems & changes** — put off, call off, set up, figure out, come up with, run into, deal with, work out, end up (multi-sense, register-aware).

### Stage 4 — consolidation & application (fluency at IELTS General ≈ 6.0)

32. **Listening: connected speech & reductions** — all reductions (gonna/wanna/gotta/hafta/kinda/sorta/dunno/lemme/gimme) + linking at natural speed; dictation + shadowing (consolidation, not first introduction).
33. **Meetings: small talk → opinions → decisions** — application lesson: open with small talk, give opinions, agree/disagree politely, clarify/confirm, summarize a decision.
34. **Negotiating & next steps (capstone)** — "To sum up," "Let's meet halfway," "What's the plan?", "Let's circle back," "I'll get back to you"; a full negotiation dialogue recycling reductions/phrasal verbs/conditionals/reported speech/opinions.

## Pedagogy principles

- **Contrast with Portuguese above all.** The highest-value content is where English does NOT behave like Portuguese. The simple present is the flagship example:
  - EN simple present = FACTS and IN-GENERAL statements only. It never touches the past, and touches the future only as a scheduled FACT ("I start school tomorrow", "the train leaves at six").
  - PT simple present → EN present perfect for duration from the past: "trabalho aqui há 5 anos" = "I HAVE worked here for 5 years" (never "I work here for 5 years").
  - PT simple present → EN present continuous for "right now": "estou estudando" = "I AM studying" (not "I study").
- **Reuse, then extend.** Start each lesson from the last one's phrases; add the new structure on top.
- **Audio everywhere, Portuguese only to explain.** Every new phrase carries ▶ TTS; PT text is for translation/explanation, not the target.
- **One idea per section.** A section teaches one thing; a chart, a dialogue or an exercise reinforces it.
- **"gonna" is a future MODE, not a verb.** Teach `[be] + gonna + verb` as the spoken future ("I'm gonna call" = *vou ligar*), and keep it separate from the verb "go" with a PLACE after it ("I'm going to the office" = *estou indo ao escritório*). "I'm gonna go to the office" is therefore not a contradiction — "gonna" marks future and "go" is the verb. The student must use "gonna" (not avoid it); it is the natural spoken form, while "going to" is the written/formal twin.
- **Track voice — business.** The business book aims at correct speech and grammar and leans on business vocabulary (meeting, deadline, invoice, client, report, schedule…), but it never avoids common expressions or contractions (don't/doesn't, I'm, gonna, 'll). Contractions are the default in speech; the "full forms" are for formal writing.
- **Practice quantity follows pedagogical weight.** When a concept is central to the lesson — or the student simply hasn't had enough exposure to it yet — generate more practice (more questions, longer phrases, an extra column) rather than fewer. A high-value, under-practised concept is never under-served just to keep a block small; add the extra practice it deserves.
- **Phrase length follows the student's place in the course.** The closer to the start, the shorter and simpler the phrases must stay; later content may lengthen them. That length in turn decides how a block reaches its width: short phrases get there with more phrases or more columns, longer phrases get there with length. There is no fixed beginner/advanced split — judge each block as you go.
- **Phrasal verbs first, formal only to map meaning.** Where a native speaker would say the phrasal verb (put off, call off, set up, get back to, go over, wrap up), that is what the student learns and produces. The formal single-word twin (postpone, cancel, arrange, return, review, conclude) appears only as a gloss so the student can connect meaning — never as the default answer or the preferred form.
- **Business-casual register is the target voice.** The book teaches the English of a normal working day — contractions, phrasal verbs, discourse markers and friendly-but-professional politeness — not jargon and not stiff written English. "Term of the art" business vocabulary is excluded; when a formal term is shown at all it is only to contrast with the natural spoken form.
- **Listening and speaking are trained, not just tested.** Every new item has audio; listening blocks teach connected speech and reductions; speaking blocks teach flow, linking and the chunks a native speaker produces automatically. Grammar is taught so the student can *generate* correct structure, not merely recognise it.

## Lesson & block types (synthesized from the 2026-10-21 research pass)

The business track keeps the established components (chart, dialogue, listen-and-choose, fill-in, translation, recording) and adds these patterns for intermediate→advanced — all built from the existing CSS components, so no new styling is required:

- **Phrasal-verb swap (natural-first).** A chart teaches the everyday phrasal verb alongside its formal twin, and the exercise asks the student to *replace the formal word with the phrasal verb* (postpone → put off; cancel → call off; arrange → set up; return a call → call back; review → go over; submit → hand in). The phrasal verb is always the answer the student produces; the formal twin is only there to map meaning.
- **Chunk bank (lexical approach).** Teach a set of whole, prefabricated expressions a native speaker says as one unit ("I'll get back to you," "Let me check," "Sounds good," "No worries," "Just a heads-up") with audio, then a matching/translation exercise. These chunks are the 55–80% of native speech that is formulaic (Lewis/Schmitt), and they build fluency faster than rules alone.
- **Connected-speech lab (listening).** Show full form vs reduced form side by side (want to → wanna; going to → gonna; have to → hafta; kind of → kinda; don't know → dunno; let me → lemme; give me → gimme) with ▶ audio, then a listen-and-choose that asks "what did you hear?" — training the ear for modern US English.
- **Micro-listening / dictation.** A short natural-speed sentence plays; the student types or picks exactly what they heard. The gap is a contiguous line, never literal underscores.
- **Register swap (formal ↔ natural).** Two columns or a chart contrast a stiff written phrase with its business-casual spoken twin ("I regret to inform you" ↔ "Sorry to say"; "We are unable to" ↔ "We can't"), and the exercise picks the natural one for speech.
- **Question-building.** Given an answer, form the question; given a context, ask the follow-up question. This is the single highest-leverage speaking/flow skill for keeping a conversation alive.
- **Repair & clarification.** Fixed phrases for when communication breaks ("Could you say that again?", "I didn't catch that", "What do you mean by…?"), practised as listen-and-repeat and a fill-in.
- **Speaking flow / shadowing.** A short, linked paragraph (or dialogue) read aloud and recorded, targeting linking, rhythm and the reduced forms from the connected-speech lab. The ⏺ recording prompt stays complete and read-aloud-able.

Sequencing rule for these new blocks: a new structure is always introduced in a chart/dialogue with ▶ audio first, then practised in Treino, then tested in the Quiz — the same one-idea-per-section discipline as the rest of the book.

## Language by student level
The mix of English and Portuguese shifts with the student's level. Portuguese text is ALWAYS italic — tag it with `<i>` (or the `.ptw`/`.pt` class), even inside a header or a chart column header.

- **Beginner (Book 1, lessons 1–10):** headers — section titles, exercise-group titles, chart column headers — are written in ENGLISH with the PORTUGUESE alongside, wherever both fit in the line/block/column header (Portuguese italic). When there is not room for both, keep only the Portuguese. Explanatory prose stays Portuguese.
- **Intermediate:** conceptual explanations stay Portuguese; simple explanations and reminders switch to English where the vocabulary is already known. Headers are English, but NEW vocabulary in those sections carries its Portuguese alongside where space allows.
- **Advanced:** almost entirely English; Portuguese appears only where there is a clear pedagogical motive or a complex explanation that needs it.
