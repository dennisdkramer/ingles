## Curriculum, Planning, and Course structure

1. Order is derived from this book's own difficulty model — not from any external grammar syllabus. 

2. Every is assessed by how far it sits from Portuguese (transparent / partial / opaque) times how important it is for understanding and producing spoken english  (core / common / marginal). 

3. Curriculum planning should distribute teaching of concepts and/or vocabulary between lessons based on the desired completion time of (about but not less than) 60 minutes. 

4. PEDAGOGICAL DIRECTIVES which direct the generation of a full lesson must include a brief and non-quantitative recommendation for balancing content quantity between concept(s) and training time


### The difficulty model

- **Distance from Portuguese (transfer).** Transparent = a direct anchor exists (a cognate, or a structure whose meaning maps cleanly). Partial = an anchor exists but misleads (the form maps, the meaning does not — or vice-versa). Opaque = no anchor; it must be built from nothing.
- **Utility in business-casual speech.** Core = the sentence cannot be formed without it (word order, do-support, the dummy subject, the top workhorse verbs). Common = frequent and useful but not sentence-blocking. Marginal = rare, formal, or replaceable.
- **Placement = the two axes combined by judgment.** Transparent + Core → earliest (fast wins). Opaque + Core → early, taught as a structural rule. Partial + Core → middle, taught as a meaning trap. Transparent + Marginal → a deliberate choice (early-with-its-kin OR late as "formal only"), recorded with a reason. Opaque + Common → mid-to-late, sized to importance. Opaque + Marginal → late or optional.
- **Vocabulary is concept → context → mapping.** Each Portuguese concept is enumerated across the contexts of a working day, mapped to the English word(s) a native actually uses in each context, and ranked by frequency/register. A cognate and its more-used cousin are taught together at introduction (with a frequency note); after that, teaching uses whichever is most common in that context. The concept inventory lives in VOCABULARY.md.
- **Carrier content escalates with the course.** Example phrases grow in length, clause complexity and register as the course advances.
- When appropriate, research several outside sources on the lesson's topic to brainstorm ideas, not to copy. 

## Pedagogy principles

- **Contrast with Portuguese above all.** The highest-value content is where English does NOT behave like Portuguese. The simple present is the flagship example:
  - EN simple present = FACTS and IN-GENERAL statements only. It never touches the past, and touches the future only as a scheduled FACT ("I start school tomorrow", "the train leaves at six").
  - PT simple present → EN present perfect for duration from the past: "trabalho aqui há 5 anos" = "I HAVE worked here for 5 years" (never "I work here for 5 years").
  - PT simple present → EN present continuous for "right now": "estou estudando" = "I AM studying" (not "I study").
- **Keep training past lesson concepts and vocabulary** Incorporate skills and vocabulary learned in prior lessons to keep knowledge fresh ONLY IF it does not distract or take time from the current lesson
- **Early foundations .** L1 teaches "vowels have no fixed sound"; cognates are taught with their suffix codes; reductions (gonna/wanna/woulda) are introduced early; `get` is taught as the wildcard ("coringa"); the quiz carries a short honesty note.
- **"gonna" is a future MODE, not a verb.** Teach `[be] + gonna + verb` as the spoken future ("I'm gonna call" = *vou ligar*), and keep it separate from the verb "go" with a PLACE after it ("I'm going to the office" = *estou indo ao escritório*). "I'm gonna go to the office" is therefore not a contradiction — "gonna" marks future and "go" is the verb. The student should use "gonna" (not avoid it); it is the natural spoken form, while "going to" is the written/formal twin.

- **Phrase length and complexity follow the student's place in the course.** The closer to the start, the shorter and simpler the phrases must stay; later content must lengthen AND grow in clause complexity and register — you must do your best to match phrasing, grammar, and vocabulary levels of content to the overall lesson level. That length in turn decides how a block reaches its width: short phrases get there with more phrases or more columns, longer phrases get there with length. There is no fixed beginner/advanced split — judge each block against the lesson's expected level.
- **Carrier content escalates with the course.** The example phrases and contexts that carry each lesson's grammar are matched to the student's level at that point: they draw on everything already taught and grow in length, clause complexity and business register as the course advances. When an early item is recycled, it reappears inside a richer frame.
- **Phrasal verbs first, formal only to map meaning.** Where a native speaker would say the phrasal verb (put off, call off, set up, get back to, go over, wrap up), that is what the student learns and produces. The formal single-word twin (postpone, cancel, arrange, return, review, conclude) appears only as a gloss so the student can connect meaning — never as the default answer or the preferred form.

- **"verb + preposition" units.** (look AT, go TO, give TO, talk TO, depend ON, wait FOR) Verbs should be introduced with accompanying prepositions. Where appropriate, contrast with related verb+preposition pairs. Understanding of prepositions is emphasized where appropriate throughout the course
- **The [mode] carries the grammar — the verb returns to base.** The sentence skeleton is [subject][mode][verb][object][place][time], where [mode] is the auxiliary slot. When [mode] is empty (affirmative simple present/past), the verb itself carries the marker (-s / -ed). When [mode] is filled — do/does/don't/doesn't (present), did/didn't (past), will/would/could/should/might/can/must (future & modals) — the marker moves to the [mode] and the verb returns to its base form. Teach this as ONE rule reused at every tense — not as separate "do-support", "negation", "past", "future" and "modal" rules.
- **High-frequency prepositions are taught by MEANING, early.** TO = direction/movement/change + the infinitive marker; FOR = purpose or duration; ON = connection/surface contact; IN = inside a larger thing; AT = a point in space or time / a target. The PT single word "para" splits into TO and FOR — flagged and drilled because it is a persistent PT→EN error. These meanings are integrated into the early routine/place/time lessons and recycled, not parked in one grammar lesson.
- **Group related items into families.** When vocabulary, phrases or structures can be taught at a similar grammatical level, teach them together as a set rather than scattered — e.g. a family of phrasal verbs built on ONE root verb (get back / get on / get up; look at / look for / look up / look over). Grouping by root or theme reveals the system and improves retention; a random assortment hides the pattern.
- **Factuality.** Every factual or statistical claim in the materials must be true and defensible. Never pad a section with an unverified assertion (e.g. a made-up claim about how common something is); say only what is true and useful.
- **Lesson size.** Each lesson robustly but concisely teaches its pedagogical concept(s) with no quantitative limit on lesson content.



## Language by student level (English-first, comprehensibility-driven)
Use ENGLISH wherever the student can actually follow it — i.e. wherever the text can be generated primarily with language the student has ALREADY been taught, or sits at a linguistic level generally below the level of the  lesson that contains it. This applies to section headlines, explanatory text, warnings, tips, block headers and column headers alike.
