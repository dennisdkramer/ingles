CAPABILITY MAP TOOL REFERENCE

The tools a lesson may use — what the course can teach with, what each one is for, what it may not be used to claim, and the markup that draws it.

What we can teach with

This page is the reference a lesson is written against. It has three parts: the way a page is written, the tools a page may use and how to choose between them, and a working example of every one of them.

Read it in this order. §1 governs the sentences, and it comes first because prose is in every block · §2 holds the four statements every tool is described by, the tests a use must pass, the words the figures own, and the table that maps a concept to the tools that show it · §3 is the examples — a figure, a layout, a look, a state, a motion, each rendered with the markup beside it · §4 is what is specified and not built · §5 the decisions already taken · §6 the concepts no tool draws.

Status key. built — in the stylesheet and rendering on this page · built, unused — renders, but no lesson uses it yet · documented — a specification only, no code behind it · superseded — keep one of the pair, prune the other · gap — the lesson must teach it in prose.

1. Prose — how a page is written



Prose is the one tool with no markup, and every block uses it: it states the rule a figure cannot draw, puts
two wordings side by side, and names the difference Portuguese hides. It is first on this page because it fails
more quietly than a figure — a wrong sentence still reads like teaching. Four statements describe it, the same
four §2 asks of every other tool:



    

  • What it is for. The rule no figure can draw, and the student's question answered in words. If a
figure on the page answers it, the prose does not say it again.
    • 

  • What it assumes. Every term is the student's own word or is defined here. A word a figure owns — the
list in §2 — is used only where that figure is drawn.
    • 

  • What it must not claim. Anything the wording does not do: a metaphor standing where a fact belongs,
two forms presented as one, two forms claimed to behave alike without naming the shared behaviour, a promise
about the lesson, or an only / never / always that is not true.
    • 

  • Whether a repeat earns its place. One rule per paragraph, with an example. A second paragraph on the
same rule adds a case, not a restatement.
    • 




A form is the exact wording. wrote, have written, has been writing are three
forms. Two forms side by side is the normal way to teach a difference, and saying what each one does that the
other does not is the point of the paragraph. What is forbidden is presenting two forms as one thing, or
claiming two behave alike without naming what they share, in the same sentence.



Nothing is coined. A word this page does not define is not given a meaning in a lesson. If something
needs a name, the ordinary word for it is used, or the thing is said in full.

difference can be seen.



2. Choosing a tool



What every tool is described by



A tool is not markup and a picture. It is an instrument, and four statements decide whether using it teaches
or decorates.



    

  • What it is for. The question it answers, in the student's words — is this still true now?, which word carries which job?, did the voice rise? A figure chosen by name instead of by question is chosen by taste.
    • 

  • What it assumes. Two separate things: the status of the concept in the student's head, and the conventions of reading the figure. The first is introduced (the page must supply the way to read the figure), known (the figure can work as a reminder), or retrievable on demand (the figure is only worth showing to contrast or to test). The second is never in the student and never in the curriculum: what a span means, what the mark for now means, that a dissolving left end means "lately". If the page does not supply it, the figure is not advanced — it is unreadable. Portuguese is often the efficient place to supply it.
    • 

  • What it must not be used to claim. Every figure asserts something. An axis of time asserts that time is a line with a present on it; it says nothing about how sure the speaker is, how often it happens, or whether the form is polite. A positions figure asserts that a transformation is available — which is false in most of the places it gets borrowed for. Used where its claim is false, a figure does not merely fail to teach: it installs an error.
    • 

  • Whether a repeat earns its place. Not a number. Two tests: does this appearance tell the student something the sentence does not already say, and is a second appearance answered or is it the same display again? Repetition inside an exercise is how a student transfers; repetition of a picture is decoration.
    • 




The tests a use must pass



    

  1. It carries information the sentence does not already state.
    2. 

  2. If it repeats, the repeat is answered rather than re-displayed.
    3. 

  3. If it were removed, the student would lose the answer to the concept's question.
    4. 

  4. The page still has one thing that is obviously the thing to look at.
    5. 




No ceilings, by design. A lesson may justifiably use one figure twenty times — a present-perfect lesson needs the axis on every example — and another may be wrong to use it once. The tests constrain the justification, not the quantity. What they forbid is the two failure modes:



    

  • Saturation — one figure repeated until it stops carrying information. Underline every line and the underline becomes typography.
    • 

  • Monotony — different content delivered through one shape. Every section a chart, every block a card, every lesson opening the same way. The variety rule is per page, not per figure.
    • 




Cost is a demand, not a price. What a figure asks of the page is attention, an explanation the reader must already have, space, and sometimes a media file. Its size, skin and placement are a construction choice the sheet already limits — not a decision to re-take per lesson.

3. The elements — examples



Every tool below is real: it renders on this page, and the markup that produced it is beside it. Each entry
says what it shows before it shows it, and names the case it is an example of — a figure carries a
concept, a layout sets how many blocks share a row and why, a look is the block's paint for one
job, a state is how an activity reports itself, and a motion is a reveal the student controls.



These are examples, not required elements. A lesson is free to compose differently, and what it
copies is the markup and the reason, never the arrangement for its own sake.



3a. Figures — what carries a concept



The figures are the instruments §2's table points at. Each one is here because it can show an answer a
sentence alone cannot, and each entry opens by saying what it shows, so this part reads without the pictures.

Where now sits — the line of time


One rule under the word that carries the time. Now is a star with its word above it, the past fades out behind on a log curve, and the future runs ahead dashed to a double arrow that steps and fades. It puts the words and now on one line and nothing else: it does not draw where a claim stops, so the claim itself stays in the sentence.

Eight figures, one family, each named above. The line is 300px by default and never narrower: measured from the left end of the faded rule to the tip of the double arrow. data-grow="1" fills its host; a single-line phrase sets the width, and a short phrase then simply centres under a 300px line. Three points sit on it — before, its centre 50px in from the left end; now, at the exact midpoint; then, 50px in from the right end — and one word of each verb group pins the line: .an names the word, and the tense says which point must land under that word's centre. Pinning moves the line, never the sentence, so a pin near the start of a phrase can push the line past its block's content edge; that is the known limit of drawing a figure inline. The phrase and the anchor word carry no underline of the timeline's own — only the part-of-speech marks a word already wears.

Words in bulk — the chart that presents


A chart holds parallel items still while the student compares them. That is its job: comparison, or a vocabulary set too large to carry in one sentence. It is not a recap of prose, and a page of charts is a page with one shape.


Three jobs follow, and they are not interchangeable: this one presents a set, the next pairs a word with the one fact that makes it usable, and the third divides one family in two.


What may be written above the rows. A header is worth its space where a column's values would be ambiguous without one. Where no column needs a name, the row can be merged into one cell and collapsed to a 3px rule (<tr class="bare">), so the chart keeps its top edge and says nothing.


Where a label covers more than one column it can be written above the rows as plain text, or it can run down the margin outside the chart as vertical text beside the rows it covers (<span class="vlab">, one per group). Neither is required — a chart whose contents say what it is needs neither. Headers are written the way a book title is written; the sheet applies no case of its own, so what is written is what prints. A chart that needs an explainer gets the explainer as its own element beside it rather than as a third column, and a thin chart can sit outside a frame — a frame whose only content is the chart has its heading setting the width and the rest of it empty.

A word and its partner


Two columns and nothing else: the word, and the word it is paired with. The pairing is the whole lesson — the opposite, the homophone, or the translation that traps a Portuguese speaker. The margin label says only which pairing a row is doing.


Everything that would have taken a third column is written here instead: emprestar covers both directions, cheap also judges quality where barato does not, and push back is to resist rather than to shove.


An index that sits beside an explainer or an activity, not a set of its own. Add a third column and it has stopped being an index and become the bulk chart above.











The label is the teaching


The margin label carries one word — which family the rows beside it belong to — and the rule is written here, where it can be a sentence: Regular adds -ed and the base never moves, so the rows are only evidence that the rule holds; Irregular has a form of its own for each verb, so each has to be learned one at a time.


The same margin label splits any family a lesson must separate — two conjugations, two registers, the words that follow the rule from the words that do not. A chart with no family to separate does not get one.

the span each block needs from what the block holds. The examples below are results at those spans.

3c. Looks — the paint for one job



A look is declared, never inferred: the builder names it on the block. It changes the paint only — the
markup, the content and the control stay the same — so a block can be re-skinned without being rewritten.
Six are defined and one is the default.





Default


Paper and a 2px accent rule on the top edge, in the track's hue. Any block that has no job beyond being a
block.


Tint


Context or register: the surface takes the register's hue, and the block is about how it sounds in the
room rather than what it means.







Tape


A thing that would sit on paper — a schedule, a reminder, an aside, where a word came from.


Float


No frame at all: a section's opening or closing line, or the higher-level view set beside the block it
frames.







Stripes


An activity or a question block. The stripes say the student does something here, and the block reports
itself when every answer is in (§3d).








Outline


The one critical element in a section: a thick accent edge and a deep shadow. If two blocks wear it, the
page no longer has one thing that is obviously the thing to look at.







Dark


Night, or fun. A scene at night, a section that is deliberately a change of light. The native controls
follow, because the block sets color-scheme: dark.








Tint, in a sentence


Kept. “Cooked is what you say to a friend. To a teacher, exhausted — the same fact,
and nobody wonders whether you are joking.”


This is the block tint exists for: the content is register, so its surface is the register's
colour. The hue is the track's — no per-block colour.

What is absent, and stays absent. No parallax, no infinite animation, no cursor effect. Every motion

5. Decisions recorded



    

  • The player. The mini player is the capability. The full audio block is a composition of the mini player with a lyrics column and one paragraph — not a second component, and not something the author has to choose between.
    • 

  • Time. The line of time on this page is the one to use — “axis new”, the now-centred rule under the word. The older axis-with-marks and the draft plot of claim spans are superseded and are prune candidates: one question, three geometries.
    • 

  • Intonation. Neither the contour nor the inline marks are in use. Build or prune, decided before either is offered to a lesson.
    • 

  • The underline is the exception among figures. Its contract is a script that reads a sentence and prints the spans, so the markup is generated, never hand-typed. An inline timeline is generated the same way, from the same call.
    • 

  • A look is declared, never inferred. The builder names it on the block. Six are defined — tint for context and register, tape for what would sit on paper, float for a section's opening or closing and for the higher-level view, stripes for activities and questions, outline for the one critical element, dark for night and play — and a block that names none is the default. The hue is the track's; no block sets its own colour.
    • 

  • Width follows content. A frame is as wide as what it holds, never narrower than a quarter of the content area, and never widened by a heading or a tag. The span a block occupies is measured and written by qa/span.mjs, not chosen; two or three blocks share a row when the reader has to compare them, and the number of neighbours is whatever the comparison needs.
    • 

  • Reading mode is dropped. It narrows the page for one reader, and a teacher and a student looking at the same lesson must see the same page. Propagating the switch from the student's device to the teacher's would be a mechanism that can fail mid-lesson, for a feature a phone's own viewport already provides.
    • 

  • A viewport-triggered reveal is dropped. Motion here is what the student controls or what an activity reports; a scroll-triggered arrival replays itself every time the page is re-read.
    • 

  • The section score is the last thing on the page. The score-reactive bar sits above the last / completed / next controls, so the result and the way on are met in one place.
    • 

  • One reference, not two. The separate elements page has been retired: everything it demonstrated is an example in §3, so the page a lesson reads and the page a person reads are the same page.
    • 




6. Gaps



Concepts the course owes and cannot draw: how sure the speaker is (modal strength), how often something happens, and register — what is too rude, too stiff or too casual for the room. Those lessons carry the work in prose and in the choice of examples, and a figure invented for them would be a diagram of taste.
