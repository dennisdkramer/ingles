CAPABILITY MAP TOOL REFERENCE

What a page may be built with — the prose, the figures, and a block of each in use.

What we can teach with

A page is content first. These are the tools that content can take, each shown in the block it is used in.

How a page gets built

Four moves, kept apart: what the page teaches, how that is arranged, how the wording sits once it is arranged, and which paint it wears. A writer deciding what to teach should not be deciding how wide a block is in the same breath, and an arrangement is easier to judge on a page whose words are already there.

Write the content

Sections and blocks in teaching order, with nothing yet decided about layout: a section is one concept, a block is one idea inside it carrying its own heading, and the words are already the words the student reads.

Arrange it

Every block is given a span by looking at it: what it holds, how much text it carries, and what it sits beside. The examples on this page are results at those spans; what a page copies is the reason.

Clean up the wording

Read every line as it will break. A line that ends in one to three words is rewritten until the block's lines are even; a short block beside a tall one gains the sentence it was missing, and a last-row stub is deleted rather than padded. Only the words change, never what they say.

Choose the look

A look is declared on the block, never inferred from what it holds. It changes the paint only — markup, content and control stay the same — so a block can be re-skinned without being rewritten.

How a page is written

Prose is the one tool with no markup, and every block uses it: it states the rule a figure cannot draw, puts two wordings side by side, and names the difference Portuguese hides. It comes before the tools because it fails more quietly — a wrong sentence still reads like teaching.

What prose is held to

What it is for

The rule no figure can draw, and the student's question answered in words. If a figure on the page answers it, the prose does not say it again.

What it assumes

Every term is the student's own word or is defined here. A word a figure owns — the list in §3 — is used only where that figure is drawn.

What it must not claim

Anything the wording does not do: a metaphor standing where a fact belongs, two forms presented as one, two forms claimed to behave alike without naming the shared behaviour, a promise about the lesson, or an only / never / always that is not true.

Whether a repeat earns its place

One rule per paragraph, with an example. A second paragraph on the same rule adds a case, not a restatement.

A form is the exact wording. wrote, have written, has been writing are three forms. Two forms side by side is the normal way to teach a difference, and saying what each one does that the other does not is the point of the paragraph. What is forbidden is presenting two forms as one thing, or claiming two behave alike without naming what they share, in the same sentence.

Nothing is coined. A word this page does not define is not given a meaning in a lesson. If something needs a name, the ordinary word for it is used, or the thing is said in full.

Two pairs, and why one of each was cut

Rejected. “Portuguese puts the whole claim inside the verb's ending: escrevi, tenho escrito. English splits it into two words that never touch, and one of them is a word you already know from do and will.”

Two forms presented as one · claim with no axis on the page · splits and never touch are figures of speech where a fact belongs · do and will share no behaviour with have.

Kept. “Escrevi is past simple: one finished event. Tenho escrito is present perfect: the writing is still true now. English keeps that difference — wrote / have written.”

Each wording is named with what it does, the difference is stated rather than implied, and the Portuguese is a fact about Portuguese.

Rejected. “has been + -ing is the same machine with a longer tail.”

Machine is a term this page does not define, and the sentence talks about the lesson instead of the English.

Kept. “He's been playing all week is he has been playing: the playing started before this week and has not stopped. The same words with he is — he's playing — say only that it is happening now.”

Both readings are shown, the deciding word is named, and nothing has to be learned before the difference can be seen.

Two words for one fact

Kept. “Cooked is what you say to a friend. To a teacher, exhausted — the same fact, and nobody wonders whether you are joking.”

Nothing else on the page wears this colour. A second tint would make the first one a decoration, and a block that is merely about a topic is not a block about the room it is said in.

Why register is stated

The same fact has a version for a friend and a version for a teacher, and the difference is the choice of word. A lesson teaches register by putting the two side by side and naming the room each belongs in; a tint is that room's colour on the page, and it is the only thing the look is for.

Choosing a tool

A tool is not markup and a picture. It is an instrument, and four statements decide whether using it teaches or decorates. A figure chosen by name instead of by question is chosen by taste.

What every tool is described by

What it is for

The question it answers, in the student's words — is this still true now?, which word carries which job?, did the voice rise?

What it assumes

Two things: the concept's status in the student's head, and the conventions of reading the figure. The first is introduced, known, or retrievable on demand. The second is never in the student: what a span means, what the mark for now means, that a dissolving left end means “lately”. If the page does not supply it, the figure is not advanced — it is unreadable, and Portuguese is often the efficient place to supply it.

What it must not be used to claim

Every figure asserts something. An axis of time asserts that time is a line with a present on it; it says nothing about how sure the speaker is, how often it happens, or whether the form is polite. Used where its claim is false, a figure does not merely fail to teach: it installs an error.

Whether a repeat earns its place

Not a number. Two tests: does this appearance tell the student something the sentence does not already say, and is a second appearance answered rather than the same display again? Repetition inside an exercise is how a student transfers; repetition of a picture is decoration.

The tests a use must pass

  1. It carries information the sentence does not already state.
  2. If it repeats, the repeat is answered rather than re-displayed.
  3. If it were removed, the student would lose the answer to the concept's question.
  4. The page still has one thing that is obviously the thing to look at.

No ceilings, by design. A lesson may justifiably use one figure twenty times — a present-perfect lesson needs the axis on every example — and another may be wrong to use it once. The tests constrain the justification, not the quantity.

  • Saturation — one figure repeated until it stops carrying information. Underline every line and the underline becomes typography.
  • Monotony — different content delivered through one shape: every section a chart, every block a card, every lesson opening the same way. The variety rule is per page, not per figure.

The tests are applied per use, not per figure: the same axis can pass in one block and fail in the next, because the failure belongs to the sentence it was asked to carry. A figure that passes all four is doing work; one that passes three is decoration with a rationale.

Cost is a demand

What a figure asks of the page is attention, an explanation the reader must already have, space, and sometimes a media file. Its size, skin and placement are a construction choice the sheet already limits, not a decision to re-take per lesson.

The cost is paid where the figure is drawn: a mark the student cannot name is a tax on the sentence, and an axis with no “now” labelled is a riddle in a lesson that had no time for one.

A figure also costs the page its one obvious thing to look at. Two marks doing the same job on one screen is the same figure bought twice, and the cheapest figure is the one the sentence already carries — a word, a line, a fence — because nothing has to be laid out around it.

One concept, one row

The mapping below is the point of this page: one row per concept the course owes, and a third column naming the figures that can show that answer, so a lesson's own plan can produce its candidates instead of relying on the author remembering what exists.

A row is written once and reused: the plan starts from its concepts, finds the rows, and takes the candidates the third column names. The same concept cannot be taught twice with different figures in two lessons, because there is only one place to look.

The status column is not decoration. Built means in the stylesheet and rendering in §4; documented means a specification with no code behind it, which a lesson may not use; gap means no figure exists, and the lesson carries the concept in prose.

A blank third column is a decision, not an omission: it says the concept is owed and cannot be drawn yet. Deleting the row would lose that.

Where two figures can show the same concept the row names both, and the lesson keeps one per section: two figures answering one question fails the saturation test before the section is written.

A row is added when a concept is planned, and its status changes when a figure is built or pruned. The table is the course's schema, not a summary of this page.

A listening item, not a page

The player is the small one: a single control row inside any block, so a listening item is a sentence plus a play button rather than a page of its own. The native controls are the floor — with no script the student still plays, pauses and scrubs — and the script only adds the drawn bar, the clock and click-a-line-to-seek for a block that carries lyrics.

A clip is cut from the file in assets/, never fetched from elsewhere: a page that shows a recording works offline and inside a printed copy.

Dialogs and activities

A dialog is the default shape for speech, and the difference is whether the student writes in it. A plain dialog is read; a dialog with blanks is worked, and a worked one carries its word list or its instruction in the same block, or the student cannot know what belongs in the gap.

Decisions recorded

What is settled, and what is dropped

What is settled

  • The player. The mini player is the capability. The full audio block is a composition of the mini player with a lyrics column and one paragraph — not a second component, and not something the author has to choose between.
  • Time. The now-centred axis is the line of time to use. The older timeline figure and the draft plot of claim spans are superseded and are prune candidates: one question, three geometries.
  • Intonation. Neither the contour nor the inline marks are in use. Build or prune, decided before either is offered to a lesson.
  • The underline is the exception among figures. Its contract is a script that reads a sentence and prints the spans, so the markup is generated, never hand-typed. An inline axis is generated the same way, from the same call.
  • A look is declared, never inferred. The hue is the track's; no block sets its own colour.
  • The span is chosen, not measured. The number is written on the block, and DOM order stays the order on the page.
  • The section score is the last thing on the page. The score-reactive bar sits above the last / completed / next controls, so the result and the way on are met in one place.
  • One reference, not two. Everything a retired elements page demonstrated is a block in §4–§7, so the page a lesson reads and the page a person reads are the same page.

What is dropped

  • Reading mode. It narrows the page for one reader, and a teacher and a student looking at the same lesson must see the same page. Propagating the switch from the student's device to the teacher's would be a mechanism that can fail mid-lesson, for a feature a phone's own viewport already provides.
  • A viewport-triggered reveal. Motion here is what the student controls or what an activity reports; a scroll-triggered arrival replays itself every time the page is re-read.
  • Hand-lettered block headings. The section resets the counter and each block's heading takes the next letter; a heading that writes its own letter shows it twice.
  • A second frame inside one block. A block is the frame; a panel inside it is a card that says the block has two subjects.

Gaps

Concepts no tool draws

Concepts the course owes and cannot draw: how sure the speaker is (modal strength), how often something happens, and register — what is too rude, too stiff or too casual for the room. Those lessons carry the work in prose and in the choice of examples, and a figure invented for them would be a diagram of taste.

This page is the tool reference a lesson is written against. It is unlinked from the site index, holds no answer, saves nothing, and loads only the player. Add a row when a concept is planned; add a block when a figure is built; delete a row when it is pruned.
