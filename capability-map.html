What we can teach with

The tools a lesson is built from, each drawn in a block that uses it.

Every word inside a block below is example content. What a block contributes is its shape: the markup in the reveal under it, its look, its span.

The reveals are open and they are the contract. Section 3 is the index: one concept, the tool that answers it, a link to it drawn.

How a page gets built

Three moves, in this order: what the page teaches, how it is arranged, how the wording is cleaned up.

Write the content

One section per concept, one block per idea, each block with its own heading, in teaching order. No layout decision yet.

Arrange it, and give it a look

The page is twelve columns and every block is full width, so a page that decides nothing flows straight down. A span is written only when something must sit beside the block: the row is split into twelfths, each block takes 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 or 12, and the numbers in a row add to 12. The span is a fit, not a category — the same prose is 6 beside a chart and 12 alone — so it follows what the block holds and what must sit beside it. Nothing is measured or packed in the browser.

The look is settled in the same pass: declared on the block, never inferred from what it holds, and it changes the paint only. One tint and one outline per section at most. A look never varies the page for its own sake, marks importance, or dresses a teaching block as an activity.

Markup — a page that flows, and the one row that does not
<h2>a section the sheet numbers</h2>

<div class="block">…full width: nothing sits beside it…</div>

<div class="block" data-span="8">…the sentence…</div>
<div class="block" data-span="4" data-look="tint">…the note beside it…</div>

No span until something must sit beside the block, then the two add to 12. Looks: tint, tape, float, stripes, outline, dark. A section is an <h2>: the sheet numbers it and restarts the lettering under it, so neither is typed.

Clean up the wording

Read every line as it will break: rewrite a line that ends in one to three words, give a short block beside a tall one the sentence it is missing, fold a last line into the one above, delete a last-row stub rather than pad it. Only the words change.

Concepts no tool draws

No figure for these: how sure the speaker is, how often it happens, register — what the room allows. Those lessons carry it in prose and in the choice of examples.

Add a row here when a tool is built, a block when a figure is built, and delete the row when it is pruned. This page is unlinked from the index, holds no answer, saves nothing, and loads only the player.

How a page is written

Prose is a tool with no markup and every block uses it: it states the rule a figure cannot draw. It comes before the figures because it fails quietly.

What prose is held to

What it is for

The rule no figure can draw, and the student's question answered in words — never the sentence a figure on the page already shows.

What it assumes

Every term is the student's own word or is defined here. A word a figure owns (§3) is used only where that figure is drawn.

What it must not claim

Anything the wording does not do: a metaphor where a fact belongs, two forms presented as one, two forms claimed to behave alike without naming what they share, a promise about the lesson, a false only / never / always.

Whether a repeat earns its place

One rule per paragraph, with an example. A second paragraph on the same rule adds a case.

A form is the exact wording. wrote, have written, has been writing are three forms. Two side by side is how a difference is taught, and saying what each does that the other does not is the paragraph's job.

Nothing is coined. A word this page does not define is not given a meaning in a lesson.

Two pairs, and why one of each was cut

Rejected. “Portuguese puts the whole claim inside the verb's ending: escrevi, tenho escrito. English splits it into two words that never touch, and one of them is a word you already know from do and will.”

Two forms presented as one · claim with no timeline on the page · splits and never touch are figures of speech where a fact belongs · do and will share no behaviour with have.

Kept. “Escrevi is past simple: one finished event. Tenho escrito is present perfect: the writing is still true now. English keeps that difference — wrote / have written.”

Each form is named with what it does, the difference is stated, and the Portuguese is a fact about Portuguese.

Rejected. “has been + -ing is the same machine with a longer tail.”

Machine is a term this page does not define, and the sentence talks about the lesson instead of the English.

Kept. “He's been playing all week is he has been playing: the playing started before this week and has not stopped. The same words with he is — he's playing — say only that it is happening now.”

Both forms are shown, the deciding word is named, and nothing must be learned first.

Register, and the one tint

Cooked is what you say to a friend, exhausted to a teacher: the same fact, and the choice of word is the register. Teach it by putting the two side by side and naming the room each belongs to.

The tint is that room's colour on the page, and the only thing the look is for. One tint per section: a second makes the first a decoration.

Choosing a tool

A tool is chosen by the question it answers, never by its name. Four statements fix whether using it teaches or decorates.

What every tool is described by

What it is for

The question it answers, in the student's words — is this still true now?, which word carries which job?

What it assumes

Whether the concept is new, known or retrievable, and every convention the figure needs: what a span means, what the mark for now means, that a dissolving left end means “lately”. The page supplies them or the figure is unreadable — Portuguese is often the efficient place.

What it must not be used to claim

Every figure asserts something: a timeline asserts that time is a line with a present on it, and says nothing about how sure the speaker is or how often it happens. Used where its claim is false, it installs an error.

Whether a repeat earns its place

Two tests: does this appearance say something the sentence does not, and is a second appearance answered rather than the same display again? Repetition inside an exercise transfers; a repeated picture is decoration.

The tests a use must pass

  1. It carries information the sentence does not already state.
  2. If it repeats, the repeat is answered rather than re-displayed.
  3. If it were removed, the student would lose the answer to the concept's question.
  4. The page still has one thing that is obviously the thing to look at.

No ceilings: one figure may justifiably appear twenty times in a lesson and be wrong once in another. The tests constrain the justification, not the quantity, and are applied per use — the same timeline can pass in one block and fail in the next, because the failure belongs to the sentence it was asked to carry.

Two failures wear a rationale. Saturation: one figure repeated until it carries nothing — underline every line and the underline becomes typography. Monotony: different content through one shape, every section a chart. The variety rule is per page.

Cost is a demand

What a figure asks of the page: attention, a convention the reader must already have, space, and sometimes a media file. It is paid where the figure is drawn — a mark the student cannot name is a tax on the sentence.

A figure also costs the page its one obvious thing to look at. Two marks doing the same job is the same figure bought twice, and the cheapest figure is the one the sentence already carries.

One concept, one row

One row per concept: start from the concept, take the tool in the second column, follow the link to it drawn. A concept with no tool has no row — §1 D names them.

One concept never names two tools in one lesson: two figures answering one question fail the saturation test before the section is written. A row is added when a tool is built and changed when a tool is pruned.

In-Block Features

Everything in this section goes inside a block: the underline, the horizontal timeline, the vertical timeline, the numbered list, the dialog, the charts, the bar, the gloss, the key, the player, the map, the strip and the flash card.

None of them is used without accompaniment. Each is half a row at most, and each is read by something — the sentence that explains it, a word list, the questions the student answers — in the cell beside it or in the block next to it. The same holds when the element is the block's own subject. That rule is the same for every element here and is not repeated under the examples.

Not one of them belongs to a particular block. Each goes wherever a lesson needs it: inside a teaching block, inside an activity block, or alone as the block's own subject at whatever span it needs. An activity about word order uses the underline and the numbered list; an activity about a series of events uses the vertical timeline; a comparison the student answers row by row uses the chart. Nothing about the element changes when the block around it becomes an activity, and the same markup moves between the two.

The chart — parallel items

A chart sets parallel items side by side while the student compares them: comparison, or a vocabulary set too large for one sentence. It is not a recap of prose, and a page of charts is a page with one shape.

Below three items a chart is a sentence with rules around it. A header earns its space only where a column would be ambiguous without one; where none needs a name the row collapses to a rule. A chart that stands alone takes a figcaption, one inside a block takes the h4 above it.

The split chart — a label per family

th.vlab carries the family name, which side the rows beside it belong to, and the rule that separates the families is written here: Regular adds -ed and the base never moves, so the rows are evidence the rule holds; Irregular has a form of its own for each verb, so each is learned alone.

The same label splits any family a lesson must separate — two conjugations, two registers, the words that follow the rule from the words that do not. A chart with no family to separate gets none.

Anything that would have taken a third column is written here instead. Add the column and it stops being a label and becomes the bulk chart beside it.
