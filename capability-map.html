What a page may be built with — the prose, the visuals, and a block of each in use.

What we can teach with

The visuals a lesson is built from, each drawn on this page.

Every word inside a block below is example content. What a block contributes is its shape: the markup in the reveal under it, its look, its span.

How a page gets built

Arrange it, and give it a look

A section's content goes into one .rows, and that is the default. A row is one line, and its direct children are its cells: each cell is one item, or two that stack inside it. Every cell starts at the width its content asks for, shares the rest of the line in proportion to what it holds and never drops below a quarter of the page, and text wraps inside whatever width it lands on; every cell is the line's height. Two cells a line by default, three or four when the items are thin, and a fifth item is a second .rows — a row never wraps. .cols is for interchangeable cells that must wrap, and .row is only the utility for a line of chips or buttons — never a layout row. A width is a repair: min-width to raise a floor, flex:0 0 calc(50% - 13px) to fix a cell at half the line, or data-span on a block that stands outside a row.

Every element below is drawn with the markup that builds it, and the drawing is the default: what the element contains, what text it carries, where that text sits, and whether it carries a title at all. Its item count is the example's own, never a count to copy. A chart takes no title of its own — the sentence beside it names it.

data-look is declared on the block, never inferred from what it holds, and changes the paint only.

Looks, and why each is chosen: float for text that applies to everything in the section and belongs to no single block · tint for content about the track's own voice — how a word taught in that voice compares with the same word in another voice, what it really means or the use cases a slang word has · outline for content important enough to stand out from the page · stripes for an activity the student answers, whose top rule turns red · tape for a note pinned to the page rather than taught in sequence · dark for content that is a night, a screen or a stage. One tint and one outline per section at most. A section is an <h2>: the sheet numbers it, so it is never typed, and it restarts the lettering under it. Choosing a visual

The mapping

The first column is the occasion, the second the visual that answers it, and every name opens it drawn. A concept with no row is one no visual shows yet. None of these items is mandatory or even preferred: a text-only explanation is equally valid if it accomplishes the teaching goal of its block. The lesson teaches; a visual is one of the ways its example is drawn. The order of the items here means nothing — it is not a ranking, a precedence or a sequence, and nothing is more important for coming first.

Activities

Training

One card per item the lesson has already taught — vocabulary, phrases, the words of an earlier section. The block shows the back of one card and the student turns cards until they find the one it belongs to: a turn tints that card red when it is not the answer and green when it is, any number of cards may be turned in a round, and only the right one turns them all back and brings the next prompt four seconds later. Nothing here is scored and no answer is saved, so it trains and never tests: when a lesson carries one it is the last activity before the quiz, never the quiz.

…

Marked activities with controls

An activity is a block the student writes in, and it carries its own instruction in the block. An activity is a unit the student completes — never a control on its own. The four controls — a gap, a radio, a checkbox, a written field — are the parts an activity is built from and are chosen by what the student must do; they are not activities themselves. A keyed gap is preferentially carried inside one of the forms below — a dialog turn, a chart row to answer, a strip under its panel, a line to translate, a sentence to correct, a line to write from what they hear — and a lone gap is only ever a component beside other teaching in a block, never an activity in its own right. The rows below hold the forms: numbered chunks, a chart whose cells are answered, and under the chunks a correction and a dictation — the last two for no beginner, and the sentence they ask for always tuned to the lesson's band. The two blocks after this one, side by side, are four options to pick one from and a matching pool. The last block in the section is fill-in-the-blank: a line the student completes with words of their own.

data-answer keys a gap, | separating alternatives; --gap-w is the width the answer needs. A radio group's key is data-a on .qitem; engine2.js marks the right option and writes .ans beside a typed field.

Each of these can stand as a block of its own or sit inside a block with the prose, the chart or the drawing it accompanies; every row below is the in-block size.

Games

Don’t add a game just to have one. A game is a skin over an activity the lesson already wanted: the activity is chosen first and the game is how it is presented, so a reskinned game is that element in the ledger and never an element of its own. Nothing counts a game and nothing requires one. A game is a flavour element: it is used occasionally, and only where it fits a particular activity well enough that the activity would be worse without it. A set of items that trains fine as a list stays a list.

A .game draws its own edge, so it stands at page level and is never put inside a .block. Every game reads the same content — .game-list > .qitem, each with a .qstem and radio options, data-a marking the right one — so that list is what prints and what the student answers if the script never runs; data-game chooses only the presentation, never the content. A game in a quiz section carries data-retry="0"; a training game carries nothing.
