CAPABILITY MAP VISUAL REFERENCE

What a page may be built with — the prose, the visuals, and a block of each in use.

What we can teach with

The visuals a lesson is built from, each drawn on this page.

Every word inside a block below is example content. What a block contributes is its shape: the markup in the reveal under it, its look, its span.

The reveals are open and they are the contract. Section 3 is the index: one concept, the visual that answers it, and a link to it drawn.

How a page gets built

Write the content

Write the words first — the headings and the sentences the student reads — and decide nothing about layout yet.

Arrange it, and give it a look

The page is divided into twelfths, so a block can take 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 or 12 of them — a quarter, a third, a half, two thirds, three quarters or the whole line. A block with no span takes the whole line. A span is written only when something must sit beside it, and the spans in a row add to 12; which one it gets is decided by what the block holds, never by what kind of block it is. Nothing is measured in the browser.

The look is settled in the same pass: declared on the block, never inferred from what it holds, and it changes the paint only. One tint and one outline per section at most. A look never varies the page for its own sake, marks importance, or dresses a teaching block as an activity.

Markup — a block on its own, and one with a neighbour
<h2>a section the sheet numbers</h2>

<div class="block">…the whole line: nothing sits beside it…</div>

<div class="block" data-span="8">…the sentence…</div>
<div class="block" data-span="4" data-look="tint">…the note beside it…</div>

No span until something must sit beside the block, then the two add to 12. Looks: tint, tape, float, stripes, outline, dark. A section is an <h2>: the sheet numbers it and restarts the lettering under it, so neither is typed.

Clean up the wording

Prose can be made shorter to remove a wrapped stub at the end of a line, or longer to fill one, as long as the lesson does not get worse for it. A block whose content is shorter than the blocks beside it in a row may gain an example or a question to even the heights.

How a page is written

Prose is not a visual: it has no markup, every block uses it, and it states the rule a visual cannot draw.

What prose is held to

What it is for

The rule no visual can draw, and the student's question answered in words — never the sentence a visual on the page already shows.

What it assumes

Every term is the student's own word or is defined here. A visual's own word (§3) is used only where that visual is drawn.

What it must not do

A metaphor where a fact belongs, two tenses presented as one, two tenses claimed to behave alike without naming what they share, a promise about the lesson, a false only / never / always. Nothing is coined either: a word this page does not define is not given a meaning in a lesson.

Whether a repeat earns its place

One rule per paragraph, with an example. A second paragraph on the same rule adds a case.

Two pairs, and why one of each was cut

Rejected. “Portuguese puts the whole claim inside the verb's ending: escrevi, tenho escrito. English splits it into two words that never touch, and one of them is a word you already know from do and will.”

Two tenses presented as one · claim with no timeline on the page · splits and never touch are figures of speech where a fact belongs · do and will share no behaviour with have.

Kept. “Escrevi is past simple: one finished event. Tenho escrito is present perfect: the writing is still true now. English keeps that difference — wrote / have written.”

Each tense is named with what it does, the difference is stated, and the Portuguese is a fact about Portuguese.

Rejected. “has been + -ing is the same machine with a longer tail.”

Machine is a term this page does not define, and the sentence talks about the lesson instead of the English.

Kept. “He's been playing all week is he has been playing: the playing started before this week and has not stopped. The same words with he is — he's playing — say only that it is happening now.”

Both are shown, the deciding word is named, and nothing must be learned first.

Markup — parallel items inside one block
<div class="cols" style="--cw:420px">
  <div><h4>first</h4><p>…</p></div>
  <div><h4>second</h4><p>…</p></div>
</div>

Two to four equal items side by side inside one block. --cw is the width at which they wrap, not a column count.

Register, and the one tint

Cooked is what you say to a friend, exhausted to a teacher: the same fact, and the choice of word is the register. Teach it by putting the two side by side and naming the room each belongs to.

The tint is that room's colour on the page, and the only thing the look is for. One tint per section: a second makes the first a decoration.

Choosing a visual

A visual is chosen by the question it answers, never by its name. These four statements decide whether using it teaches or decorates.

What every visual is described by

What it is for

The one question it answers, in the student's words.

What it assumes

Whether the concept is new, and every convention needed to read the drawing. The page supplies them or the visual is unreadable.

What it claims

One thing only. Used where that claim is false, it installs an error.

Whether a repeat earns its place

Two tests: it says something the sentence does not, and a repeat is answered rather than re-displayed.

The tests a use must pass

  1. It carries information the sentence does not already state.
  2. If it repeats, the repeat is answered rather than re-displayed.
  3. If it were removed, the student would lose the answer to the concept's question.
  4. The page still has one thing that is obviously the thing to look at.

No ceilings: one visual may appear twenty times in a lesson and be wrong once in another. The failures are saturation (one visual repeated until it carries nothing) and monotony (every section through one shape).

Cost is a demand

Every visual costs attention, space and a convention the reader must already have — sometimes a media file, sometimes a script. The cheapest visual is the one the sentence already carries.

In-Block Features

The underline, the timeline, the vertical timeline, the numbered list, the dialog, the charts, the bar, the gloss, the key, the player, the map, the strip and the flash card can each go inside a block — alone, with other elements, or beside the prose that reads them — and none of them has to. A chart, a dialog or a timeline can be a block of its own, and any of them may take more of a row when the content needs it. The examples here are the smaller in-block size.

None of them is used without accompaniment. Each is read by something — the sentence that explains it, a word list, the questions the student answers — in the same block or in the block next to it, and that holds when the element is the block's own subject. Nothing about an element changes when the block around it becomes an activity: an activity about word order uses the underline and the numbered list, and the same markup moves between the two.

The two lines are the same sentence twice: the first with the marks, the second with pos-labels, which prints the role instead of the line — N · V · M · DO · IO · ADJ · ADV · PREP — for a page or a printout that must not rely on colour. The tagger emits the marks, so they cannot drift from the sentence and no word is marked by hand. The marks go on any sentence the lesson wants marked, as many as it wants: they wrap one sentence and are not a component with a block of its own — three sentences in a block means three of these. The fence goes on the joining word; data-rel is one of add · contrast · alternative · cause · condition · time · place · comparison · content, and under pos-labels the relation prints instead of the fence. The recipient carries no line: pos-i draws an arrow from the side the object sits on, its shaft drawing itself and its head arriving after, and data-side="left" mirrors it for a recipient that follows the object — the two orders of the same verb, each with its own arrow. Under pos-labels it prints IO and the arrow is not drawn.

The chart — parallel items

A chart sets parallel items side by side while the student compares them: comparison, or a vocabulary set too large for one sentence. It is not a recap of prose, and a page of charts is a page with one shape.

Below three items a chart is a sentence with rules around it. A header earns its space only where a column would be ambiguous without one; where none needs a name the row collapses to a rule. A chart that stands alone takes a figcaption, one inside a block takes the h4 above it.

The split chart — a label per family

th.vlab carries the family name, which side the rows beside it belong to, and the rule that separates the families is written here: Regular adds -ed and the base never moves, so the rows are evidence the rule holds; Irregular has a form of its own for each verb, so each is learned alone.

The same label splits any family a lesson must separate — two conjugations, two registers, the words that follow the rule from the words that do not. A chart with no family to separate gets none.

Anything that would have taken a third column is written here instead. Add the column and it stops being a label and becomes the bulk chart beside it.

Dialogs and activities

An activity is a block the student writes in. The dialog with blanks is the worked version of the dialog in §4, and it carries its instruction in the same block or the gap has no single answer.

The dialog, worked

Fill each gap with have, has or had. Without the instruction the gap has more than one right answer.

  1. AnaDid you post the letter?
  2. BetoThree times. I already posted it.

The control sits inside the turn and the block does not change shape to allow it. data-answer is the key, alternatives separated by |; --gap-w is the width the answer needs.
