What we can teach with

The visuals a lesson is built from, each drawn on this page.

Every word inside a block below is example content. What a block contributes is its shape: the markup in the reveal under it, its look, its span.

The reveals are open and they are the contract. Section 3 is the index: one concept, the visual that answers it, and a link to it drawn.

How a page gets built

Write the content

Write the words first — the headings and the sentences the student reads — and decide nothing about layout yet.

Two inputs govern this pass, and they outrank every other. The track's register and the lesson's level decide what kind of phrase appears on the page and how complex it is: the sentence type, how many clauses it carries, which tense machinery it dares to use, which words and which slang, how much a single turn can hold. The roadmap names the concept to be taught; the register and the level decide every word used to teach it, practise it and test it. A page that gets the concept right and the voice wrong has failed this pass, however clean its grammar coverage.

The register is the voice of the student and the voice of the teacher. The student's voice is what the page asks them to produce — the quoted lines, the answers, the messages they are told to write. The teacher's voice is everything the page says to them — headings, instructions, explanations, examples, corrections, jokes. They are one voice, the persona speaking to the persona. The page therefore never alternates between a lively student text and a neutral course voice explaining it, and a sentence that could have been written by any course, in any book, for any age, is not this track's sentence.

The level fixes the band, and the level fixes the phrases. It sets what the page may assume and what it must demand: how long an utterance the student handles, whether a clause carries one idea or three, whether an answer is a word or a sentence, whether the page is still explaining something the student already had. The prose that explains may be plainer than the material; the material itself — every quoted line, example, drill item and quiz item — is written at the band.

So the first decision of this pass is not the concept, it is the scene: take it from the register's own list, then let the level say how that scene talks. Write the concept into that voice and keep writing until no block on the page is register-neutral.

The voice never buys obscurity. The register decides how a thing is said; it never decides whether the student can tell what is being taught. Simplicity and clarity of the teaching point outrank the track's flavour every time: a section that cannot be summarised in one sentence — this section teaches… — is not yet a section, and a slang word, a scene or a joke that makes the point harder to find is cut, however well it speaks in the track's voice. A lesson is a sequence of points, each made once and made plainly — never a tour of everything the concept can do.

Arrange it, and give it a look

The page is divided into twelfths, so a block can take 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 or 12 of them — a quarter, a third, a half, two thirds, three quarters or the whole line. Width starts at the minimum and is earned. A block is written at the narrowest span its content reads in — 4, or 3 when the whole block is one short line — and it widens only for a reason it can name: its own content cannot be read at that width (a chart's columns, a dialog's long turns, a marked sentence's arrows, an answer field that needs the line), or nothing sits beside it and the rest of the line would be empty. Two short blocks share a row before either takes the whole line; the spans in a row add to 12; full width is the exception, never the default. Which span it gets is decided by what the block holds, never by what kind of block it is. Nothing is measured in the browser.

Every section has one teaching point, and its heading says it. The shape serves that point and nothing else: the block that states the rule, the block that shows it failing, the block that makes the student produce it. A block that serves a different point belongs in another section, and if two headings on the page could be swapped without the page changing, those sections were a tour rather than a sequence — rewrite them until each names the one thing it teaches.

The look is settled in the same pass: declared on the block, never inferred from what it holds, and it changes the paint only. One tint and one outline per section at most. A look never varies the page for its own sake, marks importance, or dresses a teaching block as an activity.

Markup — a block at its minimum, and one that had to widen
<h2>a section the sheet numbers</h2>

<div class="block" data-span="4">…the narrowest the content reads in…</div>
<div class="block" data-span="8" data-look="tint">…the prose beside it…</div>

<div class="block" data-span="12">…no neighbour, so it takes the rest of the line…</div>

Minimum first, then the reason: a neighbour fills the row (4+8, 6+6, 3+9), a lone block takes what the line has left, and full width is the content's demand rather than the page's habit. Looks: tint, tape, float, stripes, outline, dark. A section is an <h2>: the sheet numbers it and restarts the lettering under it, so neither is typed. An unset block is paper with the 2px accent rule; --accent-h and --edge-w change that rule, and --sp, --r1, --r2 the spacing and corners. The box you are reading is <details class="reveal"><summary>…</summary><div class="body"><div>…</div></div></details> — the inner div is what folds. Print needs nothing from a page.

Clean up the wording

Prose can be made shorter to remove a wrapped stub at the end of a line, or longer to fill one, as long as the lesson does not get worse for it. A block whose content is shorter than the blocks beside it in a row may gain an example or a question to even the heights.

How a page is written

Prose is not a visual: it has no markup, every block uses it, and it states the rule a visual cannot draw.

What prose is held to

What it is for

The rule no visual can draw, and the student's question answered in words — never the sentence a visual on the page already shows.

What it assumes

Every term is the student's own word or is defined here. A visual's own word (§3) is used only where that visual is drawn.

What it must not do

A metaphor where a fact belongs, two tenses presented as one, two tenses claimed to behave alike without naming what they share, a promise about the lesson, a false only / never / always. Nothing is coined either: a word this page does not define is not given a meaning in a lesson.

Whether a repeat earns its place

One rule per paragraph, with an example. A second paragraph on the same rule adds a case.

Two pairs, and why one of each was cut

Rejected. “Portuguese puts the whole claim inside the verb's ending: escrevi, tenho escrito. English splits it into two words that never touch, and one of them is a word you already know from do and will.”

Two tenses presented as one · claim with no timeline on the page · splits and never touch are figures of speech where a fact belongs · do and will share no behaviour with have.

Kept. “Escrevi is past simple: one finished event. Tenho escrito is present perfect: the writing is still true now. English keeps that difference — wrote / have written.”

Each tense is named with what it does, the difference is stated, and the Portuguese is a fact about Portuguese.

Rejected. “has been + -ing is the same machine with a longer tail.”

Machine is a term this page does not define, and the sentence talks about the lesson instead of the English.

Kept. “He's been playing all week is he has been playing: the playing started before this week and has not stopped. The same words with he is — he's playing — say only that it is happening now.”

Both are shown, the deciding word is named, and nothing must be learned first.

Register, and the one tint

Cooked is what you say to a friend, exhausted to a teacher: the same fact, and the choice of word is the register. Teach it by putting the two side by side and naming the room each belongs to.

The tint is that room's colour on the page. One tint per section: a second makes the first a decoration.

Choosing a visual

A visual is chosen by the question it answers, never by its name.

What every visual is described by

What it is for

The one question it answers, in the student's words.

What it assumes

Whether the concept is new, and every convention needed to read the drawing. The page supplies them or the visual is unreadable.

What it claims

One thing only. Used where that claim is false, it installs an error.

Whether a repeat earns its place

Two tests: it says something the sentence does not, and a repeat is answered rather than re-displayed.

The tests a use must pass

  1. It carries information the sentence does not already state.
  2. If it repeats, the repeat is answered rather than re-displayed.
  3. If it were removed, the student would lose the answer to the concept's question.
  4. The page still has one thing that is obviously the thing to look at.

No ceiling on quantity: one visual can appear twenty times and be wrong once. The failures are saturation (one visual repeated until it carries nothing) and monotony (every section through one shape).

Cost is a demand

A visual costs attention and space, and sometimes a media file or a script. The cheapest one is the visual the sentence already carries.

In-Block Features

Each of these goes inside a block with the prose that reads it, or with other elements, or stands as a block of its own at the span its content reads in; the examples here are the in-block size. They come in two families.

A. Augment existing text — the marks, the ruby lines, the card at a word and the timeline all attach to a sentence the lesson has already written. B. Standalone visuals — a chart, a list, a dialog, a drawing, a recording or a strip is a thing of its own, read by the sentence beside it.

None of them is used without accompaniment. Each is read by something — the sentence that explains it, a word list, the questions the student answers — in the same block or in the block next to it, and that holds when the element is the block's own subject. Nothing about an element changes when the block around it becomes an activity: an activity about word order uses the underline and the numbered list, and the same markup moves between the two.

Standalone visuals

.viz frames three things: the dialog, the player and the map. A chart, a flash card and a strip carry a frame of their own, and the lists and the bar take none. .viz is never a second .block: the block is the teaching frame, so a frame inside it would be two edges of the same rank.

A cell takes an h4 only where the lesson needs to name what is in it. Only the recording must have one, because the song's own name is part of the recording; nothing else here carries a heading, and the sentence beside an element is what tells the reader what it is.

A chart takes as many rows and columns as its content needs: a verb’s forms, the Portuguese for a word, an explanation, a line of the lesson’s own wording. It goes inside a block at any width, flows beside the prose or the figure it is read with, and can stand outside a block as a block-level element of its own. Section 5 puts activity controls in its cells.

A header earns its space only where a column would be ambiguous without one; where none needs a name the row collapses to a rule. A chart that stands alone takes a figcaption; a chart in a block takes one too, unless the block already names it in an h4.

Dialogs and activities

An activity is a block the student writes in, and it carries its own instruction in the block: a gap with no instruction has more than one right answer.

Each activity type here can stand as a block of its own, sized to the content it holds, or sit inside a block with the prose, the chart or the drawing it accompanies; the examples below are the in-block size. A lesson chooses and combines them, and it can teach a concept and train it in one block or teach in one block and train in another.

engine2.js marks the right option and writes .ans beside a typed field; a radio group’s key is data-a on .qitem. --ok is the colour it uses.
