# Lesson 48 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 33 (decisions, owners, dates — the meeting machinery), lesson 15 (modals: obligation, permission,
advice), lesson 26 (grading certainty when you cannot promise), lesson 34 (tone and the reader), lesson 37 (asking
the second question — now aimed downward in the hierarchy), lesson 38 (criticism of the idea, never the person),
lesson 47 (the conditional as a commitment: *if you do X by Friday, I can…*).
`must not re-teach:` meeting roles and minutes (33); the tone ladder (34); the disagreement ladder (38); how
conditionals are formed (19/25).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Say the vision in one sentence** that survives being repeated by someone else.
2. **Brief and delegate in five parts**: the outcome, why it matters, when, the limits, and who decides what.
3. **Give feedback in situation–behaviour–impact**, with no labels and no character judgements.
4. **Ask coaching questions** instead of dispensing advice, and know the difference between coaching, mentoring and
   managing.
5. **Hold the hard conversation** without softening it into vagueness.
6. **Announce change in four lines** — what changes, what does not, what is not known yet, and when it will be.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **A brief is five parts or it is a wish.** Outcome, why, when, the limits, and who decides what. When one is
  missing the questions come back later as mistakes — and the leader's most expensive sentence is *I thought you
  knew what I meant*. This is the page's spine and it is tested on the first thirty seconds.
- **Delegation without authority is a task with a hostage.** The student is taught to hand over the decision as
  explicitly as the work: *you decide the supplier, I decide the budget.* Accountability needs a name and a date, and
  the page says plainly that a shared owner is no owner.
- **The vision is one sentence because it has to travel without you.** Three parts: what we are doing, for whom, and
  what is different when it is done. A strategy that needs slides is a deck; a sentence gets repeated in a corridor.
- **SBI exists to keep the person out of the sentence.** Situation, behaviour, impact: *in Tuesday's call you
  interrupted the client twice, and she stopped asking questions.* No *you're* adjective anywhere — because *you are
  careless* is an identity, and identities get defended instead of changed.
- **The feedback sandwich is named and dismissed**, with the reason a learner can check: the filling is never heard,
  so the praise becomes a warning signal and the criticism becomes a rumour. Straight, kind, specific, then ask.
- **Coaching is questions, mentoring is advice, managing is the decision.** Confusing the three is why so many
  one-to-ones are status reports. The four questions: *what do you think is happening? what have you tried? what
  would good look like? what do you need from me?* — and then silence, which is the hard part.
- **The hard conversation has a shape**: fact plus intent (*I want you to keep this account*), the gap between
  expectation and what was observed, a pause long enough to be uncomfortable, then one observable next behaviour with
  a date. Softening the middle is what makes it cruel later.
- **Announcing change is four lines, and the fourth is the credibility**: what changes, what does not, what I do not
  know yet, and when I will tell you. Uncertainty that is unspoken becomes a rumour; uncertainty with a date becomes
  a plan.
- **Dependencies**: 33's owners and dates, 26's grading of certainty, 47's conditional commitment.

## Situation
The student has just been given a small team and a change to deliver: a supplier is being replaced in six weeks, two
people will report to them from Monday, and one of those two has been coasting for a year. Brief, delegate, give
feedback, coach, announce, and have the conversation nobody wants — in one week, in that order.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Heard — the brief with a hole in it.** Twenty-five seconds of a manager handing over a task (*"just get it done,
  you know what I mean, make it good"*) and then *"any questions?"* — followed by silence. The student names which of
  the five parts was missing, and hears the silence do the work.
- **Done — the SBI rewrite.** Six pieces of real-feeling feedback (*"you need to be more proactive", "your attitude
  is a problem"*) turned into situation–behaviour–impact, with the label forbidden.
- **Said — the coaching turn.** Four one-line problems from a team member; the student may ask, may not advise, and
  must survive three turns without giving a solution.

## Chosen
**Heard → Done → Said.** Heard first because the failure of a brief is audible as a silence, and the student is on
the receiving end of it rather than the giving end — which is where the empathy for the five parts comes from. Then
Done, because SBI is a rewrite skill and rewrites need volume. Then Said, the coaching turn, which is the hardest
block in the lesson: it asks the student to shut up on purpose, and it only works out loud.

**First 30 seconds:** the brief plays, then *"any questions?"*, then two full seconds of nothing. One question: *what
was missing?* Two of the five are plausible and one is right. Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
One brief diagnosis and one five-part brief written from a one-line task, four real/fake delegation judgements, six
SBI rewrites, two sandwich items diagnosed, eight coaching questions produced (two per problem) with three turns
survived without advice, one hard-conversation script with the gap, the pause and the next observable behaviour plus
a date, one change announcement in four lines, three one-sentence visions judged and one written, then a
no-consultation quiz that reuses no training item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The five-part brief chart is full width (part / the question it answers / what goes wrong without it / the sentence
that supplies it) — four fields, five rows, and it is the reference for the rest of the page. The SBI chart is full
width for the same reason (the three columns *are* the technique). The coaching questions sit as halves with the
delegation judgements — four and four short items, each of which would leave a full slot more than half empty. The
hard-conversation block is a full-width card in four labelled steps, because the order is the content and it must not
be read as prose.
