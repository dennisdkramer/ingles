# Lesson 47 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 19/25 (the conditional — *if you can…, we can…* is the third/first conditional doing work),
lesson 15 (modals of offer and obligation), lesson 13 (comparatives and the recommendation frame), lesson 12 (numbers
said fast and reported with rise/fall), lesson 33 (agendas, decisions, owners), lesson 38 (conceding before
countering — the same shape in a commercial key), lesson 45 (the confirming document), lesson 46 (directness for the
reader across the table).
`must not re-teach:` how conditionals are built (19/25); the agreement ladder (38); the report/e-mail layout (45);
cultural styles (46).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Negotiate interests, not positions**: find why the number matters before arguing about the number.
2. **Name their own BATNA** and estimate the other side's, because the walk-away is where the power is.
3. **Anchor with a reason**, and recognise an anchor with none.
4. **Say the one sentence the whole skill lives in**: *If you can take delivery in March, we can hold the price* —
   a condition for every concession, never a free gift.
5. **Concede in shrinking steps**, across dimensions (date, volume, warranty, payment, scope), never straight down
   the price.
6. **Handle a stall** by agreeing the *process* (who decides, when) instead of re-arguing the content, and **close in
   writing within 24 hours**.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **Positions are the last thing the other side will tell you.** *We need 10% off* is a position; the interest is
  *our budget is fixed and our board meets Friday*. The lesson's first move is the question that turns one into the
  other, and the page makes the student ask it before anything is offered.
- **Your BATNA is your only real power.** If you do not know what you do when this fails, you are negotiating from
  hope, and every concession is anxiety made audible. The BATNA sheet is a four-line object and it is filled in by
  the student before any dialogue.
- **An anchor without a reason gets discounted.** *We're at 80 because the last contract for this scope was 78* is an
  anchor; *we're at 80* is a wish. Anchoring is taught together with the justification and with the counter-move:
  refuse the frame, don't argue inside it.
- **Every concession needs a condition, spoken in the same breath.** This is the single sentence that separates a
  negotiator from a donor: *if you can…, we can…*. Two concessions in one sentence without two conditions is the
  classic amateur error and the page names it.
- **Concession shape is information.** Shrinking steps (10 → 4 → 1) say *we are nearly at the floor*; equal steps
  (10 → 10 → 10) say *there is more*. This is taught as a chart, not a lecture, and drilled as an ordering problem.
- **Trade across dimensions, not inside one.** Price is the dimension both sides defend; the deal is found in the
  others. The trade matrix forces the student to buy and sell in different rows.
- **A stall is rarely a refusal; it is a decision process you cannot see.** So the counter is procedural: *who
  decides, and when?* A date and a name are worth more than a better argument today.
- **Win-win is a test, not a slogan**: the deal has to beat both walk-aways, and it has to survive a second meeting.
  Anything that needs the other side to have been tricked fails that test.
- **Dependencies**: conditionals (19/25), comparatives and recommendation (13), numbers (12), closing document (45).

## Situation
A renewal that has gone wrong: the supplier wants +12%, the student's budget is flat, the client (their own internal
one) wants the same service for less, and there are three other dimensions nobody has mentioned yet. One hour, one
room, one number that has to move — and a confirmation e-mail by the end of the day.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Read — the same first exchange, twice.** *"We can't go above 90." — "That's nowhere near."* against *"We can't go
  above 90." — "What would you do with the extra ten?"* The student picks which conversation has a future and says
  what the second question bought.
- **Done — the trade matrix.** Six dimensions, two sides, one blank grid of moves; the student builds a package that
  beats both walk-aways. This is where the lesson becomes visible.
- **Said — the conditional offer at speed.** Eight situations, one sentence each, condition before concession. The
  only block that has to be spoken, because the phrase is worthless if it arrives late.

## Chosen
**Read → Done → Said.** Read first because the failure is one line long and instantly recognisable, and it teaches
the position/interest split before it is named. Then Done, the matrix, because the intellectual work of negotiation
is finding dimensions rather than winning the number — and a matrix makes that physical. Then Said, the conditional
sentence, which is the one thing that must be automatic: if it is not said in the same breath as the concession, it
is not said.

**First 30 seconds:** the two exchanges, side by side, and one question — which room would you rather be in, and what
exactly did the second question change? Nothing is defined. The student's answer is wrong in an interesting way (they
usually say "it was politer"), which is the door into interests.

## What the student produces (evidence)
One exchange diagnosis, six position→interest conversions, four questions written to find an interest, one BATNA
sheet (mine / theirs / cost of no deal / first move), four anchor judgements and two anchors justified in writing,
eight conditional offers produced aloud, one concession-sequence ordering with the reason, three stall counters
(process, not content), a trade matrix with four crossed moves, one closing summary plus one confirmation e-mail,
then a no-consultation quiz that reuses no training item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The position/interest chart and the concession-shape chart are full width (what is said / what it hides / the move
that answers it) — both are three-field rows and the rows are the lesson's argument. The trade matrix is the one
genuinely tabular object on the page: full width, dimensions down the side, both sides across the top, because a
matrix in a half column is a list. The conditional-offer drill is full width (each item needs the situation, the
condition and the concession on one line). The anchors and the stalls sit as halves — four and three short items,
each of which would leave a full slot more than half empty.
