# Lesson 44 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 3 (the first repair phrases — *Sorry, could you say that again?* — are referenced, never retaught),
lesson 33 (summarising a meeting: decisions, owners, dates), lesson 37 (the follow-up question as the engine of
understanding), lesson 38 (asking for the evidence — same move, different target), lesson 41 (the surviving-error
idea), lesson 43 (the error log the student wrote at 17:00 — this lesson's raw material), lesson 30 (reported speech:
the summary is a report).
`must not re-teach:` the phrase set from 3 as new vocabulary; meeting summary format (33); the agreement ladder (38);
reported-speech backshift (30).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Repair yourself at the first wobble**: *I meant… / What I'm trying to say is… / Let me start that again.*
2. **Repair the other person with a hypothesis**, not a plea: *Do you mean X or Y?* — the strongest clarification
   move there is.
3. **Check understanding by summarising in three parts**: what is needed, by when, and why.
4. **Describe around a missing word** instead of stopping dead — by function, by category, by opposite.
5. **Say "I didn't follow that part"** without it sounding like an apology, and know when that is the strong move.
6. **Self-monitor**: name their own three recurring errors and drill those, not the syllabus.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **There are two repairs and they are one skill.** Repairing yourself (*I meant the Tuesday, not this Tuesday*) and
  repairing the other person (*do you mean…?*) are the same act: you noticed a gap and you closed it out loud
  instead of hoping. The page teaches them together because in real conversation they alternate within one minute.
- **Repair early or pay later.** A wobble repaired at second three costs a clause; the same wobble repaired at
  second thirty costs the paragraph, and unrepaired it costs a week of work. The three-second rule is the page's
  spine, and it is trained with a clock.
- **The hypothesis question beats the open plea.** *Could you explain that another way?* hands the whole job back to
  the speaker and often produces the same words louder. *Do you mean the client's date or ours?* is answerable with
  one word and reveals your actual confusion. Offering a guess is what turns clarification from an admission into a
  contribution — and it is the same instinct as 38's evidence question.
- **Summarising is the professional version of "did I get that?"** Three parts: *so you need X **by** Friday
  **because** the board meets.* If the summary is wrong, the other person fixes it for free, and they now know you
  can be trusted with the task.
- **The missing word is not a wall.** Three routes around it: function (*the thing that signs off the budget*),
  category (*one of those legal documents*), opposite (*not the final version — the one before it*). The rule: never
  stop in the middle of a sentence, go around.
- **Fake understanding is the expensive error.** Nodding is the only mistake in this lesson that has a cost measured
  in money. The page says plainly that admitting it is the strong move, and gives the two phrasings that do not sound
  like an excuse (*I didn't follow the part about the penalty* — specific, not global).
- **Self-monitoring is the actual level-44 skill**: three named personal patterns beat twenty pages of review,
  because at this level the remaining errors are personal.
- **Dependencies**: 3's phrases (used), 33's summary, 30's reporting, 43's error log.

## Situation
A handover call the student is not prepared for: a colleague speaks fast, uses three acronyms and one number that
matters, and leaves them with a task. Everything that goes wrong in the next two weeks started in one unanswered
moment in that call — and the page starts there, then rewinds.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Read — the call that was never repaired.** Twelve turns, three lines where a repair belonged, and a note at the
  end saying what it cost. The student finds the three moments and says what the repair should have been.
- **Said — the hypothesis question.** Six vague or ambiguous statements; the student must offer a guess and a
  question in one breath, against a clock.
- **Done — the summary that saves it.** The student writes the three-part summary of the call and then compares it
  with what the colleague actually meant; the gap is the lesson.

## Chosen
**Read → Said → Done.** Read first, because the cost has to be visible before the technique is offered — the student
sees a fortnight of avoidable work in one line. Then Said, because the hypothesis question is the one move that must
be produced at speed or it never happens. Then Done, the summary, where both repairs combine and the student finds
the one detail they lost.

**First 30 seconds:** two people are talking. One says *"so it's the Tuesday before the go-live"*; the other says
*"sure."* Eleven turns later the second person has done the wrong task. The student clicks the exact line where the
repair should have happened. Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Three repair moments located and rewritten, six self-repair conversions, six hypothesis questions written from
ambiguous statements, four *say it another way* requests built on an offered guess, one three-part summary of the
call, six describe-around-the-word items (three routes each), four spoken repairs inside three seconds, two
"I didn't follow that part" phrasings judged and one produced, three named personal error patterns from the 43 log
plus three retest items, then a no-consultation quiz that reuses no training item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The two-repairs chart is full width (type / what you say / what it does to the conversation / when it is too late).
The hypothesis-question drill is full width: each item needs the ambiguous statement, the student's guess and the
question, so it is three lines per item and a half would wrap badly. The describe-around drill and the self-repair
drill sit as halves in one row — six items each, two groups that would otherwise leave a full slot more than half
empty. The call transcript is a full-width `.dialog` with the cost note in a `tr.note` row beneath it.
