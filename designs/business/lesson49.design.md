# Lesson 49 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 26 (grading certainty — the same instinct, now pointed at the future), lesson 20 (the present
perfect as the tense of "so far": *the pilot has shown*), lesson 12 (numbers, because a claim without one is
decoration), lesson 13 (comparatives inside a value claim), lesson 45 (the report and the proposal as forms),
lesson 47 (the value proposition — a claim somebody has to believe), lesson 48 (the vision in one sentence).
`must not re-teach:` the deduction system and its scale (26); modal perfects (26/41); comparative forms (13); how to
write a proposal (45).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Say what will happen with the right amount of certainty**: *will · is going to · is set to / is expected to / is
   likely to · could / may / might · is due to* — chosen by the evidence, not the mood.
2. **Read and write the "is set to" pattern**, the register of the business press.
3. **Audit a buzzword** with three questions and one test: can it be measured or falsified?
4. **Use the technology vocabulary precisely** — disruption, scalability, digital literacy, adoption curve, pilot,
   rollout, sunset — each with its testable meaning.
5. **Plan in the vocabulary of gates**: pilot → gate → rollout, with the decision point named.
6. **Make a claim you cannot verify honestly** — *early data indicates*, *the pilot suggests* — without hype and
   without dismissal.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **The future is a ladder, and the evidence chooses the rung.** *Will* is a commitment; *going to* is a plan with
  evidence behind it; *is set to / is expected to / is likely to* is an informed prediction somebody else can be
  held to; *could / may / might* is possibility; *is due to* is a diary entry. Speaking one rung too high is a
  promise; one too low is a shrug. Same grammar family as 26, aimed forward.
- **"Is set to" is not decoration — it is a sourcing signal.** It says *this is not my opinion, it is the shape of
  the plan*. Taught as a pattern with its register: press releases, roadmaps, board papers; odd in a stand-up.
- **A buzzword is a word that has stopped predicting anything.** The audit is three questions: *what would we see if
  this were true? what would we see if it were false? who disagrees?* Any word that survives no answer is decoration,
  and the page makes that judgement in front of the student rather than telling them which words to avoid.
- **The terms have precise meanings that the business world has worn smooth**: disruption is not "new", it is a
  cheaper or simpler offer entering at the low end and moving up; scalability is not "big", it is cost per unit
  staying flat as volume rises; digital literacy is not "liking computers", it is a named set of skills. Precision is
  taught with a test for each, because a term you cannot test is a term you cannot use in an argument.
- **Digital literacy is taught as an example of the audit, not as a virtue**: say which three skills, and the word
  becomes usable; leave it abstract and it is filler.
- **Gates are what makes a rollout plan a plan.** Pilot, gate (who decides, on what evidence), rollout, sunset. The
  vocabulary of a decision point is the most useful part of innovation English and the least taught.
- **Honest hedging on an unverifiable claim is a skill with phrases**: *the pilot suggests · early data indicates ·
  we expect, subject to · we have not tested this at scale*. Hype and refusal are both failures of nerve.
- **The CV/pitch test**: say what changed, not what adjective. *I moved 40% of onboarding to self-service in nine
  months* beats *I am a passionate innovator*.
- **Dependencies**: 26's grading, 12's numbers, 45's forms, 47's value proposition.

## Situation
A mid-size logistics company is launching a pilot: route-planning software, six depots, one union, a board that
wants a number by Q3. The student writes the announcement, hedges the timeline honestly, audits the three buzzwords in
the slide deck, and answers "so is this AI, or what?" without lying.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Read — the press release with two facts.** Eleven buzzwords, one number, one date. The student finds the two
  facts, then the two sentences that would have been worth the whole release.
- **Done — the buzzword audit.** Ten terms, three questions each; the student either supplies the test or strikes the
  word. The lesson's intellectual core, and it produces a personal blacklist.
- **Said — the hedge ladder.** Eight claims with a target rung in brackets (*certain, expected, possible, scheduled*);
  the student produces the sentence at the right certainty, aloud.

## Chosen
**Read → Done → Said.** Read first because a press release is the natural habitat of the vocabulary and the failure is
funny — the student has read a hundred of them without noticing there was nothing in them, and now cannot un-see it.
Then Done, the audit, which is where the lesson turns from cynicism into a tool. Then Said, the hedges, because
certainty is a spoken decision and it has to arrive with the sentence, not after it.

**First 30 seconds:** a headline with eleven buzzwords — *"Future-ready platform set to disrupt logistics, driving
scalable outcomes"* — and three facts to find. The student finds none in the headline, one in the second paragraph.
Then a single question: *which word would you delete first?* Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Two facts located and two rewritten sentences, six rungs placed on the future-certainty chart, six *is set to*
conversions from plain futures, ten audited buzzwords (test supplied or word struck), six claims put through the three
questions with a verdict each, one pilot→gate→rollout plan ordered with the gate's evidence named, six unverifiable
claims hedged honestly, three CV/pitch lines rewritten as what changed, one spoken "so is this AI?" answer in three
sentences, then a no-consultation quiz that reuses no training item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The future-certainty ladder is the page's full-width chart (structure / the evidence it needs / what you commit to /
where it belongs) — four fields across six rows, and it is the lesson's spine. The buzzword audit is full width too:
term, the testable meaning, the three questions, the verdict — a half column would turn a chain of reasoning into
three fragments. The *is set to* conversions and the hedge drill sit as halves — six items each, both one-line
answers, and each would leave a full slot more than half empty. The pilot plan is a full-width ordered list inside a
`.panel`, because sequence is its content.
