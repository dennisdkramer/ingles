# Lesson 27 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 1 (the sentence skeleton — two sentences become one), lesson 16/24 (nouns and articles inside the
clause), lesson 21 (the e-mail register), lesson 24 (articles — *the* report we discussed), lesson 26 (hedges in the
report), lesson 23 (the report as a genre).
`must not re-teach:` articles (24), the passive (29 — the reduced clause is *not* the passive), phrasal verbs (18-23),
the past perfect (36).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Combine two sentences into one** with who / which / that / where / whose / when — and choose the word by what it
   replaces (person, thing, place, possession, time).
2. **Hear the comma**: *the team that presented yesterday* (one of several teams) against *the team, which presented
   yesterday,* (there is only one team, and this is extra).
3. **Drop the object pronoun** (*the report we sent*) but never the subject pronoun (*the team that presented*).
4. **Compress further** with a reduced clause: *the team working on it*, *the report sent yesterday*.
5. Write a report line that carries three facts in one sentence without losing the reader.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **The relative pronoun is a stand-in for a noun already mentioned.** The choice is mechanical: what is the noun?
  person → who/that; thing → which/that; place → where; possession → whose; time → when.
- **The comma is meaning, not punctuation.** Restrictive (no comma) identifies; non-restrictive (comma) adds. *My
  brother who lives in Rio* (I have several) against *My brother, who lives in Rio,* (I have one). The lesson drills
  the consequence, not the terminology.
- **`that` is the restrictive pronoun** — it never appears after a comma. This gives the student a second, audible
  test for the comma decision.
- **The object pronoun is optional; the subject pronoun is not.** *The report (that) we sent* is fine; *the team that
  presented* needs its *that*, because the clause would lose its subject.
- **The reduced clause is the third step of compression**: two sentences → one with a pronoun → one with a
  participle phrase. Taught as recognition and light production, which is what a report needs.
- **Dependencies**: articles from 24 (the noun inside the clause); the report register from 23/24; hedges from 26.

## Situation
A quarterly report that has to fit on one page: three facts per sentence, no ambiguity about which team, which
report, which office. The student compresses a colleague's five short sentences into two, then repairs a report line
where a missing comma accuses the wrong person.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Read — the report line.** Four sentences with the relative pronoun removed; the student restores the right one.
- **Done — the comma test.** Six pairs where the comma changes which person/thing is meant; the student says which
  reading is true.
- **Heard — the briefing.** Four spoken lines where the relative clause carries the identifying information; the
  student ticks which of two people/things is meant.

## Chosen
**Done → Read → Heard.** The comma test first because it is the decision that carries meaning, and it is invisible in
translation; then the report, where the compression is the point; then the ear, because spoken English uses
restrictive clauses constantly and almost never the comma.

**First 30 seconds:** *The manager, who called yesterday, is in Berlin.* against *The manager who called yesterday is
in Berlin.* — the student clicks which one says there is only one manager. Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Six comma decisions, four pronoun choices, four object/subject omissions, four reduced-clause matches, four
listening items, a compressed paragraph of three sentences, then a no-consultation quiz with no reused item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The pronoun chart and the compression chart are full width (five fields per row). The comma pair and the
omission pair sit as halves in one row — two four-item groups that would each leave a full slot more than half
empty, and the contrast is the teaching.
