# Lesson 24 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 1 (a/an by sound — now systematised, not introduced), lesson 16 (countable/uncountable — the
zero-article half), lesson 13 (superlatives — *the* best), lesson 12 (numbers/ordinals), lesson 23 (the report and
the minutes where a missing *the* reads as carelessness).
`must not re-teach:` the plural and the countability decision itself (lessons 4 and 16); *a few / few, a little /
little* (lesson 16 owns the quantifier contrast); the phoneme chart (lesson 10) — only the one *the* contrast is
taught here.

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Choose among three**: *a/an* (one of many, first mention), *the* (both speakers know which), zero (plurals and
   uncountables in general statements).
2. **Hear the question that decides**: *does the listener know which one?* — yes → *the*; no, and it is one of many
   → *a/an*; no, and it is a whole class → zero.
3. **Use the fixed set**: a/an with jobs; *the* with superlatives, ordinals and specific-of-a-group; zero in *go to
   work / school / bed*, *at home*, *on time*; *in the end* against *at the end*.
4. **Handle the heavy uncountables** (*information, advice, research, feedback, equipment, furniture, luggage,
   money*) — they take no *a/an* and no plural, but they do take *the* when specific.
5. **Say the two *the*'s**: /ðə/ before a consonant, /ði/ before a vowel sound.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **The article is a claim about shared knowledge.** Everything else (first mention, uniqueness, second mention) is
  a special case of that one question. The lesson returns to the question rather than to the list.
- **A/an is not "um".** It is *one of many*, and it is why jobs take it: *she's an engineer* (one of the engineers of
  the world). Portuguese drops it — that is the highest-frequency error here.
- **Zero article is a statement about the class**, not about a missing word: *cats are independent* is about all
  cats; *the cat is a predator* is about the species as a category; *a cat* is one animal. The three-way contrast is
  the lesson's intellectual centre.
- **The generic *the* is an argumentative choice** — it sounds scientific and slightly formal, which is why reports
  use it. Taught as register, not as grammar trivia.
- **Uncountables take *the*** when specific: *information is expensive* (class) against *the information we bought*
  (specific). This closes the loop with lesson 16.
- **Fixed phrases are memorised as blocks**: at work, at home, on time, in the end, at the end — with the meaning
  difference for the last pair.
- **The pronunciation is one rule**: /ði/ before a vowel sound (*the office, the hour*), /ðə/ elsewhere.
- **Dependencies**: countability from 16; superlatives from 13; ordinals from 12.

## Situation
A quarterly report that a colleague wrote without articles: the student repairs it, and in doing so has to decide,
line by line, whether the reader knows which thing is meant. Same content, two audiences: the internal note (loose)
and the client report (tight).

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Read — the report with the missing articles.** Eight lines, each missing one decision; the student inserts
  *a/an*, *the* or nothing.
- **Done — the shared-knowledge sort.** Twelve noun phrases sorted by what the listener knows: first mention, second
  mention, unique thing, whole class.
- **Heard — the two *the*'s.** Six short lines; the student ticks /ðə/ or /ði/ and then repeats them.

## Chosen
**Read → Done → Heard.** The report first because the article is invisible until it is missing; then the sort,
which forces the question *does the listener know which one?*; then the ear, because /ði/ before a vowel is the
detail that makes a careful speaker sound careful.

**First 30 seconds:** *Report was submitted before deadline.* — the student clicks what is missing and why. Nothing
is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Eight article insertions, twelve sort decisions, four job/nationality items, four fixed-phrase items, four
uncountable items, six pronunciation ticks, a repaired paragraph for the client, then a no-consultation quiz with no
reused item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The three-way chart (a/an · the · zero) and the fixed-phrase chart are full width (five fields per row). The
generic-vs-specific group and the uncountable group sit as halves in one row — both are four-item groups that would
each leave a full slot more than half empty, and the contrast between them is the teaching.
