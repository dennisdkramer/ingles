# Lesson 50 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` the whole business track, and names it: 5 and 41 (the skeleton and the speed), 14 (contractions and
reductions — the sound of the informal registers), 26 and 41 (grading a claim), 34 and 45 (the reader and the
document), 36 (the long answer as a story), 42 (the spoken verb and its written twin), 44 (repair while going),
46 (clarity over idiom), 47 (the negotiation), 48 (the vision sentence), 49 (honest hedging).
`must not re-teach:` any system. **This is the last page of the track and it is a performance, not a review.** The
graded assessment is lesson 40's and is not this page; nothing here is scored.

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Hold a ninety-second uninterrupted answer** with a structure inside it (where we were → where we are → what I
   would do next), repairing without stopping.
2. **Switch register on purpose** between the track's three options — business-casual, peer-to-peer, candid
   informal — and name the markers that do the switching.
3. **Use the candid register where it belongs**, including the swears and the slang, knowing their strength and the
   room they cost you in.
4. **Keep clarity ahead of idiom** when the room is international.
5. **Write the one-page recommendation** that survives being forwarded.
6. **Leave with a plan**: their own three surviving errors, three drills and one habit.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **Fluency is not speed; it is not stopping.** The final skill is the long turn: ninety seconds, one structure, and
  the repair moves of 44 running *inside* it rather than ending it. The page trains the turn before it lets the
  student perform it, and it says plainly that an answer with a structure and a hesitation in it is worth more than a
  fluent list.
- **Register is a set of visible switches, not a feeling.** Contractions and phrasal verbs on; first names; short
  sentences; *hey* rather than *dear*; the swear only where the relationship earns it. Code-switching is taught as
  the deliberate flip of those switches — including the flip *back* when the client walks in, which is the move most
  learners cannot make and the one that marks a professional.
- **The candid register is core content, not decoration.** It is the register of the corridor, the lunch, the Slack
  channel: *that's bullshit* (the claim is false and I am annoyed), *we're screwed* (in serious trouble), *it's a
  mess*, *hell of a week*, *ain't*, *wtf*, *that's cooked*. Each is given its strength, its audience and its cost.
  The page is honest that the strongest words exist, are used, and are a career decision in a meeting — recognise,
  understand, and choose.
- **Clarity beats idiom abroad.** The three moves from 46, now as a closing standard: slower, shorter, fewer idioms —
  plus write the number down. *Being understood* is the finish line, not *sounding native*.
- **The recommendation is the track's last written form**: purpose, findings, recommendation, one page, the
  recommendation where a senior reader looks. All of it is 45's; here it is produced cold.
- **The plan is the real output.** At level 50 the remaining errors are personal and permanent unless named: three
  patterns, three drills, one habit, thirty days. This replaces a grade the page is not allowed to give.
- **Dependencies**: everything. Nothing new is introduced; the new material is the student's own performance.

## Situation
A final interview for a role the student wants, with a panel of three: a hiring manager who wants the long answer, a
future peer who wants to know if they are bearable at lunch, and a director who reads only the one-page
recommendation. Plus the offer conversation afterwards, which is a negotiation (47) and will be conducted at two
registers within ten minutes.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Said — the ninety-second turn.** One prompt, a clock, no notes, and a structure to fill. Everything the track
  built has to show up here or it never existed; the page cannot fake this and does not try.
- **Read — the same message in three rooms.** One short message written for a client, a colleague and a friend, with
  the switches marked in the margin. The fastest way to make register visible, because the differences are countable.
- **Heard — the corridor and the meeting.** Two recordings of the same two people, ten minutes apart, one on each
  side of a door; the student marks every switch and decides which one was the mistake.

## Chosen
**Read → Heard → Said.** Read first, because register has to be seen before it can be heard: three versions, the
switches marked, and the student counts contractions the way they once counted tenses. Then Heard, the same two
people on either side of a door, where the flip has to be caught in real time — including the flip back. Then Said,
the ninety-second turn and the offer conversation, which is the only honest closing assessment this page is allowed
to make.

**First 30 seconds:** one line, three ways: *"We need to talk about Thursday."* / *"Got a sec to talk about
Thursday?"* / *"Thursday's a mess — we need to sort it."* One question: *which one would you send to your boss, and
which one would get you a reply from a friend?* Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Three register placements from the first line, one message produced in three registers with the switches marked, six
swear/slang items matched to strength, audience and cost (with the two strongest recognised and not deployed), eight
switch-points identified in the two recordings including the flip back, one clarity rewrite of an idiomatic sentence
for an international room, one ninety-second spoken answer with its structure named afterwards, one one-page written
recommendation, four register turns inside the offer conversation, three surviving errors named with a drill for each
and one thirty-day habit, then a closing no-consultation quiz that draws from the whole track and reuses no training
item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The three-register chart is the page's full-width spine (register / the markers that show it / one line of the same
message / the room it belongs in) — four fields, three rows, and everything else on the page points back to it. The
swear-and-slang table is full width (item / strength / who you may say it to / what it costs) because the cost column
is the teaching. The clarity rewrites and the switch-point items sit as halves — six items each, one line apiece. The
ninety-second turn is a full-width `.panel` holding only the prompt, the clock and the structure, because anything
else on that block would be read instead of spoken.
