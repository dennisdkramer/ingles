# Lesson 26 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 15 (the operator grid: can/have to/should — the present forms this lesson grades),
lesson 19 (*should have* met inside the 3rd conditional; the past perfect block *had + participle*),
lesson 14 (*might've / must've* as sound), lesson 21 (*look at / seem* in reports), lesson 25 (the hypothetical as
the polite hedge).
`must not re-teach:` ability, permission, obligation, advice (lesson 15 owns them); the conditionals (19/25);
the past perfect as a narrative tense (36); *would rather / wish* (25).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Grade a claim**: *must be* (certain), *might / may / could be* (possible), *can't be* (impossible),
   *should be* (expected). The same evidence, four levels of confidence.
2. **Deduce about the past**: *must have / might have / may have / could have / can't have + participle*.
3. **Separate *should have***: the expectation that failed (*the report should have arrived*) against regret
   (*I should have checked*) — same form, two jobs.
4. **Hedge in a report**: *seem, appear, look like, sound like*; *probably, definitely, I doubt*.
5. Write a status report where every claim carries its level of certainty.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **Deduction is arithmetic on evidence, not opinion.** *Must be* means "the evidence leaves no other option";
  *can't be* means "the evidence excludes it". The lesson always shows the evidence first, the modal second.
- **The scale is the lesson**: must (95%) — should (80%, expected) — may/might/could (50%) — can't (0%). One
  vertical line, four stops, and every sentence is a position on it.
- **The past forms are the present forms + *have* + participle**: the operator does not change its meaning when it
  moves to the past. That is why lesson 15's grid is the prerequisite.
- ***should have* has two lives**: expectation that failed (*the train should have arrived*) and regret
  (*I should have called*). The subject decides: a thing expected → expectation; a person judging themselves →
  regret. The lesson names both, because the map says so.
- **Hedges are the professional layer**: *seem / appear / look like* lower the claim without lowering the
  information; *probably / definitely / I doubt* grade it in one word.
- **Dependencies**: the operator grid from 15; *have + participle* from 19/20; the report register from 24.

## Situation
A client's package has not arrived and nobody knows why. The evidence accumulates: a tracking scan, a phone that
rings out, a colleague who saw the van. The student has to say what must have happened, what might have happened,
and what can't have happened — and then write it in a status e-mail without overclaiming.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Read — the status e-mail.** Six claims, each with its modal removed; the student restores the level of
  certainty that the evidence supports.
- **Done — the evidence board.** Six pieces of evidence and six conclusions; the student matches and grades.
- **Heard — the phone call.** Four lines where the modal carries the whole message (*it must have gone to the wrong
  depot*); the student ticks what the speaker believes.

## Chosen
**Done → Read → Heard.** The evidence board first because deduction is a procedure (evidence → exclusion →
conclusion), not a vocabulary list; then the e-mail, where the register lives; then the call, where the whole
message is the modal.

**First 30 seconds:** *The tracking says "delivered", but nobody signed.* — the student clicks the strongest
conclusion the evidence supports. Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Six evidence→conclusion matches, four present deductions, four past deductions, four *should have* decisions, four
hedge choices, four listening items, a spoken deduction chain, a written status line, then a no-consultation quiz
with no reused item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The certainty scale and the past-forms chart are full width (five fields per row). The hedges group and the
*should have* pair sit as halves in one row — two four-item groups that would each leave a full slot more than half
empty, and the contrast between expectation and regret is the teaching.
