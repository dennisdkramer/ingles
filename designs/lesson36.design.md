# Lesson 36 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 6-7 (the simple past of the workhorse verbs — the spine of any story), lesson 11 (past
continuous as the scene, past simple as the interruption), lesson 19 (the past perfect exists from here on),
lesson 20 (the present perfect and its "it matters now" job), lesson 34 (reading the audience), lesson 26 (grading a
claim — *must have* as the listener's deduction about the story).
`must not re-teach:` the past simple / past continuous pair and its *when* + interruption frame (11); the form and
the four uses of the present perfect (20); how the past perfect is built (19); the e-mail ladder (34).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Use the shape**: hook → set-up → complication → climax → resolution — five beats, and know which one a given
   answer is missing.
2. **Give each tense its job**: past continuous for the wallpaper, simple past for the events, past perfect for the
   reach-back, present perfect for the point the story is making now.
3. **Join the beats out loud**: *at first, then, that's when, by the time …, in the end* — and *by the time* + past
   perfect as the one-frame reach-back.
4. **Cut detail that does no work**: keep the number and the consequence, drop the adverb.
5. **Tell the same story at three lengths** — the one-liner, the 30-second version, the interview answer — and name
   what was dropped at each length and what never is.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **A story is a shape, not a chronology.** A list of what happened in order is not a story; the shape is the
  tension and its release. Every beat has one job and one question it answers (*how did it start? who are we with?
  what went wrong? what did you do? what changed?*).
- **The tense is the signpost, and the listener reads it.** One story, four tenses: the continuous paints the room,
  the simple past walks through it, the perfect reaches back over the shoulder, the present perfect turns to the
  person listening and says *this is why I'm telling you*. Getting the tense wrong is what makes an account sound
  like a report instead of a story — the theme of 34, now in speech.
- **The reach-back has a condition**: the past perfect is only for what happened *before* the story started, and
  only when the listener needs it. *By the time the client called, we had already shipped it* — the flashback is one
  clause, not a new section.
- **The joinery carries the sequence** so the tense can carry the meaning. *At first* also means *and then it
  changed* — it is a promise of a complication.
- **Detail earns its place by doing work**: a number, a name, a cost. *We were late* is nothing; *we were nine days
  late* is a story. Adjectives decorate; numbers testify.
- **Length is a choice, and the choice is what gets cut.** Never the hook, never the resolution; the set-up
  compresses to a clause; the complication is the last thing to go.
- **The ending decides the conversation**: a story that ends with what changed invites the next question; a story
  that ends with *and that was it* closes the room (hands over to 37).
- **Dependencies**: the past simple from 6-7, the interruption frame from 11, the past perfect from 19, the present
  perfect from 20.

## Situation
A promotion panel and the corridor outside it. The same project — a launch that nearly died — told four ways by
four people: the CV line, the 30-second networking version, the interview answer, and the version a colleague tells
about someone else. The student is the one who has to tell it, then the one who has to listen to it and decide what
to ask next.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Done — the shuffled story.** Nine sentences of one project, out of order and with the verbs in the wrong tense;
  the student rebuilds the beats and chooses the tense per beat. Highest diagnostic yield: shape and tense in one
  object.
- **Heard — the answer that kills the interview.** Three recorded answers to *tell me about a time you fixed
  something*; the student ticks which one earns the follow-up question and which beat is missing from the others.
- **Read — one story at three lengths.** The CV line, the 30-second version and the 140-word answer, side by side;
  the student marks what each length threw away.

## Chosen
**Heard → Done → Read**, then the student's own story.
The heard opening first because the failure is audible before it is nameable — three answers, one of which gets the
next question — and the student has to say *why* before any beat is defined. Then the shuffled story, because the
shape and the tense system have to be handled together, not explained twice. Then the three lengths, which is the
professional payoff: the same material, cut to fit, and nothing important lost.

**First 30 seconds:** three answers play. The panel says one line after each: *"…okay."* / *"…and?"* / *"Wait — how
did you get them to agree?"* The student clicks which answer earned the third line and says what the other two were
missing. Nothing is defined yet.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Five beat labels and their questions recovered from a scrambled story, eight tense choices inside one story, six
sequencing joins, four reach-back conversions (*by the time*), three detail edits (a number in, an adjective out),
three listening items from the panel answers, a length audit of the same story at three sizes, one written 120-word
career story of their own with the five beats marked, a spoken 60-second version, then a no-consultation quiz that
reuses no training item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The five-beat chart (beat / its question / what it sounds like / what it must not be) is full width — four fields
per row and the rows are the spine of the lesson. The four-tense chart is also full width (tense / its job in the
story / one line of this story / what it would sound like if you used it here). The sequencing pairs and the
*by the time* conversions sit as halves in one row: two four-item drills that would each leave a full slot more
than half empty. The three-length comparison is one full-width card stack — the point is the *vertical* comparison,
so halves would destroy it.
