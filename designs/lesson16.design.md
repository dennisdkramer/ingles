# Lesson 16 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 12 (prices, invoice, receipt, how much does it cost), lesson 1 (a/an by sound — used, not
taught), lesson 5 (do/does questions), lesson 4 (the -s of the third person and of the plural), lesson 11 (was/were
for stock that is gone).
`must not re-teach:` the article system — a/an/the/zero (lesson 24 owns it); a/an by sound (lesson 1); the numbers
and prices themselves (lesson 12); time prepositions (lesson 17); *some/any* as determiners of a specific previous
mention (that is the article lesson's business — here only quantity).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Classify a noun by what it lets you do**: countable = you can put a number in front and it takes a plural;
   uncountable = it does not take a plural and never takes *a/an*.
2. **Choose the quantifier by the noun, not by the Portuguese**: *many / a few* with countables, *much / a little*
   with uncountables, *a lot of* with both, *some / any* by sentence type.
3. **Measure the unmeasurable**: a cup of coffee, a bottle of water, a box of paper, a piece of advice — the
   container is what makes an uncountable countable.
4. **Ask the right question**: *How many boxes?* against *How much coffee?* — and hear the same *how much* doing
   the price job of lesson 12.
5. **Treat money as a mass and coins/notes as objects**: *how much money*, but *how many coins*.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **Countability is a property of the sense, not of the word**: *coffee* is a substance, *two coffees* are two cups;
  *paper* is a material, *a paper* is a document. The lesson names the shift without teaching articles.
- **The quantifier is a slot, not a translation**: *muito* is three English words and the noun decides which one.
  This is the central error the lesson exists to kill.
- **much/many live in negatives and questions; a lot of lives in affirmatives.** Taught as a preference of English,
  not a rule of logic, with the note that *a lot of* is always safe.
- **The container is the bridge.** A partitive phrase turns a mass into something you can order, count and price —
  which is exactly what the supplier call needs.
- **some/any**: *some* for a quantity that exists (and for offers), *any* for a quantity in doubt (negative,
  question). *Any* in an affirmative means "it doesn't matter which" — one line, not a system.
- **Dependencies**: the prices and figures come from 12; *is there / are there* comes from 9; the plural from 4.

## Situation
An office manager orders supplies before a client visit: coffee, water, paper, sugar, and a budget that is a single
number. The supplier calls to confirm quantities, and the manager has to answer with the right quantifier every
time, because *how much* and *how many* get different answers.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Done — the two-column sort.** Twenty nouns, one decision each: countable or uncountable. Fast, and it exposes
  the nouns the student has been treating as countable (*information, advice, money, paper, coffee*).
- **Read — the order e-mail.** The manager writes the order in full sentences with quantifiers and partitives; the
  student repairs the four wrong quantifiers and rewrites two lines.
- **Heard — the supplier call.** Six short questions (*How many boxes? How much coffee?*) at speed; the student
  ticks what is being counted and answers with a quantity.

## Chosen
**Done → Read → Heard.** The sort first because the decision is binary and cheap and it creates the need for the
vocabulary; then the e-mail, where the quantifiers sit in real sentences and the containers appear; then the call,
because *how much / how many* is only hard when someone is waiting for an answer. The money contrast closes the
lesson, since it is the one place where the same noun is both (money as mass, coins as objects).

**First 30 seconds:** four words on screen — *coffee, box, information, coin* — and the student clicks which two can
take a number. Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Eight countable/uncountable decisions, four *some/any* choices, four quantifier choices, six partitive completions,
four *how much/how many* questions, a repaired order e-mail, a spoken order confirmation, then a no-consultation
quiz with no reused item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The classification chart and the partitive table are full-width (four to five fields per row). The *some/any* and
*how much/how many* panels sit as halves in one row — both are four-item groups that would each leave a full slot
more than half empty, and the contrast between them is the point.
