# Lesson 41 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lessons 4-7 (present simple/continuous and the two pasts), 11 (the two pasts in one sentence), 15 (the
modal grid of obligation, permission and offer), 19 (past perfect and the third conditional), 20 (present perfect
simple and continuous, already/yet), 25 (the four conditionals), 26 (modals of deduction and the modal perfects),
36 (the reach-back as a narrative move — now under a clock).
`must not re-teach:` any tense or modal as new grammar. **Consolidation only.** The page may *name* a form and show
its one-line job, but every form is met here as something the student already owns and has to reach for fast.

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Make three decisions instead of choosing among twelve tenses**: *when* (past / now / future), *aspect* (simple /
   continuous / perfect), *stance* (fact / expectation / hypothesis).
2. **Use the two-clocks sentence**: *By the time we shipped, they had been working on it for six months.*
3. **Reach for the four modal perfects** without stopping to build them: *should have, must have, would have, might
   have*.
4. **Recognise and produce the conditional with the *if* removed** — *Had I known, I would have called* — and know
   when it belongs (written, formal, dramatic) and when it does not.
5. **Hold accuracy while the clock runs**, and know which of their own errors survive speed.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **Fluency is grammar with the attention somewhere else.** That is the thesis of the page and it has to be
  *demonstrated in the first thirty seconds*, not asserted: the student is asked a factual question while a fast
  update plays, and answers it wrongly if they were listening to the grammar.
- **Three decisions, not twelve tenses.** *When* is chosen by the time word; *aspect* is chosen by whether the
  listener needs the shape of the action (in progress / complete / repeated / relevant now); *stance* is chosen by
  whether the sentence is a fact, an expectation or a hypothesis. The decision tree is the whole consolidation, and
  it is drawn once.
- **The two clocks.** The past perfect continuous exists because a duration was already running when something else
  happened. One sentence holds both clocks: *by the time* + past simple, *had been* + -ing + *for*.
- **The modal perfects are stance, not tense**: *should have* = the expectation that failed; *must have* = the
  deduction I am sure of; *would have* = the branch not taken; *might have* = the one I cannot rule out.
- **Speed changes the error, not the knowledge.** At speed the failures are retrieval failures: the form exists, it
  arrives late or half-built. So the training is timed *retrieval*, not more explanation — and the page says plainly
  that a correct answer given in eight seconds is a wrong answer in a meeting.
- **The inverted conditional is one swap, not a new system**: *if I had known* → *had I known*. Formal, written,
  dramatic; using it in speech sounds like a speech.
- **Your surviving errors are the syllabus now.** The student leaves with three named errors and a retest, because
  at level 41 the remaining work is personal, not general.
- **Dependencies**: everything from 4-7, 11, 15, 19, 20, 25, 26 — nothing new is introduced.

## Situation
A project that is late: a stand-up that runs on a clock, a risk review with three unknowns, and a written summary
that has to be right the first time because it is going to a director. Fast speech, fixed facts, no time to think
about grammar — which is the point.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Heard — the 20-second update under a factual question.** A fast project update plays; the student is asked only
  *what is the new date?* and fails it if they were tracking the grammar. Then a second pass with the forms in view.
- **Done — the timed grid.** Twelve sentences, four seconds each, no consulting: the clock is the teaching device,
  and the score tells the student where retrieval is slow rather than where knowledge is missing.
- **Said — the risk assessment.** Six statements about an uncertain project, each demanding a stance (fact, deduction,
  expectation, hypothesis); the student says them aloud against a five-second clock.

## Chosen
**Heard → Done → Said.** Heard first because the lesson's thesis is that attention is the scarce resource, and the
student should feel that before being told. Then Done, timed, because retrieval speed is the actual gap at this
level. Then Said, where stance has to be chosen and spoken, which is the one thing a grid cannot train.

**First 30 seconds:** the update plays at natural speed with a countdown in the corner. It stops. One question:
*"So — what's the new launch date?"* If the student cannot answer, that is the lesson: they were listening to the
grammar. Nothing has been defined yet.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Three factual-recall questions from the fast update, six form identifications on the second pass, one decision tree
completed with twelve time-word triggers, four two-clocks sentences (*by the time … had been … for …*), six stance
choices for the four modal perfects, four inverted conditionals converted from their *if* forms, a twelve-item timed
grid, six spoken risk statements at five seconds each, three named personal errors plus a three-item retest, then a
no-consultation quiz that mixes tenses and modals and reuses no training item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The decision tree is the page's one full-width chart (decision / the trigger that decides it / the form it produces /
what it sounds like when it is wrong) — twelve rows, so any split would break the reading order. The two-clocks and
inverted-conditional drills sit as halves in one row: four items each, and splitting them further would leave a full
slot more than half empty. The timed grid is a full-width `.game` block (the countdown has to be the widest thing on
the page), and the personal-error log is a half with the retest beside it.
