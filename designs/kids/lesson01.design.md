# Lesson 1 (kids) — design (Pass A)

## Fundamental idea taken from `designs/lesson01.design.md`
The skeleton: **the subject is always spoken**, `be` is the whole first verb, `a/an` is decided by *sound*, and `it` is the word that carries time, weather and day. Plus the two sound habits that decide whether you are understood: the final consonant stops clean, and `p/t/k` come out with a puff (/ɪ/ vs /æ/ kept as the pair to start the ear).

Everything else — the job, the badge, the office door, the welcome note — is business furniture and is thrown away here.

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. Say who they are and where they are from with the subject said out loud: *I'm Gui. I'm from Fortaleza. I'm 14.* (never "Am from…", never "Is my name…").
2. Choose `a` / `an` by the **sound** of the next word, on the things in a school bag and a game library: *a backpack, an ID card, a friend, an hour, a uniform, an Xbox*.
3. Say the time, the day and the weather with `it`: *It's 8:15. It's Monday. It's raining.* — and stop saying "Are eight hours".
4. Pass the two first tests of being understood: the last consonant of a word lands (*friend*, not "fren-jee"), and *p/t/k* leave with air (*pizza*, *token*, *Kai*) — with /ɪ/ vs /æ/ drilled as the pair the ear needs first (*big/bag*, *sit/sat*).

## Teaching beats (per core item) and dependencies
- **The subject is never hidden.** Portuguese hides it (*Sou do Brasil*); English pays for it every single time. One sentence to the student: *in English the "eu/você/ele" is never optional — no subject, no sentence.* **Dependency:** the pronouns land here for the first time (*I, you, he, she, it, we, they*) — only as the thing that fills the slot, glossed *eu, você, ele…*, never as a table to memorise.
- **`be` = *ser* and *estar* at once.** *I'm Gui* (sou), *I'm tired* (estou). One English verb where Portuguese has two — said once, plainly, because the student will otherwise hunt for a second verb that does not exist. **Dependency:** no other verb on the page; `be` is the only verb here, on purpose.
- **`a/an` is an ear decision, not a spelling decision.** *an* before a vowel **sound** — *an ID card, an hour, an Xbox, an 8* — and `a` before everything else, including vowels written as consonants: *a uniform, a university, a user*. Mental model: *the letter does not decide; the first sound does.* **Dependency:** `hour` and `university` are glossed inline (*hora*, *universidade*) because they are the two words that break the pattern the student just built.
- **`it` is the empty subject.** Time, day, weather, temperature: English puts an `it` there because the sentence will not stand without one. *It's nine. It's Friday. It's cold.* Portuguese says *São nove / É sexta / Está frio*; the English `it` is not *isso* — it means nothing, it just holds the slot. **Dependency:** numbers 1-12 and the days are a short reference marked *você já sabe* — nothing is spent teaching them.
- **The two sound laws.** (i) **The final consonant lands.** *friend · card · game · school · name* — Brazilian speakers add a vowel to the end ("fren-djee") and the word stops being recognised. (ii) **`p/t/k` at the start of a beat get a puff of air** — *pizza, token, Kai, controller*; say the pairs with a hand in front of the mouth: no air, no /p/. **Dependency:** /ɪ/ vs /æ/ is drilled as *big/bag, sit/sat, pin/pan, hit/hat, fit/fat, did/dad, list/last, pitch/patch* — eight items, one listen-and-choose. The /ɪ/ vs /iː/ pair (*ship/sheep*) is **lesson 10's** and is deliberately absent.
- **Silly stakes, real use.** The profile card is what other people read before they decide whether to talk to you — on a school forum, in a game lobby, in the class chat. `a engineer` or "Am from Brazil" on that card is the joke the lesson exists to prevent.

## Situation
The teacher and strangers, the class group chat — first day at a new school, the class group chat, a gaming profile, the school ID card.
Day one at a new school, and the class group chat is already open. Everyone's profile shows a name, a city, an age and one line. The student has four people to meet before the bell (the chat's four pinned voice notes), a bag on the desk to label, and one message to post about themselves.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Heard — four voices in the chat.** Four voice notes from classmates, six seconds each (name, city, one thing about them). The student ticks who is from where, then copies the one they liked best.
- **Done — the profile card.** Build the card: name / city / age / one `it` line, then label six things on the desk and in the game library with `a/an`.
- **Read — the pinned rules.** The group chat's pinned message: five short rules written with `be` and `it`, half of them wrong on purpose; the student fixes the ones that break the skeleton.

## Chosen
**Done (the profile card), entered through Heard.** A 14-year-old fills in a profile card without being asked twice, and the card makes every target sentence *true of them* in under a minute — the visible progress this persona needs. The four voice notes are the entrance because the first thing that happens in a new group chat is that you hear people before you read them. The pinned rules are the weakest of the three at this level: decoding text is exactly the skill a first-lesson student does not have yet.

**First 30 seconds:** a voice note plays — *"Hey, I'm Kai. I'm from Salvador."* — and the only thing on the page is one box for the student's own name. They type it. The subject law arrives **after** they have already used it correctly.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Typed profile lines (name/city/age), eight `a/an` choices on real objects, four listen-and-tick items on the voice notes, four `it` lines (time/day/weather), one recorded sentence of introduction (*"Hey, I'm ___. I'm from ___."*), then a quiz that re-tests all four behaviours on material the training never used.

## Conflicts, and how I resolved them
- **The roadmap entry asks for the consonant set, the final consonant and /ɪ/ vs /æ/ in one lesson.** That is three sound topics in one sitting for a student with a short attention span. Resolution: the stops are *presented* inside words the student is already saying (profile, pizza, token, controller) with no separate consonant section; the final consonant gets one four-word "say it and stop" beat; **/ɪ/ vs /æ/ gets one eight-item listen-and-choose.** Flagged: coverage traded for retention.
- **Recording at 14 can be cringe.** Resolution: one sentence, two options to pick from, model played twice first, and typing is always an equal route — the recording is never the only way to finish.
- **`be` = *ser* + *estar* is a big claim for a kid.** Resolution: not taught as grammar but as one line beside two sentences from their own life (*I'm Gui / I'm tired*), trained only through `I'm`; the other persons are recognised, not produced.
- **The day/time reference could swallow the lesson.** Resolution: numbers and days are a four-line reference marked *você já sabe*; `it` is the teaching, the calendar is not.

## Shape of the session (what is met, in what order)
1. **Quatro vozes** — the Heard entrance: four voice notes, tick who is from where, copy one.
2. **O seu card** — the profile card (name / city / age / one `it` line): the spine of the page.
3. **O sujeito nunca se esconde** — the skeleton with `be` (I'm / you're / he's…), *ser* + *estar* in one line, four gap items.
4. **a ou an?** — the ear rule, the two traps (`an hour`, `a university`), the bag and the game library to label.
5. **O `it` que não é "isso"** — time, day, weather: one chart, four gaps.
6. **Lab do som** — final consonant, the puff of `p/t/k`, /ɪ/ vs /æ/ (eight pairs).
7. **Treino** — mixed, plus one recording.
8. **Quiz — sem consulta.**

Sections 3-5 are short and physical (a card, a bag, a clock) rather than one long grammar table: this persona reads one idea per block and moves on.
