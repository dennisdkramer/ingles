# Lesson 4 (kids) — design (Pass A)

`band: kids · L04 · C04 · registers: 2b (friends and classmates in the school break) · 2a (the person behind the snack-bar counter) · contexts: the school snack bar at break, the class group chat, Saturday football practice, the phone at home`

## Fundamental idea taken from `designs/kids/lesson03.design.md` and its parent `designs/lesson04.design.md`
The parent design's idea: **the simple present is not "the present"** — it is what is *always* true (habit, routine, fact), and "right now" needs a **second machine** (`am/is/are + -ing`); the **-s** on the verb belongs to the *owner* (he/she/it), not to the tense; and `-ing` has **no hard g**. Kids keep the idea and drop the office, the coffee shop, the colleague and the onboarding page; they keep the pair (*I play …* / *I'm playing …*) and the "who owns the -s" rule, and move the field to the school snack bar at break.

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. Say what is **always** true and what is **now** true, and hear the difference in their own sentence: *I play football on Saturdays* (rotina) vs *I'm playing now* (agora).
2. Put the **-s** on the verb when the owner is *he / she / it*: *she studies, he watches, she goes, it has* — and spell it by sound (*+s / +es / -ies / has*).
3. Order at the snack bar and understand the answer: *a cheese bread and a juice, please · for here or to go? · Here, please.*
4. Say `-ing` without a hard *g* — the nasal that gives away the accent in *playing, watching, going*.

## Teaching beats (per core item) and dependencies
- **The simple present is habit, fact and routine — it does not mean "right now".** *I play football* is a fact about the student's week, not about this minute. The student already says sentences about themselves (lesson 1's skeleton); the only new decision is **which column the sentence belongs to**.
- **"Now" needs a different machine: am/is/are + -ing.** Both machines arrive in the same lesson because the *contrast* is the lesson. *I play* and *I'm playing* are not two ways of saying one thing; they answer two different questions (*what do you do?* / *what are you doing?*). **Dependency:** `am/is/are` is lesson 1's `be`; only the `-ing` is new.
- **The -s belongs to the owner, not to the tense.** One person or one thing (he/she/it) takes it; I/you/we/they do not. The subject rule of lesson 1 is the platform; *have → has* is the one irregular and is taught as an exception, not as a rule.
- **Spelling follows sound.** *+s* after most verbs; *+es* after the sounds that cannot take a bare *s* (*watch, finish, go, do*); *consonant + y → -ies* (*study → studies*). The three sounds (-s / -z / -ɪz) come back in the sound lab, because the `-es` spelling exists *because* of the third sound — teaching the spelling without the sound would leave the rule unexplained.
- **The snack bar is the carrier, not the topic.** *snack bar, menu, juice, water, cheese bread, change, for here, to go* live inside a real order; the grammar rides on it, and the counter's question *for here or to go?* is the one line the student will hear for the rest of their life.
- **-ing has no g.** The back of the tongue touches, the air goes out through the nose, and the *g* never releases: *playing* is not "playin-gui". **Dependency:** the nasal ending is new; nothing else in the word changes.
- **Silly stakes.** The break is twelve minutes long and the queue is the whole school. The student who orders in one sentence eats; the student who starts "I want… a… how do you say…" hears the bell.

## Situation
Break at school. The student is at the snack bar with the class, phone in one hand, and has to (a) order without holding up the queue, (b) understand *for here or to go?*, and (c) answer two questions from a classmate in the group chat: *what do you usually do on Saturdays?* and *what are you doing right now?* — the two machines in one conversation.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Heard — the counter.** Three lines are heard before anything is read (the order, the counter's question, the price), and the student picks what was said. Good, but alone it does not install the *always / now* contrast.
- **Done — two columns of truth.** The student sorts **their own** Saturday and **their own** right-now into two columns, then converts a classmate's routine into the third person. The contrast only exists if the student's own week is on one side and their own minute on the other.
- **Read — the class group chat.** A thread where two people describe a normal week and one describes what is happening at that moment. The best reading material of the three, but at C04 it turns into translation instead of speech.

## Chosen
**Done (two columns of truth), entered through Heard.** Sorting their own sentences is the shortest path to *hearing* the pair, and the break-at-the-snack-bar audio gives the entrance a place. The chat thread is used later, only as the situation the recordings are addressed to.

**First 30 seconds:** two voice notes play — *"I play football on Saturdays."* / *"I'm playing right now, call you later."* — and the student picks **which one is "agora"**. No rule is named until both columns exist.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Six verbs written for he/she/it, six sentences sorted or converted (always vs now), a written order at the counter with the field words gap-filled, a listen-and-tick on `-ing` and on the three `-s` sounds, three recorded sentences (one habit, one now, one about a friend), then a quiz on all four behaviours with none of the training items.


## Conflicts, and how I resolved them
- **The roadmap gives the frequency adverbs (always / usually / often / sometimes / never) to lesson 8.** Resolution: they appear here **only as the time cue in brackets** (`todos os dias`, `neste momento`) because they are the student's decision-maker for the column; they are *used*, never named as a system, and the adverb's position is lesson 8's.
- **Ordering is arguably lesson 3's (polite requests).** Resolution: the counter lines here are **statements and fixed phrases** (*a cheese bread and a juice, please · for here, please*), not questions; *Can I have…?* is glossed as a fixed phrase and never analysed — lesson 3 already owns the polite question and this page does not re-open it.
- **The -s sound set could belong to the sound lessons (10).** Resolution: it is taught here because it is the sound *of this lesson's own ending*; the consonant map stays in lesson 10 and is not anticipated.
- **The -s of the verb vs the -s of the plural.** Resolution: named once, as cousins, in a note row — the student is not asked to produce the plural here, only told why the shape is familiar.
- **`-ing` after `be` vs `-ing` as a noun** (*swimming is fun*). Resolution: the noun use is not opened at all; only the two machine slots (*is + -ing*) appear.

## Shape of the session (what is met, in what order)
1. **Duas colunas** — the Heard entrance (two voice notes, one question: which is "agora"?), then the pair laid out side by side and the question that decides (*todos os dias? / neste momento?*).
2. **O -s** — who takes it (the owner), the he/she/it row, and the spelling (+s / +es / -ies / has).
3. **O agora** — am/is/are + -ing as the second machine, born next to the first, with the pair table.
4. **O lanche** — the snack-bar field: menu words, the counter dialogue, *for here or to go?*, order gaps.
5. **Lab do som** — `-ing` with no *g*; the three sounds of `-s`.
6. **Treino** — he/she/it conjugation, always vs now, the order, listening, two recordings.
7. **Quiz — sem consulta.**

The two columns come first and the rule second: this persona needs a win in the first minute, and "you already know both sentences — which one is now?" is that win. The -s arrives only after the two machines have been *heard*, so the student never meets three new things at once.
