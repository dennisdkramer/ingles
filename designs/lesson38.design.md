*if we're talking about cost…*), lesson 33 (the meeting and its decisions), lesson 34 (tone and the reader),

`must not re-teach:` the deduction system and its scale of certainty (26); how conditionals are formed (19/25); the
meeting roles and procedure (33); the e-mail ladder (34).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Place the disagreement on the ladder** — six rungs from *I completely agree* to *I don't think that holds* — and
   pick the rung the stakes deserve.
2. **Concede first, then counter**, without the word *but* erasing the concession.
3. **Hedge the certainty, never the facts**: strong about the idea, soft about the person.
4. **Ask for the evidence** as a move ("What's that based on?") and offer your own the same way.
5. **Attack the idea and protect the person**, checked by the third-person test.
6. **Say what would change your mind**, which is what makes a disagreement credible instead of stubborn.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **Disagreement is a dial, not a switch.** Most learners own two settings — full agreement and open war — so the
  lesson is the *scale*: *I completely agree · I agree with most of that · I see your point, but · I'm not sure
  that's the whole picture · I'm not convinced · I don't think that holds.* Each rung states how much of the other
  person's claim survives, and choosing the rung is the whole professional skill.
- **The rung is chosen by the stakes, not by your mood.** A rung too high loses the room; too low loses the point
  and leaves the meeting with a decision you disagree with. The page says plainly: you cannot fight every rung, and
  the skill includes deciding which one is worth it.
- **The word *but* erases the concession.** *I take your point about cost, but the timeline is impossible* — the
  listener hears only the second half. Concession then counter, no *but*: two sentences, and the concession is real
  because it is not being cancelled in the same breath.
- **Hedge the certainty, not the substance.** *I'm not sure that's the whole picture* is soft on the person and hard
  on the claim. *It's maybe possibly a bit of an issue* is soft on the claim too — which reads as unprepared, and
  gets you ignored. The hedge lives on the verb of belief, not on the facts.
- **Evidence is the neutral ground.** Asking *what's that based on?* moves the fight from people to data; offering
  *if we had the churn number I'd know how worried to be* does the same and looks generous. Both are taught as
  phrases the student can say at speed.
- **The third-person test decides person vs idea.** Would I say this if their boss were in the room? Would I say it
  to their face exactly as I say it behind their back? Two yeses, say it; anything else, rewrite it.
- **Falsifiability is the credibility move**: *what would change your mind?* asked of yourself first. A position with
  no possible counter-evidence is not a position, it is a mood.
- **Dependencies**: certainty grading from 26 (used, widened), conditionals from 19/25, meeting context from 33.

## Situation
A design review that goes wrong in a recognisable way: four colleagues, one proposal, one person who says the same
true thing twice — once as an attack and once as a contribution — and the room reacts to the person, not the
argument. The student sits in it, then runs it.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Read — the same line twice.** Two versions of one disagreement, printed side by side with the room's reaction
  after each. The student finds the four words that changed the outcome. Fast, and it teaches the ladder's stakes
  before the ladder exists.
- **Said — the rung drill.** Six opinions on screen, each with a target rung in brackets (*disagree strongly, keep
  the person intact*); the student produces the line aloud. The core motor skill.
- **Done — the transcript repair.** A twelve-turn review with four turns attacking people and two turns conceding
  with *but*; the student rewrites the six turns and re-scores the room's temperature.

## Chosen
**Read → Said → Done.** Read first because the failure is social before it is linguistic and the student can see the
damage without knowing any vocabulary. Then Said, because the rung has to come out of the mouth at speed. Then Done,
where everything is applied to material the student did not write, which is what a real review feels like.

**First 30 seconds:** two lines on screen. A: *"That's never going to work — people don't read emails."* B: *"I see
your point about reach. The thing I'd worry about is whether people read it."* The panel's reaction: A gets a sigh,
B gets an answer. The student clicks the four words that made the difference. Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
One comparison diagnosis, six rung placements on the ladder chart, six spoken disagreements at a given rung, four
*but*-removals rewritten, four hedge placements (soften the claim, keep the fact sharp), four evidence questions and
two evidence offers, four person-to-idea rewrites checked by the third-person test, two "what would change your
mind" lines of their own, a twelve-turn review transcript with six repairs, a spoken three-turn disagreement, then a
no-consultation quiz that reuses no training item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The agreement ladder is the one full-width chart of the page (rung / the phrase / what it commits you to / when the
stakes earn it) — six rows, four fields, and it is the reference the rest of the lesson points back to. The
*but*-removal drill and the hedge drill sit as halves in one row: two four-item drills that would each leave a full
slot more than half empty. The person-to-idea rewrites are full width — the before/after needs the length. The
transcript block is full width with the room's reaction in a note row beneath each repaired turn.
