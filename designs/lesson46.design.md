# Lesson 46 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 34 (the reader — now the *foreign* reader), lesson 37 (topics that travel, boundaries, small
talk), lesson 38 (disagreeing without attacking the person — now across cultures), lesson 42 (which phrasal verbs and
jargon travel badly), lesson 44 (checking understanding — the tool that makes accommodation work), lesson 12 (how
numbers are said, which varies more than learners expect).
`must not re-teach:` the tone ladder (34); the agreement ladder (38); the repair system (44); the phrasal-verb sets
(42).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Speak lingua-franca English**: clarity first, idiom second — and know that idiom is the first thing to drop, not
   the last.
2. **Set the directness dial deliberately** for a low-context or a high-context reader.
3. **Read what "yes" can mean**, and test it by asking for the next step rather than repeating the question.
4. **Save face with wording**: give the other person a route out and a way to be wrong in private.
5. **Treat silence, pace and interruption as information** rather than as rudeness.
6. **Accommodate in three moves** — slower, shorter, fewer idioms — plus write it down, and check.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **Most English in the world is not spoken to native speakers**, so the target is not the native idiom: it is
  being understood by someone whose English is also a second language. This single fact reorders everything the
  learner has been taught about sounding natural — and it is demonstrated in the first thirty seconds, not asserted.
- **Idiom is the tax on the listener.** *Ballpark*, *touch base*, *off the top of my head*, a joke, a football
  reference — the cost of each is paid by the person decoding it. The rule is not "never use idiom"; it is "spend
  the listener's attention where the meaning is".
- **Directness is a dial, and culture sets the default** — not politeness, not honesty. Low-context readers (much of
  northern Europe, the US) want the conclusion early and the reason after; high-context readers want the relationship
  and the frame first, and read a bald *no* as a rupture. Plainly: *I'm just being direct* is not a virtue, it is one
  culture's default; and indirectness is not dishonesty.
- **"Yes" is at least four things**: I agree · I hear you · I will try · I will not say no to your face. The test is
  never to repeat the question louder; it is to ask for the next step: *so — who does what by when?*
- **Face is the currency.** The wording that saves it: *perhaps I've misunderstood* (takes the blame first),
  *if that's not possible, tell me and we'll find another way* (offers the exit), criticism in the third person
  (*some teams have found…*), and never correcting someone in front of their boss.
- **Silence is a turn.** In some rooms a pause after a proposal is thought; in others it is rejection; in others it is
  the interpreter working. The lesson refuses to give a lookup table and gives the honest instruction instead: watch
  two meetings before you decide, and put the decision in writing afterwards.
- **Accommodation is not accent-mimicry.** Three moves: slower (not louder), shorter clauses, fewer idioms — plus
  writing the key line down and checking it back. This is also the inclusive-language habit the advanced element
  names: it makes the room bigger rather than making you smaller.
- **Dependencies**: 34's reader, 38's person/idea split, 42's travel-badly audit, 44's checking.

## Situation
A four-country project call: the student, a Dutch engineer (blunt, fast), a Japanese supplier (polite, silent, never
says no), a Brazilian marketing lead (warm, indirect about deadlines) and a British chair who uses three idioms a
minute. One decision has to be made and written down. Nobody's English is the problem; everybody's assumptions are.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Heard — the word that stopped the call.** Ninety seconds of the four-country call at natural speed, one idiom
  and one indirect refusal inside it. Two questions: what did the chair mean, and did the supplier agree? Both are
  answerable, and both are traps.
- **Read — four replies, one request.** The same request answered by a German, a Japanese, a Brazilian and a British
  colleague; the student decides what each reply actually commits to, then finds the single follow-up that would
  settle all four.
- **Done — the directness dial.** Eight statements rewritten for a low-context reader, a high-context reader and a
  room where the student outranks everyone.

## Chosen
**Heard → Read → Done.** Heard first: the student has just enough English to follow the words and still gets the
meaning wrong, which is the whole lesson in ninety seconds and cannot be argued with. Then Read, where the four
replies force the "what did they actually commit to" question. Then Done, the dial, because producing the same
message at three settings is the skill that survives the flight home.

**First 30 seconds:** the call plays. The chair says *"let's park the timeline and pick it up offline — the numbers
are in the ballpark anyway."* One question: *did anyone agree a date?* The student almost certainly says yes. The
clip replays with the two decisive words highlighted and nothing else changes. Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Two comprehension traps from the call, one idiom audit (which of six lines costs the listener most), four replies
decoded into commitments, one follow-up question written to settle all four, a low/high-context chart completed with
three markers each, eight statements produced at three directness settings, six "yes" readings, four face-saving
rewrites, three room-reading judgements with a stated caution, four spoken accommodated versions of one sentence, one
written summary of the decision with owners and dates, two reflection items on the student's own default, then a
no-consultation quiz that reuses no training item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The low/high-context chart is full width (style / how the message is carried / what a direct *no* does / the wording
that works), because the four fields *are* the comparison. The same-message-three-ways block is full width in a
three-column `.itemrow[data-cols="3"]`: three versions of one sentence, each of which needs a full clause of room.
The idiom audit and the "yes" readings sit as halves — six items each, both short, and each would leave a full slot
more than half empty. The four replies are four `.card`s stacked full width, one per culture, so they can be compared
by scrolling rather than by squinting.
