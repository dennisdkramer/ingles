# Lesson 37 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 3 (wh- questions and the embedded/polite question — the follow-up is built out of them),
lesson 36 (the 30-second story is the disclosure tool; its "ending that invites a question" is used here, not
taught again), lesson 26 (hedges as softeners), lesson 35 (empathy as a sentence — now in a friendly key instead of
a service one), lesson 34 (reading the reader).
`must not re-teach:` question formation or embedded questions (3); the five-beat story shape (36); the repair
phrases as a system (3 introduces them, 44 owns them); de-escalation (35).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Open without an interview**: three openers that invite a person rather than a fact.
2. **Ask the second question** — the one built on the other person's own words — from three recipes.
3. **Signal that you are following** with back-channels, an echo, and the follow-up, so the other person keeps going.
4. **Disclose a little to get a little**: one specific, low-stakes detail about yourself, at the right moment.
5. **Leave cleanly**: the exit line that names the value, names the reason and leaves the door open.
6. **Read the room**: which topics travel, which do not, and where the boundary is cultural rather than personal.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **Small talk is a test, not an exchange.** Nobody is after the weather. They are finding out, cheaply, whether you
  are easy to talk to and safe to talk to. That purpose is why it is not a waste of time, and why it is being graded
  by the other person the whole time.
- **The first question is the door; the second question is the conversation.** The skill is not a better opener — it
  is what you do with the answer. Three recipes cover most of it: *echo a word back* ("Busy how?"), *ask the how or
  the why* ("How did that land on you?"), *ask for the small story* ("What happened?"). A change of subject instead
  of a follow-up is the single most common failure.
- **Signals keep the floor open.** *Right. / Yeah, no way. / That makes sense. / Mm.* plus an echo. Silence after an
  answer reads as a verdict; the back-channel is what tells the other person to continue.
- **Disclosure is a trade, and it is asymmetric on purpose**: you offer first, small, and specific. Specificity is
  what authenticity sounds like — *I'm learning to sail, badly* beats *I like sports*.
- **The exit needs three parts**: the value ("this was useful"), the reason ("I should let you get back to it"), the
  door ("let's pick this up at the next one"). Two out of three sounds like an escape.
- **Boundaries are not coldness.** Salary, someone's health, age or weight, politics and religion on a first meeting,
  and complaints about the host are the reliable no-go list — and the list is cultural: family and personal
  questions that read as friendly in Brazil read as forward in Germany or the UK. The page says plainly that there
  is no universal list, and gives the test: *would I be happy to answer this in front of their boss?*
- **Depth is not a topic, it is a follow-up.** You can go deep on the weather (a flood, a parent's farm) and stay
  shallow on work for an hour.
- **Dependencies**: question forms from 3, the short story from 36, hedging from 26.

## Situation
A two-day industry conference. The student arrives knowing nobody, works a coffee break, a lunch table and a
standing reception, meets a peer, a senior person they would like to know, and a talker they need to leave. Each
encounter is the same four moves at a different depth.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Heard — six conversations that die.** Short exchanges where the second turn is a one-word answer or a subject
  change; the student ticks the exact line where it died and says what the move should have been.
- **Said — the follow-up ladder.** An answer on screen, three candidate follow-ups offered; the student picks the
  one that goes deeper, then writes the fourth one themselves. Pure production of the one skill that matters.
- **Read — the transcript with the follow-ups removed.** A twelve-turn reception conversation where every third line
  is a blank; the student restores the follow-up that keeps the thread.

## Chosen
**Heard → Said → Read.** Heard first because a dying conversation is funny and instantly diagnosable — the student
laughs at the failure before hearing a rule. Then Said, because the follow-up is motor skill and has to be produced,
not recognised. Then Read, where the whole eleven-line arc (open → follow → follow → disclose → exit) can be seen at
once and the student supplies the missing moves.

**First 30 seconds:** *"How's the week going?" — "Busy." — (nothing).* Two seconds of silence. The student clicks
what killed it: the answer, the missing follow-up, or the topic. Then two more exchanges do the same thing with a
different cause. Nothing has been named yet.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Three death-point diagnoses, six follow-ups written from answers, four back-channel items (does this line keep the
floor open or close it?), three disclosures rewritten from general to specific, three exit lines matched to three
situations, a topic sort (travels / does not / depends on where you are), one cultural-boundary decision item, a
spoken four-move encounter of six turns with a partner or aloud, then a no-consultation quiz that reuses no
training item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The opening-moves chart (opener / what it invites / what it risks) and the four-move sequence chart are full width.
The follow-up drills are a single column of answer-plus-three-options: the option set is the teaching, so it cannot
be split across halves. The topic sort is a two-bucket block, full width, because the *does not* bucket needs room
for the reasons. The exit lines sit as halves with the disclosure lines — two four-item groups, each of which would
leave a full slot more than half empty.
