# Lesson 19 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 15 (will / going to — the 1st conditional's future half), lesson 11 (past simple — the 2nd
conditional's "past"), lesson 14 (woulda / coulda / shoulda as sound), lesson 18 (sort out / get through — the
problem-solving verbs), lesson 12 (prices and figures inside the conditions).
`must not re-teach:` mixed and alternative conditionals — *unless, provided, as long as, in case, Had I known,
Were I* (lesson 25 owns them); *should have* for the expectation that failed (lesson 26); the past perfect as a
narrative tense (lesson 36) — it appears here only as the form the 3rd conditional needs; reporting (lesson 11).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Choose by distance, not by time**: 0 = always true; 1st = real and future; 2nd = unreal now; 3rd = unreal
   then. The if-clause never takes *will*.
2. **Build all four**: if + present/present; if + present/will; if + past/would; if + had + participle/would have +
   participle.
3. **Use *would* for the polite move**: I'd like…, Would you mind + -ing…? — the request that leaves the other
   person an exit.
4. **Say what would not happen**: wouldn't / shouldn't / didn't, one negative per clause.
5. Solve a problem out loud: state the condition, the consequence, and the regret.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **One law: the if-clause goes back one step, the result takes the operator.** Present → will; past → would;
  past perfect → would have. The student already owns *will* (15) and *would* (11, 15), so the lesson is a
  *mapping*, not a new system.
- **The past in the 2nd conditional is not past time, it is distance.** *If I had more time* is about now. This is
  the sentence that unlocks the whole lesson, and the one Portuguese speakers resist.
- **if + will is the error to kill.** After *if*, the future never appears: *if it rains*, never *if it will rain*.
- **The 3rd conditional is where the past perfect first appears** — as a fixed block (*if I had known*), not as a
  tense to conjugate. Only *had + participle* inside the frame.
- **woulda/coulda/shoulda come back as the spoken form** of *would have / could have / should have* — the sound
  lesson (14) is now grammar.
- **would for requests** is one small extra room: *I'd like…* and *Would you mind + -ing* — the same word doing
  politeness instead of hypothesis.
- **Dependencies**: the problem verbs come from 18; the figures from 12; the past simple from 11.

## Situation
A project about to miss its deadline: three people in a room deciding what happens if the client says no, what they
would do with more budget, and what they would have done differently. Every sentence in the meeting is a condition.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Read — the decision meeting.** Six turns, each one conditional; the student labels the form and hears the
  difference between a real plan and a hypothetical.
- **Done — the chain.** Given the first clause, the student writes the second; then the same chain is pushed into
  the past, so the same idea appears in the 1st, 2nd and 3rd forms.
- **Heard — the regret.** Three lines with *woulda / coulda / shoulda* at speed; the student writes the full form.

## Chosen
**Read → Done → Heard.** The meeting first, because conditionals are argument, not conjugation; then the chain,
which is the only exercise that makes the four forms one system instead of four rules; then the regret, because the
3rd conditional is mostly heard, not written.

**First 30 seconds:** *If we miss the deadline, we lose the client.* — the student clicks whether this is a real
risk or a hypothetical. Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Six form labels from the meeting, four 0/1st choices, four 2nd choices (including *if I were*), four 3rd-condition
completions, a three-step chain (1st → 2nd → 3rd), four listening items, a spoken problem-solving turn, then a
no-consultation quiz with no reused item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The four-form chart is full width (five fields per row). The 0/1st pair and the 2nd/3rd pair sit as halves in one
row: both are four-item groups that would each leave a full slot more than half empty, and the contrast *real vs
unreal* is the teaching.
