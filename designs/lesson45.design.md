# Lesson 45 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 34 (tone and the reader — the ladder is used, never retaught), lesson 29 (how the passive is
built and what agency means), lesson 33 (decisions, owners, dates), lesson 43 (the follow-up e-mail the student
wrote), lesson 38 (conceding before countering — now as paragraph order), lesson 13 (comparatives inside a
recommendation), lesson 12 (numbers in a findings section).
`must not re-teach:` the formal ↔ informal ladder, requests, apologies, bad news and subject-line tone (34); the
passive's formation and the agency lesson (29); meeting minutes (33).

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Build the e-mail that survives forwarding**: subject line, first line, last line and attachment name carry the
   whole message for a reader who reads only those four.
2. **Write one idea per paragraph**, with a topic sentence that names it.
3. **Use the report shape** — purpose, findings, recommendation — and put the recommendation where a busy reader
   looks (bottom line up front).
4. **Choose the passive on purpose**: where it protects the reader or the doer is irrelevant, and where it is only
   hiding who decided.
5. **Cut a fifth of the words** without losing a fact, and know which fifth goes (the throat-clearing).
6. **Hold one house voice** across documents while the reader changes.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **A professional document is read twice, and the first read is not a read.** Somebody skims the subject line, the
  first line, the last line and the attachment name, and decides from those four whether to open it properly. So
  those four are not decoration: they are the document. This is the architecture lesson that 34's tone lesson never
  covered, and it is the one that decides whether the tone is ever heard.
- **Format is a promise about effort.** A wall of text tells the reader the writer did not think about them; four
  short paragraphs tell them the writer did. Same content, different respect.
- **The topic sentence is a contract**: the first sentence names the paragraph, and the paragraph keeps the promise.
  The test is brutal and easy to teach: delete every sentence after the first in each paragraph — the document
  should still make sense as a list.
- **The report is an argument, not a log.** Purpose (why you are writing), findings (what is true), recommendation
  (what should happen). A log stops at findings, which is why logs get sent back. BLUF is taught as a placement
  decision: the recommendation can go first when the reader is senior and the reasoning is short.
- **The passive earns its place three ways** — the doer is unknown, irrelevant, or the reader (a *you were* sentence
  that spares a person). It **hides** when the doer is the writer or their team: *the decision was made* means *I
  decided and I would rather not say so*. The form is 29's; the ethics is this page's.
- **Length is a discipline with a target.** The one-page test, and the known saving: the first two sentences and the
  last one are usually throat-clearing, and about a fifth of the words go without a single fact leaving.
- **Salutation and closing are a machine, not a mood** (Dear Mr Smith / Kind regards; Hi Sam / Best; Hello all /
  Thanks). A mismatch — *Hey* with *Yours faithfully* — is the fastest way to look like you do not know the room.
- **House voice is repetition done on purpose**: the same notice from a bank, a startup and a consultancy differs in
  three decisions — sentence length, whether it says *we*, and how it names the reader.
- **Dependencies**: the tone ladder and the reader from 34, the passive from 29, minutes from 33.

## Situation
A supplier has changed a delivery date and the student has to (a) e-mail the client, (b) write the one-page report
that goes to the programme board, and (c) turn the same facts into a notice for a partner whose house style is
nothing like theirs. Three documents, one afternoon, one set of facts — and a director who reads only the first line.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Heard — the read-aloud test.** A director reads a colleague's e-mail aloud, stops at line four and asks *"so
  what do they want?"* The wall of text becomes audible, which no amount of formatting advice achieves.
- **Done — the rebuild.** A 190-word unstructured e-mail turned into subject + four paragraphs with topic sentences,
  then the report skeleton built from the same facts.
- **Read — three documents, one fact set.** The e-mail, the report and the partner notice side by side, with the
  three decisions that differ marked.

## Chosen
**Heard → Done → Read.** Heard first because the failure is a reader's failure, and hearing a reader fail is
unforgettable; it also quietly installs the four-line rule before it is named. Then Done, because layout is
construction and has to be performed. Then Read, at the end, when the student can see the same facts wearing three
different shapes and say why each is right.

**First 30 seconds:** a voice reads an e-mail at speaking pace. At line four it stops: *"Sorry — what do they actually
want?"* The four likely sentences are on screen. The student picks the one that should have been line one. Nothing
is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
One diagnosis of the read-aloud failure, four "carrier line" choices (subject / opener / closer / attachment), six
topic-sentence identifications and three topic sentences written, one ordered report skeleton plus one BLUF version,
six passive decisions (earn it / hides it) and two rewrites naming the doer, six salutation–closing matches and two
mismatches fixed, one 190→~150-word cut with the deleted sentences marked, one 90-word e-mail written from facts,
one notice rewritten in three house voices, then a no-consultation quiz that reuses no training item.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The four-carrier-lines chart and the report-shape chart are full width (role / what it must contain / what happens
if it is missing) — the rows are the lesson's spine. The passive decision drill is full width because each item
needs the sentence, the verdict and the reason. The salutation–closing machine is a full-width two-column chart (the
pairing has to be *seen* as a pairing). Only the topic-sentence items and the cut list sit as halves — six and eight
short items, each of which would leave a full slot more than half empty.
