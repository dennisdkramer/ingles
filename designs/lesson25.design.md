`must not re-teach:` the four forms themselves (0/1st/2nd/3rd are lesson 19's); the past perfect as a tense

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Mix the times**: *If I had taken the job, I would be in Berlin now* (past condition, present result) and
   *If I weren't so busy, I would have called* (present condition, past result).
2. **Replace *if* with the alternatives**: unless, provided / as long as, in case, otherwise, even if,
   whether…or.
3. **Invert instead of *if***: *Had I known…, Should you need anything…, Were I in your position…* — formal, and it
   removes the *if* entirely.
4. **State preference and regret**: would rather (same subject / different subject + past), rather than,
   it's time + past, wish / if only.
5. **Negotiate with a hypothetical** — the polite conditional as a tool: *If we could split the cost, would that
   work?*

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **A mixed conditional is not a fifth form: it is two forms stitched at the seam.** The two halves keep their own
  time, and that is exactly what the speaker wants: *if I had taken it* (then) *I would be there* (now).
- **Each alternative to *if* carries a different logic**: *unless* = the only exception; *provided / as long as* =
  the condition that must hold; *in case* = preparation for a possibility (not a condition!); *otherwise* = the
  result of NOT doing it; *even if* = the result does not change; *whether…or* = the two branches both lead there.
- **The inversion is register, not meaning** — it is what makes a proposal or a formal e-mail sound senior. It is
  built from the auxiliary the form already has: *Had I…* (3rd), *Should you…* (1st), *Were I…* (2nd).
- **would rather has two syntaxes**: same subject takes the bare verb (*I'd rather wait*); a different subject takes
  the past (*I'd rather you waited*). *It's time* follows the same pattern (*it's time we left*).
- **wish / if only are the regret operators**: *wish + past* for now, *wish + had* for then. They are the emotional
  counterpart of the 2nd and 3rd conditionals.
- **The negotiation move is the point of the whole lesson**: a hypothetical lowers the cost of saying no.
- **Dependencies**: the four forms from 19; the operator grid from 15; the perfect from 20.

## Situation
A contract renegotiation that has to happen without offending anyone: the supplier raised the price, the deadline
is fixed, and the only way to move either is a hypothetical — *if we could split the cost, would that work?* The
student plays the meeting and then writes the follow-up e-mail with one inverted conditional.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Read — the negotiation.** Six turns built on alternatives and inversion; the student identifies which condition
  logic each turn uses.
- **Done — the two-seam builder.** Given a past fact and a present result, the student writes the mixed conditional,
  then reverses it.
- **Heard — the regret.** Four lines with *wish / if only / should have*; the student ticks what is regretted and
  when.

## Chosen
**Read → Done → Heard.** The negotiation first because alternatives to *if* are negotiation moves, not grammar
trivia; then the mixed builder, which is the one place the student has to hold two times at once; then the regret,
because *wish* is almost only ever heard.

**First 30 seconds:** *If I had taken the job, I would be in Berlin now.* — the student clicks whether the sentence
is about the past, the present, or both. Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
Four mixed-conditional completions, six alternative choices, four inversion rewrites, four preference/regret items,
four listening items, a spoken negotiation turn, a written follow-up with one inverted conditional, then a
no-consultation quiz with no reused item.
