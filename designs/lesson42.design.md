# Lesson 42 — design (Pass A)

`recycles:` lesson 18 (the machinery: particle as meaning-carrier, separable vs inseparable, the *get* family),
lesson 22 (people and talk: bring up, go over, come up with, catch up, turn up), lesson 23 (work and problems: set
up, wrap up, put off, carry out, sort out, look into, figure out, get over, keep up with), lesson 34 (register and
the reader), lesson 29 (the passive single-word style of a report).
`must not re-teach:` the definition of a phrasal verb, the particle senses and the separable/inseparable rule (18);
any of the three sets as new vocabulary (22, 23). **Consolidation only — no new verbs.**

## Core (what the student can do afterwards)
1. **Retrieve the whole strand in a situation it was not taught in**, which is the only test that matters after
   three slices: the same verb, a new room.
2. **Read the particle** to guess an unfamiliar verb's meaning from its shape.
3. **Place the pronoun correctly at speed** — *turn it off*, never *turn off it*.
4. **Keep the collocation**: the verb travels with its noun (*carry out a review*, *wrap up a meeting*).
5. **Switch to the single-word equivalent** when the writing has to survive a client, and know that the switch is a
   register decision, not a correction — and back again when the room is spoken.
6. **Audit the jargon**: which phrasal verbs carry meaning for an international audience and which are office
   fashion with a half-life of two years.

## Teaching beats and dependencies
- **Consolidation is retrieval in a new context.** Reviewing the list is not the lesson; the lesson is that *look
  into* has to arrive when a *client* complains, not when a list of work verbs is on the page. Every block here
  changes the room the verbs were learnt in.
- **The particle is the meaning**, so an unseen verb is still half-readable: *up* completes or approaches, *out*
  exits or solves, *off* separates or postpones, *on* continues, *through* completes against resistance, *into*
  investigates. That is the generalisation that turns 40 memorised items into a system.
- **The pronoun settles separability.** If the object is a pronoun it goes inside (*sort it out*, *put it off*);
  when in doubt, inside is almost always safe — and the few verbs that refuse it (*look into it*, *get over it*) are
  the ones worth naming.
- **Collocation is the difference between translated and spoken.** *Carry out a review* — not *execute a review*; you
  *wrap up* a meeting and *sign off* a budget. Wrong nouns are what make fluent speech sound assembled.
- **The single word is not "better English" — it is a different room.** Phrasal verbs are spoken; the Latin single
  word is written and formal. A report full of *sort out* reads as a chat; a stand-up made of *investigate* and
  *postpone* reads as a memo being read aloud. Both directions are taught, because the escape hatch works both ways.
- **Jargon has a half-life.** *Reach out, touch base, circle back* are the current three: they mean little to an
  international audience and less in two years. The page names them as fashion, gives the plain replacement, and
  says plainly that this is a style judgement, not a rule.
- **Dependencies**: 18's machinery, 22's and 23's sets, 34's reader, 29's formal style.

## Situation
One day, two rooms: the 9 a.m. stand-up where everything is said the short way, and the 5 p.m. report to a client
where the same day is said the long way. In between: a supplier who has gone quiet, a deadline that has moved, and
a colleague who has solved something. Same events, two registers, one vocabulary.

## Candidates (three channels)
- **Heard — the 30-second stand-up.** Nine phrasal verbs at natural speed, one factual question underneath so the
  student cannot simply hunt the list, and a count to self-check.
- **Done — the two-way conversion.** The same eight statements turned stand-up → report and back, with the
  register consequence of each direction shown after every item.
- **Said — the pronoun test at speed.** Eight sentences with a pronoun object, said aloud against a clock, where the
  only possible error is placement.

## Chosen
**Heard → Done → Said.** Heard first because the whole strand lives in speech and the student has to meet it at
speed before meeting it on a page. Then Done, the two-way conversion, because register is the strand's untaught half
and it is mechanical enough to drill. Then Said, the pronoun, which is pure automaticity and belongs last, when
nothing else is competing for attention.

**First 30 seconds:** nine phrasal verbs in thirty seconds of stand-up, and one question: *"He says the supplier has
gone quiet — what does he do about it?"* The answer is in the four-word reply (*we'll look into it*). The student has
to catch it. Nothing is defined.

## What the student produces (evidence)
One count-and-catch task, three particle-sense guesses for unseen verbs, eight pronoun placements, six collocation
choices, eight two-way conversions with a register note each, a marked comparison of one day told two ways, a
six-item jargon audit (keep / replace, with the replacement written), eight spoken updates at speed, then a
no-consultation quiz that reuses no training item and mixes both registers.

## Width note (for Pass B)
The particle chart is full width (particle / the sense it carries / two verbs it explains / a third the student
completes) — three fields plus a blank, and the rows are the lesson's generalisation. The pronoun drill and the
collocation drill sit as halves in one row: eight and six items, each of which would leave a full slot more than

jargon audit is full width — each row needs the phrase, the plain replacement and the reason.
